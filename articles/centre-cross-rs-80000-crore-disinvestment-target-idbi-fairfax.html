Centre to Top Rs 80,000 Cr Disinvestment Target, First Since FY19

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The government is likely to exceed its budgeted Rs 80,000 crore disinvestment and asset monetisation target for FY27.
  • •- Rs 55,757 crore has already been raised through minority stake sales in nine PSUs, a strategic sale and SUUTI remittances.
  • •- The Rs 52,878 crore IDBI Bank strategic sale to Fairfax will push total proceeds beyond Rs 82,000 crore.
  • •- The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs approved Fairfax's Canadian subsidiary as the strategic buyer on Wednesday.
  • •- The last time the target was crossed was FY19, when receipts hit Rs 84,972 crore against an Rs 80,000 crore target.
  • •- Receipts have fallen short of targets in every year since, including Rs 13,534 crore against Rs 78,000 crore in FY22.

The Indian government is on course to do something it has not managed in eight years: actually exceed its disinvestment target. Budgeted to raise Rs 80,000 crore through disinvestment and asset monetisation in FY27, the Centre has already collected Rs 55,757 crore — and with Wednesday's Cabinet approval of the IDBI Bank strategic sale to Fairfax, the full-year tally is expected to cross Rs 82,000 crore.

The arithmetic is dominated by one transaction. The IDBI Bank deal values the government's 30.48 per cent stake at roughly Rs 26,620 crore and LIC's 30.24 per cent stake at around Rs 26,440 crore — a combined Rs 52,878 crore flowing to two public balance sheets once the transaction closes, expected before the end of FY27. The Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs approved Fairfax subsidiary M/s 10955230 Canada Inc as the strategic buyer on Wednesday, clearing the political hurdle for what would be the largest bank privatisation in India's history.

The rest of the year has already been busy. The Rs 55,757 crore collected so far has come through minority stake sales in nine public sector undertakings including Life Insurance Corporation of India and Coal India, the strategic sale of Indian Medicines Pharmaceuticals Corporation, and remittances from SUUTI, the successor trust holding shares of companies that were nationalised in the 1980s.

The context makes the milestone more significant. Since the government stopped setting separate disinvestment targets from FY24 and folded the goal into "miscellaneous capital receipts" alongside asset monetisation, the numbers have consistently disappointed. The budgeted path ran Rs 30,000 crore in FY24, Rs 33,000 crore in FY25, Rs 33,837 crore in FY26 and then a sudden jump to Rs 80,000 crore for FY27 — a target that most analysts assumed would be quietly revised down, as targets routinely were.

The track record justified the scepticism. In FY23 the government raised Rs 35,294 crore against a revised estimate of Rs 50,000 crore. In FY22 it raised Rs 13,534 crore against Rs 78,000 crore — one of the largest shortfalls in the history of the programme. FY21 delivered Rs 32,886 crore against Rs 32,000 crore, and FY20 Rs 50,300 crore against Rs 65,000 crore. The last genuine beat was FY19, when receipts reached Rs 84,972 crore against the Rs 80,000 crore budget, powered by the massive LIC-era slate of offers that now feels like a different fiscal era.

This year's outcome rests on a deliberate strategic shift. Rather than dribbling out minority stakes through offer-for-sale windows at whatever discount the market demands, the government concentrated on one large, competitive strategic sale with a named acquirer — and the Fairfax-IDBI structure, with its regulatory approvals and open-offer mechanics, gives the transaction a completion timeline that fits inside the fiscal year. Minority stake sales in marquee PSUs then filled in the base.

For the exchequer, the timing is fortuitous. The Centre's fiscal math for FY27 assumed substantial non-tax revenue to offset a heavy borrowing programme at precisely the moment global oil prices have spiked and subsidy pressures are rebuilding. A Rs 82,000 crore-plus disinvestment year does not close that gap by itself, but it materially de-risks the deficit arithmetic and gives the finance ministry room if fuel or fertiliser costs escalate further.

There are caveats worth noting. "Proceeds expected" is not the same as cash in hand: the IDBI transaction still requires regulatory clearances, and the money lands only on completion. And the structural critique of India's disinvestment programme — that it sells assets to fund consumption rather than reinvestment — remains untouched by one good year. The strategic-sale pipeline beyond IDBI is also thin; FY28's target will be negotiated against whatever the government can realistically bring to market.

The IDBI deal itself illustrates both the promise and the difficulty of that pipeline. Previous attempts at large strategic sales — BPCL's stalled privatisation being the most prominent — consumed years of regulatory and political effort before being quietly shelved. IDBI succeeded where those efforts failed partly because the structure was cleaner: a listed bank with a defined shareholding, a competitive process with a named acquirer, and a buyer in Fairfax with a long India track record through its Prem Watsa-led platform. Replicating that combination across other CPSEs is the task that will define whether FY27 is a turning point or an outlier.

For markets, the programme's revival carries a quieter significance than the headline number. Predictable, transparent disinvestment gives the equity market a stream of supply to absorb and gives index providers and foreign investors confidence that Indian state assets are genuinely in play — a sentiment that feeds directly into the valuation debate over PSU stocks, which have traded at a wide discount to private peers for years.

Still, after eight consecutive years of missed targets, crossing Rs 80,000 crore would be more than an accounting footnote. It would signal that the strategic-sale machinery — dormant since the Air India privatisation — can function at scale, and it would hand the government a rare fiscal credential in a year when almost every other macro variable, from oil to the rupee to wholesale inflation, is moving the wrong way.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
HOW MUCH HAS THE GOVERNMENT RAISED VIA DISINVESTMENT IN FY27?
Rs 55,757 crore so far from minority stake sales in nine PSUs, a strategic sale and SUUTI remittances. Including the IDBI Bank deal, total proceeds should exceed Rs 82,000 crore.
WHAT IS THE IDBI BANK DEAL WORTH?
The strategic sale is valued at Rs 52,878 crore — the government's 30.48% stake at about Rs 26,620 crore and LIC's 30.24% stake at around Rs 26,440 crore.
WHO IS THE BUYER?
Fairfax subsidiary M/s 10955230 Canada Inc, approved by the Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs on Wednesday.
WHEN DID INDIA LAST EXCEED ITS DISINVESTMENT TARGET?
In FY19, when receipts reached Rs 84,972 crore against a target of Rs 80,000 crore. Every year since has fallen short. GLOSSARY: - Disinvestment: Sale of government equity in public sector enterprises to raise non-tax revenue. - Strategic sale: Transfer of management control to a private buyer, not just shares. - SUUTI: Specified Undertaking of the Unit Trust of India, holding residual shares of nationalised companies. - CCEA: Cabinet Committee on Economic Affairs, the Union cabinet's top economic decision-making body. - Asset monetisation: Leasing or selling rights in operational infrastructure assets to unlock capital.
