Piyush Goyal: India Will Become Global Car Hub as Europe Rules Bite

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said on Monday that India will become the global hub for car manufacturing as Europe's regulations make competitiveness impossible on the continent
  • 02/India has already offered quota-based tariff concessions to the UK and the European Union in their respective free trade agreements, building the trade architecture for deeper auto ties
  • 03/April-August exports rose 17.85% year-on-year to $215.91 billion against a $1 trillion full-year export target set by the commerce ministry

Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal said on Monday that India will become the global hub for car manufacturing. He argued that Europe's rising regulatory burden will push global manufacturers to shift production toward India over the coming years.

Goyal made the remarks at The Dilli Dialogue on October 12, 2026. "Finally India is going to become the global hub for car manufacturing. Everybody is going to come and manufacture in India and with Europe having such crazy regulations, it is going to be impossible to be competitive. All the European manufacturers will come to India," he said.

The minister did not specify which European regulations he was referring to. India and several other countries have raised objections to the European Union's carbon border adjustment mechanism, or CBAM, a known friction point in trade talks with Brussels for carbon-intensive industries.

Goyal pointed to existing trade architecture that could support such a shift. India has offered quota-based tariff concessions to both the United Kingdom and the European Union in their respective free trade agreements, creating channels for deeper automobile trade.

The comments come as India chases $1 trillion worth of exports this fiscal year. The commerce ministry has set an ambitious trajectory for outbound shipments despite global headwinds, uneven demand in key markets, and rising protectionism in parts of the developed world.

Early numbers support the momentum story. India's exports rose 17.85% year-on-year to $215.91 billion in April-August of the current fiscal year. Imports climbed 18.21% to $363 billion over the same five-month period.

Goyal also cited the Tata Motors acquisition of Jaguar as a case study in automotive turnaround. "I do not know if you are aware. Tatas had so much trouble after they bought Jaguar. Tata Motors itself was going to collapse and then they put in a lot of re-engineering, and shift some models to India...Finally they were able to make money after years," he said.

The anecdote works as both aspiration and warning. The Tata-Jaguar turnaround took years of painful restructuring before it generated returns, a reminder that building a global car hub is a marathon for any economy, not a sprint. Landing European factories would also demand competitive logistics, skilled labor, and stable policy.

The policy backdrop to Goyal's claim is real, even if the specifics were thin on Monday. India has concluded or is negotiating free trade agreements with nearly every major auto-producing region, including the recently concluded pacts with the United Kingdom and the European Union.

In those negotiations, India offered quota-based tariff concessions on automobiles — a limited number of imported cars can enter at reduced duties once the agreements take effect. The idea is to make India a credible export base: European, Japanese and Korean manufacturers get preferential access to partner markets if they build in India, and domestic supply chains deepen in turn.

The Jaguar Land Rover story Goyal cited is the government's favourite cautionary tale turned success story. Tata Motors' acquisition of the British marques in 2008 from Ford nearly broke the company — a combination of debt, the global financial crisis and collapsing demand left investors pricing in distress. Re-engineering the product line and shifting models to Indian manufacturing eventually turned the business profitable, a turnaround Goyal explicitly credited from the stage on Monday.

The trade numbers Goyal cited are consistent with the ambition. Merchandise and services exports for April to August of fiscal 2026-27 rose 17.85 per cent year on year to $215.91 billion, while imports climbed 18.21 per cent to $363 billion over the same period. The commerce ministry's target for the full year is $1 trillion worth of exports.

There are headwinds the minister did not dwell on. India's car exports are dominated by a handful of models and marques, and the country still imports most of its high-end automotive components and semiconductors. Electric vehicle makers face stiff Chinese competition in both technology and cost, and the EU's carbon border adjustment mechanism — which taxes embedded emissions in imports — cuts both ways for Indian-made cars depending on their energy source.

Still, the direction of policy is hard to miss. A series of production-linked incentive schemes has already pulled fresh automotive investment into India, and the FTA architecture now being assembled is designed to make the country a low-tariff export platform rather than a protected domestic market. Monday's speech was less a prediction than a statement of intent.

GLOSSARY: Free trade agreement (FTA): A pact between two or more countries to reduce tariffs and trade barriers on goods and services exchanged between them. Quota-based tariff concession: A trade deal provision allowing a fixed quantity of imports at reduced duty rates, beyond which normal tariffs apply. Carbon border adjustment mechanism (CBAM): An EU levy on imports based on the carbon emissions embedded in their production, designed to match the carbon price paid by European producers. Production-linked incentive (PLI) scheme: An Indian government programme that pays manufacturers incentives based on incremental production and sales. Average revenue per user (ARPU): Monthly revenue a telecom company earns per subscriber; higher ARPU generally indicates a richer customer mix.
