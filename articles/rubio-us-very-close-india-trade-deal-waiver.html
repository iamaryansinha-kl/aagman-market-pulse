Rubio Says US 'Very Close' to India Trade Deal, Cites Waiver Link

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Marco Rubio said Monday the US is "very close" to finalising a trade deal with India.
  • •- He explicitly linked trade progress to national-security waivers on Russian energy sanctions.
  • •- Rubio acknowledged India's energy constraint: "Geography is real" for countries unable to source alternatives.
  • •- Sanctions were waived "over the past few months because of the impact on global supplies and prices."
  • •- The comments come as Rubio travels to India for trade and economic discussions.
  • •- A deal would cap months of deadlock over agriculture, energy purchases and tariff parity.

The stalled US-India trade negotiation received its most direct acknowledgment yet from Washington on Monday, when Secretary of State Marco Rubio said the United States is trying to finalise a trade deal with India and is "very close" to doing so. The statement, made while Rubio is travelling to India, converts months of diplomatic fog into a timeline — and, more significantly, it did so while explicitly tying the trade track to the sanctions track, in a single sentence that revealed how the two files have become entangled in the Trump administration's India policy.

The linkage was the headline. Explaining the administration's decision to waive sanctions and tariffs on Russian energy under the Graham Act, Rubio said the waivers were necessary "because of the impact on global supplies and prices," and then moved directly to the trade file: "We're trying to finalise a trade deal with India right now. We're very close to doing that. That's one of the things we take into consideration when the President makes a decision about national security waivers and things of that nature." In plain terms: the waiver that let India keep buying discounted Russian crude without penalty is part of the same ledger as the trade deal — each a chip in the other's negotiation.

Just as striking was Rubio's language on India's energy position, the most explicit American acknowledgment to date of the constraint that has defined the dispute. "Every country has a complex set of factors that it has to take into account. Geography is real. For some countries, it's very difficult to acquire energy from alternative sources. That's not an excuse, but all of that has to be balanced when making foreign policy," he said. Coming from the administration that spent the year demanding India sever its Russian oil purchases — and threatening secondary tariffs if it did not — the concession is substantial, effectively validating New Delhi's core argument that energy security is a geographic fact, not a diplomatic choice.

The remarks knit together three threads of recent coverage. The first is the waiver itself: Friday's executive order under the Graham Act suspended the sanctions architecture that had hung over India's refining sector, and oil markets immediately traded the news as a supply event. The second is the trade deal's tortured path: a February framework that promised to cut the headline tariff from 25 per cent to 18 per cent was devalued when US courts struck the reciprocal-tariff regime that gave it meaning, and talks had since stalled over agriculture market access, US energy purchase commitments, and demands for tariff parity with Vietnam — the "last one percent" that negotiators could not close. The third is Rubio's own itinerary: his arrival in India this week, skipping Delhi for Mumbai and Jamnagar in favour of economic deliverables, now reads as the closing scene of the negotiation rather than another round of positioning.

For India, the economics of closure are compelling. Exports to the United States rose twenty-one per cent in August to $8.3 billion even under the current tariff regime, and Indian officials have consistently conditioned operationalising any deal on preserving competitive advantage against Southeast Asian peers. A reduction toward the eighteen per cent band — or better — would remove the single largest cloud over India's export cycle, while the waiver eliminates the risk premium that crude-driven inflation had installed in the bond and currency markets. New Delhi's negotiating posture has been to refuse a bad deal rather than a fast one; Rubio's comments suggest Washington has decided the current draft is close enough to good. Indian officials have so far offered no public confirmation of a timeline, maintaining their posture that nothing is agreed until everything is — a discipline that has served the negotiation well through a year of American political turbulence, court rulings and tariff shocks.

What remains unspoken is the sequencing. Rubio framed the waiver as one input "when the President makes a decision about national security waivers" — language that keeps the leverage alive until signatures exist. A trade announcement during Rubio's India visit would vindicate that reading; a return to talking points about agriculture and parity would confirm that "very close" is diplomacy's most elastic unit of distance. Either way, Monday's statement reset the market's priors: the two tracks are one negotiation now, and both are pointed at a conclusion.

The market's reaction to the sequencing risk will show up first in the rupee and in rate expectations. The RBI's October rate decision already surprised toward tightening, and a credible trade-deal horizon would ease the imported-inflation channel that forced it — lower tariff risk on exports, a stable waiver on crude, and a reduced risk premium all feed the same direction. India's equity market has spent two years pricing the exit of foreign capital against exactly this cluster of risks; the cluster is now, on the American side at least, being described in the past tense.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID MARCO RUBIO SAY ABOUT THE INDIA TRADE DEAL?
Rubio said the US is trying to finalise a trade deal with India right now and is "very close to doing that," speaking during his visit to India.
HOW ARE SANCTIONS WAIVERS LINKED TO THE DEAL?
Rubio said trade progress is one factor the President weighs when deciding on national-security waivers for Russian energy sanctions.
WHAT DID RUBIO SAY ABOUT INDIA'S RUSSIAN OIL PURCHASES?
He acknowledged that "geography is real" and alternative energy sourcing is difficult for some countries — the clearest US recognition yet of India's constraint.
WHAT STICKING POINTS REMAIN?
Agriculture market access, US energy purchase commitments and tariff parity with competitors such as Vietnam have held up finalisation. GLOSSARY: - Graham Act: The US law authorising sanctions on countries buying Russian energy, waived by executive order. - National-security waiver: A presidential exemption from sanctions on grounds of US national interest. - Tariff parity: Equal tariff treatment across competing exporter countries. - Secondary tariffs: Penalties imposed on a third country for trading with a sanctioned nation.
