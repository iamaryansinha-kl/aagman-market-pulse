India's Greenfield FDI Doubles China's at $351 Billion Over 2021-25

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/India drew $351 billion in greenfield FDI in 2021-25, up 61% from $222 billion in 2016-20 and more than double China's $159 billion.
  • 02/China's greenfield FDI fell 54% from $350 billion in the prior five years and sits far below its $514 billion peak of 2006-10, BCG data shows.
  • 03/BCG's Nikolaus Lang says India must mobilise capital, skill workers and stimulate domestic demand to convert the inflow into a full manufacturing footprint.

India attracted $351 billion in foreign direct investment for greenfield projects during 2021-25, more than double the amount China pulled in for new ventures over the same five-year window, according to data compiled by the Boston Consulting Group. The figures, reported on October 13, mark a decisive shift in global manufacturing geography.

The number represents a 61% surge from the $222 billion in greenfield FDI that India received during 2016-20, signalling that the country's manufacturing push has moved well beyond pilot projects into large-scale, long-horizon commitments from global corporations. No other large economy came close to that pace of acceleration.

China, meanwhile, is moving in the opposite direction. Greenfield FDI into newer ventures there dropped 54% to $159 billion in 2021-25 from $350 billion in the preceding five years, and remains a fraction of the $514 billion peak recorded in 2006-10, BCG data shows.

Nikolaus Lang, managing director and senior partner at BCG, described the Indian market as the single biggest draw for investors amid what he called a sweeping global realignment — one that is redrawing where the world's factories get built in the years after the pandemic and repeated supply shocks.

Industry experts say investor concerns about China are actively steering capital toward India and other Southeast Asian markets, as companies that once concentrated production in one country now hedge against geopolitical shocks, tariffs, rising compliance costs and supply disruptions that have rattled boardrooms since 2020.

Lang said multinationals are fundamentally rethinking sourcing. The old model — buying everything from a single supplier, chosen purely on cost — is giving way to dual or triple sourcing, where critical inputs come from two or three countries at once to contain risk.

"So, from that perspective, I think India has a role to play because the industry is looking for alternative sources than the one source they used to have," Lang said in an interview with the Economic Times, framing the shift as structural rather than a passing trade diversion.

Southeast Asia's numbers show the realignment is region-wide but uneven. Malaysia surged 125% to $140 billion, Indonesia grew 34% to $133 billion, and Thailand jumped 97% to $56 billion in greenfield FDI over the five years to 2025, according to the BCG figures in the period under review.

The laggards are instructive. Vietnam slipped 12% and the Philippines fell 24% during the same period over the five years under review, a reminder that early gains under the China Plus One strategy do not guarantee continued inflows once competitors ramp up their own incentives and infrastructure.

China Plus One itself dates to the aftermath of the pandemic, when multinationals began adding production capacity outside China while keeping their Chinese operations running throughout Southeast Asia. The Philippines and Vietnam were among its earliest and most visible beneficiaries.

Holding India's gains will require sustained focus on three fronts, Lang said: infrastructure development, skilling, and cost competitiveness. Together they determine whether India merely hosts assembly operations or builds a full manufacturing footprint at global scale across sectors from autos to electronics over the next decade.

Capital mobilisation comes first. Land, power, logistics corridors and component supply chains all demand funding, and the speed at which India can marshal that capital will shape how much of the $351 billion in announced intent converts into operating plants on schedule without delays.

The second front is the workforce. "If you don't have adequate people being able to handle stamping, painting, assembly, mechatronics, and things like this, you will always struggle to localise," Lang said — a pointed warning for a country banking on factory jobs for its policymakers.

Third is demand stimulus. Lang's logic: if the local market is big enough, localisation becomes worthwhile, because factories built to serve domestic consumers are far more resilient than export-only plants exposed to tariff cycles and currency swings in overseas markets over time.

Commerce and industry minister Piyush Goyal has made a related argument, pointing out that India's large consumer market hands the country an edge in free trade agreement talks with trading partners — leverage that smaller manufacturing rivals simply cannot match at the negotiating table.

The BCG data also frames the competitive stakes. China's $159 billion is still substantial, and its $514 billion peak from 2006-10 shows the scale of manufacturing ecosystems India has yet to replicate — a reminder that inflow momentum and industrial depth are different things.

What happens next depends on execution. The 61% growth in India's greenfield FDI proves the demand-side pull is real; converting it into jobs, exports and supplier networks will test the supply-side plumbing of ports, power, skills and credit across Indian states in the next phase.

For global capital allocators, the takeaway is blunt. As the world's factory system fragments, India has moved from backup plan to primary destination — and the $351 billion already committed suggests the reordering has a long way left to run from here.

WHAT DOES BCG'S NIKOLAUS LANG SAY INDIA NEEDS TO ATTRACT MORE INDUSTRIES?
LANG LISTED THREE PRIORITIES: MOBILISING CAPITAL, BUILDING A SKILLED WORKFORCE FOR JOBS LIKE STAMPING, PAINTING, ASSEMBLY AND MECHATRONICS, AND A DEMAND STIMULUS SO THE LOCAL MARKET IS BIG ENOUGH TO JUSTIFY LOCALISATION. GLOSSARY: Greenfield FDI: Foreign direct investment that builds new facilities and operations from scratch, rather than acquiring or expanding existing ones. Brownfield investment: Investment that involves buying, leasing or upgrading existing factories or facilities instead of constructing new ones. China Plus One strategy: A supply chain approach, adopted by multinationals after the pandemic, of keeping operations in China while adding production capacity in one or more other countries. FDI (foreign direct investment): Cross-border investment in which a company takes a lasting stake and management role in a business based in another country. Capital mobilisation: The process of pooling financial resources — from domestic savings, banks, markets or foreign investors — to fund projects and infrastructure. Free Trade Agreement (FTA): A pact between two or more countries to reduce or eliminate tariffs and trade barriers on goods and services exchanged between them.
