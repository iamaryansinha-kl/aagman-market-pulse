Bond Yields Breach 7.45% as Oil Surge Fuels Rate Hike Bets

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The benchmark 6.94% 2036 government bond yield ended at 7.4596%, breaching the psychological 7.45% level and extending a three-session slide.
  • •- The yield has risen 69 basis points over the last 10 weeks, tracking the oil shock and a shift in the RBI's policy stance.
  • •- The central bank raised the repo rate to 5.50% earlier this month — its first hike in nearly four years — and adopted a "calibrated tightening" stance.
  • •- Economists expect retail inflation to breach the RBI's 6% upper tolerance limit as oil and food prices climb.
  • •- InCred's Tanuj Khosla expects the benchmark yield to test 7.50-7.60% before year-end unless Brent retreats from $112, with a December hike likely.

The oil shock that ended the rupee's eight-day rally on Thursday found its second victim in the same session: the government bond market. Indian sovereign bonds fell for a third straight session, pushing the benchmark 6.94% 2036 bond yield to a close of 7.4596% from 7.4427% the previous day — through the 7.45% level that traders had treated as the line separating a drifting market from a repricing one. The move is not small: benchmark yields have risen 69 basis points in ten weeks, one of the sharpest sustained sell-offs in the Indian bond market in recent years. Thursday's session added roughly 1.7 basis points to the benchmark, a modest daily increment that mattered less for its size than for what it confirmed: the selling pressure is persistent, orderly, and driven by macro fundamentals rather than positioning squalls.

Two forces converged on the market. The first was crude. Brent climbed to $112.50 per barrel in Asian trading, its highest since May, after fresh Houthi attacks on Saudi Arabia stoked fears of a broader escalation and supply disruption. The second was the publication of the Reserve Bank of India's latest policy minutes, which signalled further rate hikes to come — confirmation, if any were needed, that the central bank's inflation fight is not finished. The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.50% earlier this month, its first increase in nearly four years, and shifted its stance to "calibrated tightening", language that bond markets read as a standing invitation to price the next move.

Higher oil prices and elevated US yields are pushing bond yields up across emerging markets, and India is carrying the double burden of an oil import bill that rises with every escalation headline and a food-price pulse weakened by a deficient monsoon. Economists now expect retail inflation to breach the RBI's 6% upper tolerance limit — the threshold beyond which the central bank's inflation-targeting framework demands a written explanation to the government. India imports nearly 90% of its crude, so a war premium in oil transmits directly into the import bill, the currency, and ultimately the price level, each of which tightens the policy vice on the bond market. The transmission is mechanical: costlier crude widens the trade deficit, weakens the rupee, and makes imported inflation harder to ignore, and each link in that chain argues for a higher terminal policy rate than the market was pricing even six weeks ago.

The market's rate expectations have moved accordingly. Overnight indexed swap rates, which proxy where traders think policy rates are heading, ended Thursday largely unchanged — the 1-year at 6.56%, the 2-year at 6.63% and the liquid 5-year at 6.8% — not because the outlook improved, but because most of the bearish news is already in the price. Look at what that curve is saying: the 1-year OIS sits roughly 100 basis points above the 5.50% repo rate, a gap that prices in not just one more hike but the risk of a faster sequence, while the shallow slope between the 2-year and the 5-year tells you traders think the tightening cycle will be short — painful, but front-loaded rather than prolonged. Tanuj Khosla, managing director and head of multi-strategy credit at InCred Global Asset Management, put the near-term path plainly: unless Brent retreats meaningfully from $112, he expects the benchmark yield to test 7.50-7.60% before year-end, with the RBI likely forced to hike again in December.

That December call is the hinge of the trade. If oil holds near current levels and inflation prints breach the tolerance band, a second consecutive hike becomes the base case, and the bond market will spend the next six weeks pricing it in — pushing yields higher still, steepening the government's borrowing costs, and tightening financial conditions across the economy without the RBI having to lift a finger. If crude de-escalates, the 69-basis-point sell-off starts to look overdone, and the carry on a 7.46% sovereign yield begins to attract the buyers who have been waiting out the storm. The asymmetry is what keeps the sell-off going: every day that Brent holds above $110 without a diplomatic off-ramp, the probability distribution shifts a little further toward the December hike, and the market is forced to reprice — not violently, but relentlessly. Either way, the bond market has stopped waiting for the RBI to tell it what to do: it has concluded that the central bank is behind the oil curve, and it is pricing the catch-up in advance.

For the government, the arithmetic is uncomfortable either way. Every basis point on the benchmark yield feeds into the coupon it must offer on new issuance, and a drift toward 7.60% before year-end would raise the cost of borrowing just as the fiscal math is being tested by a fertiliser and fuel subsidy bill that swells with crude. For banks and insurers sitting on large holdings of government paper, the mark-to-market losses accumulate quietly but persistently, constraining the room to add duration even at these levels. And for the RBI, the bond market's message is a warning it cannot dismiss: the longer oil holds its war premium, the more the entire domestic yield curve becomes a referendum on its credibility, priced in real time by traders who no longer need the central bank's permission to move first.

GLOSSARY: - Basis point: One-hundredth of a percentage point; the standard unit for measuring bond yield moves. - Overnight indexed swap: A derivative whose rate reflects where markets expect central bank policy rates to be in the future. - Calibrated tightening: A central bank stance signalling measured, data-dependent rate increases rather than a fixed path.
