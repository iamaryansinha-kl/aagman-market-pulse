FII Selling Hits 10th Week as Nifty Falls All Four Sessions

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- FIIs were net sellers of Indian equities for a tenth straight week, extending one of the longest sustained outflow streaks in recent years.
  • •- The Nifty 50 fell in all four sessions this week and ended near 22,600; the Sensex closed at 73,123.83, and nearly Rs 25 lakh crore of market value was wiped out.
  • •- The market capitalisation of all BSE-listed companies fell below Rs 450 lakh crore for the first time since April.
  • •- Drivers include Brent crude near six-month highs of $114-115, a hawkish RBI at 5.5%, US yields near 5% and an 80% probability priced for a December Fed hike.

There is a point at which a streak stops being a data point and becomes a regime. Foreign institutional investors sold Indian equities for a tenth consecutive week, a run of outflows that has now outlasted most pullbacks of the past decade and is testing the patience of even the most sanguine domestic investors. The weekly scorecard was grim: the Nifty 50 declined in all four sessions of the week, ending near 22,600, while the Sensex closed at 73,123.83. Nearly Rs 25 lakh crore of market capitalisation was erased in five trading days, and the total market value of all BSE-listed companies slipped below Rs 450 lakh crore for the first time since April — a psychological threshold whose breach signals how far the tide has gone out.

The causes are not mysterious, and none of them has resolved. Brent crude is parked near six-month highs around $114-115, feeding directly into India's import bill, its inflation outlook and, through both, the Reserve Bank's policy stance; the RBI has already raised the repo rate to 5.5% and shifted to calibrated tightening, with minutes that read hawkish and money markets pricing further moves. US ten-year yields hovering near 5% keep the dollar bid and emerging markets unattractive, and futures now put roughly an 80% probability on a Federal Reserve rate hike in December — the opposite of the rate-cut relief trade that powered risk assets for years. The rupee, at record-weak levels near 94.70 to the dollar, removes the last cushion: for a foreign investor, Indian equities now offer falling prices, rising hedging costs and no currency tailwind. Each of these alone would be manageable; in combination, they describe a market where the risk-reward for incremental foreign money is simply better elsewhere, and ten weeks of selling says foreign investors have noticed.

The counterweight, as it has been for months, is domestic money. DIIs have been persistent net buyers, absorbing a large share of the foreign supply and preventing the kind of air-pocket declines that unopposed outflows produce — in the week to October 10, domestic institutions bought over Rs 30,000 crore, roughly matching the foreign supply share for share. That absorption has a cost, though: it is largely rotation, not conviction — domestic funds stepping in where foreigners step out, leaving the market's direction hostage to the marginal foreign seller's appetite. And that appetite remains absent. Friday's session made the pattern plain: foreign investors sold another net Rs 3,459 crore on Thursday, have been net sellers in eleven of fourteen October sessions, and show no sign of treating the decline as a buying opportunity. When the marginal price-setter is a forced or fearful seller, every rally gets sold into.

So is the worst over? The honest answer is that the conditions for a durable bottom are not yet visible. A genuine floor typically needs one of three things: foreign selling to exhaust itself, the macro trigger to fade, or valuations to become compelling enough that buyers stop waiting. On the first, ten weeks of selling argues the positioning washout is advanced but not complete — FII index futures positioning remains heavily short, which cuts both ways, since crowded shorts can fuel sharp squeezes. On the second, the calendar is against near-term relief: the Fed meets October 27-28, US PCE data lands alongside it, and December hike pricing leaves little room for dovish surprises. On the third, strategists point out that India's premium to other emerging markets has compressed sharply from its highs, and that nearly Rs 3 lakh crore of IPO refund money is expected back with investors from Monday — liquidity that has historically found its way back into the secondary market. Seasonality offers one more thread for the optimists: the festive quarter has traditionally been kind to Indian equities, and corporate earnings so far — with banks reporting double-digit profit growth — have held up far better than the index's behaviour implies.

None of that makes a prediction; it makes a map. The bull case is a short-covering squeeze into domestic liquidity against an oversold tape — the same tape that has now fallen four weeks in five and left technical indicators at their most stretched since the 2025 correction. The bear case is that oil stays high, the Fed follows through in December, and ten weeks of FII selling becomes fifteen. What this week established is that the market can no longer shrug the outflows off — not with the Nifty falling four sessions out of four, not with Rs 25 lakh crore gone in a week, and not with every macro input still pointing the same direction. The streak will end eventually; all streaks do. The question that matters for the next fortnight is whether it ends with a capitulation flush or a grinding continuation — and on the evidence of this week, grinding still has the better odds.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
HOW LONG HAVE FIIS BEEN SELLING INDIAN STOCKS?
This week marked the tenth consecutive week of net FII selling, one of the longest sustained outflow streaks in recent years.
WHAT HAPPENED TO MARKETS THIS WEEK?
The Nifty 50 fell in all four sessions to close near 22,600, the Sensex ended at 73,123.83, nearly Rs 25 lakh crore of market value was wiped out, and total BSE market capitalisation fell below Rs 450 lakh crore for the first time since April.
WHAT WOULD STOP THE SELLING?
Analysts point to three potential circuit-breakers: exhaustion of foreign selling, fading of the macro triggers (crude, US yields, Fed hawkishness), or valuations cheap enough to attract fresh buying; plus roughly Rs 3 lakh crore of IPO refund money returns to investors from Monday. GLOSSARY: FII — Foreign Institutional Investor, overseas funds investing in Indian markets; their flows are a major driver of index direction. CAPITULATION — A final wave of panic selling that marks a market bottom, as the last unwilling holders are forced out. POSITIONING WASHOUT — The process by which overextended investor positions (here, longs held by FIIs) are unwound through sustained selling. REPO RATE — The Reserve Bank of India's key policy interest rate, currently 5.5% after recent hikes under a calibrated tightening stance.
