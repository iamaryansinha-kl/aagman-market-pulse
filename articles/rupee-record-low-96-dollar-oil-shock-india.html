Rupee Hits Record Low Above 96/Dollar as Oil Shock Bites India

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/The Indian rupee hit a record low above 96 against the dollar on May 15, down more than 5% since the Middle East crisis began in February 2026.
  • 02/India's current account deficit is likely to exceed 2% of GDP this fiscal year, the widest since 2012-13, per Bank of America Securities estimates.
  • 03/The Reserve Bank of India has drained over $23 billion from forex reserves to defend the currency, with more drastic measures potentially on the anvil.

The Indian rupee plunged to a record low above 96 against the US dollar on Friday. The currency has shed more than 5% since the Middle East crisis erupted in February 2026. It is now Asia's worst-performing major currency this year.

The Reserve Bank of India has deployed billions of dollars to stabilise the rupee. It curbed speculative trading and offered a special credit line to oil importers. Forex reserves have fallen from over $720 billion to roughly $697 billion.

Prime Minister Narendra Modi urged voluntary austerity measures to reduce dollar demand. He asked citizens to cut gold purchases and foreign travel for one year. These steps aim to ease pressure on the currency without imposing official capital controls.

India's current account deficit is likely to exceed 2% of GDP this fiscal year. That is more than double last year's level and potentially the widest since 2012-13, according to Bank of America Securities estimates.

Foreign investors have dumped more than $20 billion in Indian stocks since the conflict began. This marks the fastest pace of outflows on record. Dollar inflows have slowed, opening a balance-of-payments gap possibly as large as $67-88 billion.

"The whole system has been disturbed," said Dilip Parmar of HDFC Securities. He cited heavy foreign outflows, weaker growth and elevated crude prices as the core problem. He noted the rupee fall is ultimately a function of demand and supply.

The rupee's slide is rippling through the domestic economy. Manufacturers and food processors importing raw materials face surging costs. In Kerala's cashew industry, over 80% of processing units have shut in recent years.

Nomura analysts warn more drastic measures may follow. These could include fuel price hikes, tighter controls on overseas remittances and steps to attract dollar deposits from non-resident Indians. Economists caution intervention alone cannot reverse underlying pressures.
