India Faces Price Shock, Not Supply Crunch as Crude Soars: CEA Nageswaran

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/Chief Economic Advisor V Anantha Nageswaran on May 2, 2026, said India's crude surge is a price shock, not a supply shock, as petrochemical and petroleum import costs have climbed sharply since February 28.
  • 02/India's current account deficit, which was below 1% of GDP in FY26, could widen to 2% or higher in FY27, while the budgeted fiscal deficit target of 4.3% faces pressure from oil and fertiliser prices.
  • 03/Watch for rupee stability, FY27 CAD financing, and whether the UK and EU free trade agreements scheduled for 2026 can offset external trade risks.

Chief Economic Advisor V Anantha Nageswaran on Saturday, May 2, 2026, drew a sharp distinction between India's crude oil challenge and that of other nations. Speaking at the ICPP Growth Conference organised by Ashoka University, he declared the current scenario a "price shock and not a supply shock" for India. His assessment is based on inputs from the Ministry of Petroleum and Natural Gas.

Nageswaran mapped four distinct channels of economic impact. The first is the direct oil price channel, where petrochemicals, petroleum products, and base crude oil import costs have surged far above early-February levels. The second is trade disruption: as global growth and Gulf economies slow, India's outbound shipments face headwinds. The third covers logistics, freight, and insurance costs, which he warned are "sticky" and unlikely to return to pre-February 28 levels. The fourth is a remittance shock, with India receiving roughly $120 billion annually in remittances, of which $30-40 billion flows from the Gulf region.

The current account deficit, estimated below 1% of GDP in FY26, is now projected to climb to around 2% of GDP or higher in the current fiscal year. Rating agency Crisil had earlier flagged that a prolonged West Asia crisis could push the CAD to exactly that level, driven by a 23% year-on-year rise in crude prices and higher fertiliser import bills. Nageswaran identified managing the CAD, ensuring its financing, and preventing further rupee depreciation as key priorities for FY27.

On the fiscal front, Nageswaran conceded the FY27 budget target of 4.3% gross fiscal deficit will be "under challenge." India had brought the ratio down to 4.4% in FY26. Elevated fertiliser prices, oil prices, and petroleum product costs are now threatening that consolidation. He declined to provide revised deficit numbers at the conference.

Despite the external stress, Nageswaran highlighted a resilient FDI outlook. He projected FY26 would close with gross foreign direct investment of about $90-95 billion, signalling that global investors have not abandoned India. He also pointed to upcoming trade agreements, with the UK FTA expected to take effect in May 2026 and an EU agreement targeted before the calendar year ends, alongside an interim deal with the United States.

India's macro vulnerabilities are well-quantified. The country imports roughly 85-91% of its crude requirements, and about 50% of those imports transit through the Strait of Hormuz. Icra estimates every $10 per barrel increase in crude prices widens India's CAD by 30-40 basis points, adding roughly ₹1.3 lakh crore to the annual import bill at current exchange rates. The rupee has already breached record lows near 92.33 against the US dollar in early March, and RBI Governor Shaktikanta Das had previously noted that headline inflation could spike to 4.6% in FY27 from 2.1% in FY26 under an $85 per barrel average assumption.

Nageswaran's reassurance that India has "some fiscal leverage" compared to peers is anchored in the fact that the Centre has already reduced its gross fiscal deficit ratio from higher levels in prior years. However, with Brent crude trading near $106-115 per barrel through April and May, and global inventories drawing down at record rates, the margin for policy error is shrinking fast.
