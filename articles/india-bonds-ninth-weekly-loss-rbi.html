Indian Government Bonds Post Ninth Straight Weekly Loss on RBI

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The benchmark 6.94% 2036 bond yield rose about 8 bps this week to 7.3789%.
  • •- The nine-week climb now totals over 62 basis points.
  • •- The RBI's rupee defence and open-market liquidity drainage bruised sentiment.
  • •- Bets on a larger December rate hike lifted short-end yields; the 2031 note rose 18 bps this week.
  • •- India's 10-year premium over US Treasuries widened to 227 bps from 206.
  • •- The one-year overnight indexed swap rose 32 bps to 6.56%.

India's government bond market has now spent nine consecutive weeks in the red — a losing streak that ranks among the longest in recent memory and one that carries a clear author. The benchmark 6.94 per cent 2036 bond yield rose around 8 basis points this week to 7.3789 per cent, extending a climb that has now totalled more than 62 basis points since the streak began. The proximate cause is the Reserve Bank of India, whose defence of the rupee has tightened liquidity, sharpened its policy bias and left bond investors pricing a central bank that is no longer fighting only inflation.

The mechanics run through the currency. To keep the rupee orderly, the RBI has sold dollars from its reserves — nearly $60 billion from the September peak — and offset the rupee liquidity that leaves the system by draining it elsewhere, including through open-market sales of government bonds. Both operations land on the same market: more supply of bonds, less cash to buy them. The governor's comments on Tuesday added the policy dimension, saying that while monetary policy is not generally the tool for safeguarding financial stability, "the two cannot be fully separated." Nomura's read was blunter: "RBI comments suggest that inflation is no longer the sole driver of monetary policy decisions. If global conditions remain stressed, more measures against speculation and targeting BOP flows are likely to be announced."

The market has responded by repricing the December meeting. Expectations of a rate hike had already firmed after the October surprise; this week's rhetoric pushed bets toward something larger, lifting short-end yields sharply — the 6.36 per cent 2031 note rose 18 basis points this week. The overnight indexed swap curve flattened violently: the one-year OIS rose 32 basis points to 6.56 per cent, its biggest weekly rise this financial year, while the two-year surged 18 basis points to 6.6225 per cent. The swaps market is effectively pricing a front-loaded tightening cycle driven by external-defence logic rather than domestic demand.

There is a grimmer interpretation doing the rounds among traders: that the RBI may simply tolerate higher bond yields to preserve the interest-rate spread over US Treasuries, making Indian assets harder to bet against. India's 10-year premium over its US counterpart widened to 227 basis points this week from 206 — a meaningful re-widening that improves the carry case for foreign investors even as it raises the government's borrowing cost. With a heavy central and state borrowing calendar ahead, every basis point of that premium lands in the fiscal math.

The one counterweight came from overseas: US Treasury yields fell over 13 basis points this week to 5.11 per cent, heading for their best week since the Iran war began in February. That easing gave emerging-market debt a tailwind everywhere except, notably, India — where domestic policy is currently the dominant force. It is an unusual inversion: Indian bonds are behaving like a tightening-cycle market in a world where the global cycle is finally turning friendlier.

For the week's narrative, the bond market is where the RBI's rupee war shows up in domestic prices. Reserves falling by $60 billion is an abstraction; a 7.38 per cent 10-year yield and a 32-basis-point weekly jump in one-year swaps is a cost that borrowers, banks' treasury desks and the government's interest bill all feel immediately. The RBI has signalled it considers that cost acceptable — even useful — in the fight against speculative pressure on the currency. Nine weeks of losses say the bond market believes it, and is pricing accordingly.

The streak also has distributional consequences worth noting. Banks, which hold the bulk of government bonds, are sitting on widening treasury losses even as their lending margins benefit from the higher-rate environment — the same divergence that showed up in this week's PSU-bank results, where those with one-off gains masked underlying treasury pain. Insurance companies and mutual funds face mark-to-market pressure on their duration books. And the government's own borrowing programme, which must be absorbed at these higher yields, gets progressively more expensive the longer the streak runs. Every participant in the bond market is effectively paying a share of the rupee's defence budget.

Whether the streak ends soon depends less on domestic data than on the external variables the RBI cannot control: US yields, oil prices and the dollar's global direction. The week's drop in US 10-year yields to 5.11 per cent is the first genuinely helpful signal in months — if it holds, the pressure to keep Indian yields elevated as a defence mechanism eases, and the December meeting could yet deliver the hike without the extra hawkishness the swaps market now fears. Bond investors, nursing nine weeks of losses, are entitled to hope the turn is close. They have earned the right with their patience, and paid for it with their portfolios.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHY HAVE INDIAN BONDS FALLEN FOR NINE WEEKS?
The RBI's rupee defence drains liquidity and signals a tighter policy path; weekly losses have pushed the benchmark 2036 yield up over 62 bps since the streak began.
WHAT HAPPENED THIS WEEK?
The 2036 bond yield rose ~8 bps to 7.3789%, the 2031 note rose 18 bps, and the 1-year OIS jumped 32 bps to 6.56%.
WHAT IS THE MARKET PRICING FOR DECEMBER?
Rising bets on a larger rate hike, with the swaps curve signalling front-loaded tightening driven by balance-of-payments defence rather than demand pressure.
DOES THE RBI WANT HIGHER YIELDS?
Traders suspect the central bank will tolerate them to preserve the 227 bps spread over US Treasuries, making shorting the rupee costlier. GLOSSARY: - Open-market sales: The RBI selling government bonds to banks to absorb rupee liquidity. - Overnight indexed swap (OIS): A derivative whose rate reflects expectations of average policy rates over a horizon. - Basis point: One-hundredth of a percentage point; bond yields and rates move in these units. - Spread over US Treasuries: The extra yield Indian bonds offer over American ones — a gauge of relative attractiveness to foreign capital.
