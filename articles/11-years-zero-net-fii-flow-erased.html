$64 Billion FII Exit Wipes Out Nine Years of India Stock Buying

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Cumulative FII equity flows since October 2015 stand at approximately minus $110 million — effectively zero.
  • •- $63.6 billion of selling since October 2024 erased the $63.5 billion accumulated over the preceding nine years.
  • •- Foreign investors have sold $32.8 billion in 2026 after $18.9 billion in 2025; March saw the worst month at $12.7 billion.
  • •- Financial services bore the brunt: $12.2 billion of selling, plus $4.1 billion in autos and $3.9 billion in oil and gas.
  • •- Foreign money is not gone — $21 billion flowed into primary-market offerings while $63 billion left secondaries.
  • •- HSBC notes the Nifty trades at about 16 times forward earnings, a level hit only twice in a decade.

Eleven years of foreign investment in Indian equities, measured end to end, now net out to approximately minus $110 million. That is the extraordinary arithmetic compiled from NSDL and exchange data: between October 2015 and October 2024, foreign institutional investors accumulated $63.5 billion of Indian equity. Since October 2024, they have sold $63.6 billion. Two years of sustained exit have cancelled nearly a decade of conviction — not in a crash, but in a slow, grinding redistribution of ownership from foreign hands to domestic ones that the market has absorbed, session by session, without ever fully pricing.

The selling has intensified rather than faded. Foreign investors have withdrawn $32.8 billion from Indian equities so far in calendar 2026, following $18.9 billion in 2025, and the monthly register shows how relentless the exit has been: March 2026 brought the largest single-month withdrawal at $12.7 billion, followed by $6.5 billion in April and $5.2 billion in June. The drivers are the ones this column has tracked all year — US Treasury yields at 5.3 per cent, an 18-month-high dollar, crude above $100, and the simple mathematics of relative returns: Amnish Aggarwal of PL Capital notes the Nifty 100 has delivered just 1.2 per cent annualised in dollar terms over five years, a return no global allocator can defend against a risk-free rate twice that level.

The exit, though, has a shape that aggregate numbers hide. Selling has concentrated in foreign-heavy sectors: $12.2 billion out of financial services, $4.1 billion out of automobiles and $3.9 billion out of oil and gas in 2026 alone, according to Motilal Oswal Financial Services, while foreign investors actually added to metals, capital goods and services. Because financials dominate the large-cap indices, their exit explains the peculiar divergence of this bull market — mid- and small-caps, where domestic money plays, have outperformed large-caps consistently while the benchmark has been carried by a narrowing band of stocks. The flow reset is, in effect, a style reset.

Two details from the data complicate the easy narrative of foreign abandonment. The first: foreigners sold nearly $63 billion in the secondary market over two years but invested $21 billion in primary-market offerings — meaning the money has not left India so much as changed its entry point, favouring new issues and priced deals over listed shares at full valuations. The Jio Platforms IPO opening this week will test exactly that preference. The second: domestic absorption has been total — in September alone, foreign investors sold $4.1 billion while domestic institutions invested $8 billion, nearly twice the supply. Kotak Institutional Equities offered the contrarian framing that flow explanations for price moves are "risible" and that earnings and multiples do the real work — a view that two years of domestic buying would support.

The valuation argument cuts both ways. HSBC notes the Nifty 50 trades at about 16 times twelve-month forward earnings, a level reached only twice in the past decade, with financials at 1.7 times forward book — roughly 30 per cent below their five-year average of 2.4 times — and IT at about 18 times forward earnings against a five-year mean near 23. On every historical yardstick, the exit has left Indian equities cheap for the first time in the cheap-money era, and HSBC argues global investors rotating out of AI-concentrated markets will eventually notice. The counterweight is the one that has governed all year: none of the valuation support activates while US yields stay at 5.3 per cent and oil stays above $100, because the dollar-return math that drives the selling ignores the multiple.

Eleven years of net zero, then, is less a verdict on India than a measurement of a regime change. The foreign investor who underwrote India's 2014-2024 bull market has been replaced, seat by seat, by the domestic systematic investor — and the market that resulted is younger, less valuation-tolerant, and structurally more volatile in both directions. The $110 million number is an accounting artefact of that handover. What it measures is not what foreigners think of India, but who owns India now — and the answer, for the first time in the modern market's history, is Indians.

The implications reach beyond flows into the market's own reflexes. A shareholder base dominated by systematic domestic money behaves differently from one dominated by global allocators: it sells less in a panic, buys more mechanically on dips, and cares less about quarterly currency moves and more about local earnings — which is why corrections in 2025 and 2026 have been shallower and faster to recover than the macro headlines warranted. But the same structure has a failure mode: if domestic flows ever falter — a prolonged SIP slowdown, an insurance-industry regulatory squeeze on equity allocations — there is no foreign bid waiting to replace them at today's valuations. The eleven-year flow chart is therefore both a triumph and a dependency. India finally owns its own market. It now also bears its own risk, alone.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DOES ZERO NET FII FLOW OVER 11 YEARS MEAN?
Foreign investors bought $63.5 billion of Indian equities between October 2015 and October 2024, then sold $63.6 billion since, leaving cumulative flows near zero.
WHY ARE FOREIGN INVESTORS SELLING?
US Treasury yields near 5.3%, a strong dollar, oil above $100 and weak dollar returns — just 1.2% annualised on the Nifty 100 over five years — have made other assets more attractive.
WHICH SECTORS SAW THE HEAVIEST SELLING?
Financial services ($12.2 billion), automobiles ($4.1 billion) and oil and gas ($3.9 billion) in 2026, per Motilal Oswal data.
HAVE FOREIGN INVESTORS COMPLETELY LEFT INDIA?
No. They sold $63 billion in secondary markets but invested $21 billion in primary offerings, shifting toward new issues rather than exiting the country. GLOSSARY: - FII: Foreign institutional investor — overseas institutions investing in Indian markets. - Cumulative flows: The running total of money invested minus money withdrawn over a period. - Forward earnings multiple: A stock index's price divided by expected next-twelve-month profits. - Primary market: New-share offerings such as IPOs, as distinct from trading in listed stocks.
