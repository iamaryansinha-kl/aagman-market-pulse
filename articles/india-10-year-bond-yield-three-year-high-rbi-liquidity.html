India 10-Year Bond Yield at 7.37% Three-Year High as RBI Drains Cash

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/India's benchmark 10-year bond yield hit 7.3707% on October 12, its highest in three years, extending a run of eight straight weekly gains
  • 02/The RBI will sell Rs 250 billion of bonds on October 13 via an OMO sale, and from October 16 banks must hold 99% of their daily cash reserve requirement, up from 90%
  • 03/Overnight indexed swap rates jumped to 6.34% at the one-year end, signalling the market is pricing further rate hikes after last week's first increase in nearly four years

Indian government bonds fell sharply on Monday, October 12, 2026. The yield on the benchmark 6.94% 2036 bond rose to 7.3707% by 10:20 a.m. IST, from 7.2994% at Friday's close.

That is the highest level in three years for the benchmark yield. It also marks an eighth consecutive week of increases, taking the cumulative rise over the period to 54 basis points.

The immediate trigger is the Reserve Bank of India's decision to drain liquidity from the banking system. The central bank will sell Rs 250 billion ($2.58 billion) of bonds on Tuesday, October 13, through an open market operation sale.

A second measure kicks in from October 16. Banks will be required to maintain 99% of their daily cash reserve requirement, up sharply from the current 90%.

Together, the two steps tighten the surplus cash that had been suppressing short-term rates. They come just days after the RBI raised its key policy rate for the first time in nearly four years and signalled that more hikes are likely.

The liquidity reversal has sharpened questions about the central bank's communication. At the policy meeting, the RBI announced no liquidity-draining measures, and Governor Sanjay Malhotra said the recent surge in surplus cash would be temporary.

The shift within days caught traders off guard. "Such flip-flops impact the credibility and often lead to a larger reaction in asset prices, as compared to what would have been," a trader at a primary dealership said.

Currency measures are adding to the tightening. The RBI has allowed oil marketing companies to buy dollars directly from the central bank through a special window and has tightened hedging rules. Traders said these steps will also deplete rupee liquidity.

The rates market is now repricing aggressively. Overnight indexed swap rates moved sharply at the shorter end: the one-year OIS is at 6.34%, the two-year at 6.53%, and the five-year at 6.76%.

That curve shift suggests investors expect the policy tightening cycle to run further than they had assumed a week ago. Shorter-dated swaps reprice fastest when markets conclude that more hikes are coming.

Supply pressure is not limited to the RBI's auction. Indian debt funds are set to extend a $4.5 billion bond selloff following the central bank's policy shift, a move that would add to upward pressure on yields.

External factors are complicating the picture. Brent crude remained above $100 per barrel, a concern for oil-import-dependent India's inflation trajectory and government finances.

Domestic price data lands the same day. India's September retail inflation print is due later on Monday, with a Reuters poll expecting a rise of 5.4% year-on-year, up from the previous month.

A print at or above that level would reinforce the case for additional rate increases. It would also test whether the benchmark yield can hold below the new three-year high of 7.3707%.

For bond market participants, the sequencing matters as much as the measures themselves. At its October policy review, the RBI had said the flood of surplus liquidity would be temporary and announced no drainage steps — only to return within days with a quarter-trillion-rupee bond sale and a tighter reserve rule. A trader at a primary dealership put the market's frustration plainly: flip-flops on liquidity damage the central bank's credibility and tend to produce larger price reactions than a consistent message would have.

The arithmetic behind the squeeze is straightforward. Requiring banks to hold 99 per cent of their mandated cash reserves against the earlier 90 per cent locks up more of the liquidity parked at the central bank, while the Rs 250 billion open market operation on Tuesday pulls cash out outright. Neither move touches the policy rate — and none was needed to move the market, because the quantity of money, not just its price, sets bond yields at the margin.

The rupee channel compounds it. The special window letting oil marketing companies buy dollars directly from the RBI removes a large, predictable source of dollar demand from the market, but every dollar the central bank sells drains an equivalent amount of rupee liquidity from the banking system. Traders noted the hedging-rule tightening has the same indirect effect: stabilising the currency while shrinking the domestic cash pool.

The repricing is now visible across the rates curve. Overnight indexed swaps — the market's purest expression of where policy is heading — jumped most at the short end, with the one-year at 6.34 per cent, two-year at 6.53 per cent and five-year at 6.76 per cent. That is the bond market doing what the RBI's language has not yet done: pricing in more hikes than the policy statement implied.

The backdrop adds pressure from outside the central bank. Brent crude held above $100 a barrel, a direct risk to India's import bill and government finances, and September retail inflation data is due the same day, with a Reuters poll pencilling in 5.4 per cent. Mutual funds, which had been buyers through the year, are positioned to extend a $4.5 billion selloff. Eight straight weeks of rising yields — 54 basis points in total — suggest this is no longer a blip but a regime change in Indian rates.

GLOSSARY: Open market operation (OMO): The central bank buying or selling government bonds to add or drain banking-system liquidity. Cash reserve requirement: The share of deposits banks must hold with the RBI; the maintenance ratio sets how consistently it must be met. Overnight indexed swap (OIS): An interest-rate swap whose fixed leg reflects the market's expectation of average policy rates over the tenor. Primary dealership: A bank or broker authorised to deal directly with the RBI in government securities auctions and operations.
