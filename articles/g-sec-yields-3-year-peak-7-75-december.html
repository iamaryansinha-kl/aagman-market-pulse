G-Sec Yields at 3-Year Peak, May Hit 7.75% by December

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • Key points unavailable

India's bond market is sending a signal that the equity market has been slow to absorb. The benchmark 10-year government security yield settled at 7.47% on Friday, its highest closing level since March 2023 — a three-year peak. The move has been relentless rather than dramatic: a steady grind higher that has caught equity investors focused on earnings season largely by surprise.

The yield has now surged 78 basis points in just four months. It is closing in on the 7.61% high touched during the 2022-23 monetary tightening cycle, the previous episode of aggressive rate increases. For bond market veterans, that comparison carries weight — the 2022-23 peak marked the top of a cycle that ultimately required the central bank to hike aggressively to tame inflation.

Traders and treasury heads say the move has further to run. Rajeev Pawar, head of treasury at Ujjivan Small Finance Bank, expects the 10-year yield to touch 7.75% by December.

"The rate differential between India and the US justifies higher yields," Pawar said. "At a time when the market is struggling with heavy bond supply, liquidity is getting drained and crude oil prices are moving higher."

The biggest risk, dealers say, is crude. Brent crude futures rose to $116.72 a barrel, their highest level since April, as fears persist that supply disruptions linked to the US-Iran conflict will last through year-end. Every sustained $10 increase in the price of crude adds measurably to India's import bill, worsens the trade balance and keeps the rupee under pressure — a currency dynamic that foreign investors watch closely when deciding whether to hold Indian debt.

That matters for India more than most markets. The country imports the bulk of its crude requirements, so sustained oil above $115 raises both inflation risks and the current account deficit — the two forces that pull bond yields higher. A wider deficit means more demand for dollars, a softer rupee, and an inflation impulse that the central bank cannot ignore.

The market's earlier assumption that oil disruption would be temporary has given way to fears that elevated prices could persist, keeping inflation risks alive and delaying any meaningful decline in yields.

Supply and liquidity are compounding the problem. A heavy government borrowing calendar means a steady stream of paper for the market to absorb, while tight banking-system liquidity keeps short-term rates elevated. When the banking system is short of funds, bond dealers have less room to warehouse government securities, which amplifies each auction's impact on yields.

Foreign investors have not helped. Foreign portfolio investors have sold Rs 20,772 crore of Indian debt so far this fiscal year, according to NSDL data, removing one of the buyer groups that had anchored the long end of the curve. In a falling market, the absence of a marginal buyer matters as much as the presence of a seller.

There is also the expectation of further monetary tightening. The RBI has already been draining liquidity, and dealers price in the possibility that policy rates follow if crude-driven inflation pressure builds. That expectation creates a self-reinforcing loop: anticipating tightening, dealers sell bonds, yields rise, and the central bank's task becomes harder.

The stress is not confined to India. Tanuj Khosla, managing director at InCred Global Asset Management, described it as an oil-and-treasury shock spreading across emerging markets, with the interplay of crude prices and US yields pressuring currencies and bond markets worldwide.

For borrowers, the cost is already visible. Higher G-Sec yields transmit directly into corporate bond pricing, bank lending rates and the government's own interest bill — Sun Pharma reportedly fell short of its bond fundraising target amid the rising-yield environment. For a government running a large fiscal deficit, every basis point on the benchmark yield translates into thousands of crores of additional interest payments over the life of its borrowings.

For equity investors, rising bond yields matter through the valuation channel. When the risk-free rate climbs, the present value of future corporate earnings falls, and the high-multiple stocks that led previous rallies tend to de-rate first. That mechanism has been visible through October, when rate-sensitive and high-valuation segments of the market sold off even as value and cyclical pockets held up better.

For the RBI, the bind is familiar. Yields rising on imported inflation is a problem monetary policy cannot fix; only cheaper crude or a calmer currency can do that. Governor Malhotra's calibrated-tightening stance — draining liquidity while holding the policy rate — has so far prevented disorderly moves, but the direction of travel is set by factors outside Mint Street's control.

A sustained move to 7.75% would put the 10-year yield above its 2022-23 cycle peak, effectively pricing the bond market for a tightening cycle that the equity market has only begun to consider. The two markets rarely disagree for long — historically, either bonds soften or equities catch down to the rate reality. Through October, it has been equities doing the catching down.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHERE IS THE 10-YEAR G-SEC YIELD NOW?
It settled at 7.47% on Friday, October 24, the highest since March 2023. The yield has risen 78 basis points in the past four months and is approaching the 7.61% peak seen during the 2022-23 rate-hike cycle.
WHY ARE BOND YIELDS RISING?
A combination of elevated crude oil prices (Brent at $116.72 a barrel), a heavy government bond supply calendar, tight banking-system liquidity, foreign investor selling of Rs 20,772 crore so far this fiscal year, and expectations of further RBI monetary tightening.
WHAT WOULD PUSH YIELDS TO 7.75%?
Treasury dealers say yields keep climbing unless crude prices cool. Persistent oil-driven inflation pressure, continued heavy supply and further policy tightening could take the 10-year yield to 7.75% by December, above the previous cycle peak.
HOW DOES THIS AFFECT BORROWERS?
Higher G-Sec yields raise benchmark rates for corporate bonds, bank loans and government borrowing alike. Companies trying to raise funds in the bond market — like Sun Pharma, which reportedly fell short of its target — are already facing headwinds from rising yields. GLOSSARY: G-SEC: A government security — a bond issued by the Indian government. The 10-year G-SEC yield is India's benchmark interest rate and sets the reference for most other borrowing costs. BASIS POINT (BP): One hundredth of a percentage point. A 78bp rise in yields means the 10-year moved from, say, 6.69% to 7.47%. NET INTEREST MARGIN (NIM): For banks, the gap between what they earn on loans and pay on deposits. Weak NIMs compress bank profitability, one reason the lending sector's profit share fell this quarter. CURRENT ACCOUNT DEFICIT (CAD): The excess of a country's imports of goods and services over its exports. India imports most of its crude oil, so expensive oil widens the CAD and weakens the rupee — both bad for bond yields. FPI (FOREIGN PORTFOLIO INVESTOR): An overseas investor buying Indian stocks, bonds or other securities. FPI debt sales of Rs 20,772 crore this fiscal year have removed a key buyer from the bond market. NSDL: National Securities Depository Limited, which tracks and reports foreign investor flows into Indian markets.
