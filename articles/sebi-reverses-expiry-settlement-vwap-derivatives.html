SEBI Scraps Closing Auction for Derivative Expiry Settlement

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Derivative settlements on expiry days will again use the volume-weighted average price of underlying stocks in the final 30 minutes of trading.
  • •- The move reverses the Closing Auction Session mechanism introduced on August 3, which caused wild swings in settlement prices.
  • •- Average daily equity derivatives turnover fell to an 18-month low on the NSE in the auction's first month, and dropped by a third on the BSE.
  • •- Brokers expect greater predictability to gradually restore volumes toward pre-auction levels.
  • •- Jefferies sees lower expiry-day volatility benefiting brokers, and kept a buy on Groww's operator Billionbrains Garage Ventures.
  • •- SEBI's wider curbs on speculative derivatives trading remain in place.

India's equity-derivatives market, battered by a year of regulatory tightening, received an unexpected reprieve late Thursday when the Securities and Exchange Board of India announced a reversal of its most disruptive recent change. Expiry-day settlement of derivatives will once again be calculated using the volume-weighted average price of underlying stocks during the final thirty minutes of continuous trading — abandoning the Closing Auction Session mechanism, introduced on August 3, that had thrown settlement prices into chaos and sent trading volumes to multi-year lows. It is among the most significant regulatory climbdowns in the market's recent history, and the market's first response was relief.

The August experiment was well-intentioned. The CAS was designed to produce a single, orderly closing price for the thousands of index and stock contracts expiring each week, replacing the final-half-hour VWAP that SEBI believed could be manipulated by large orders in thin trading. In practice, the auction concentrated all expiry pressure into a short window with limited participation, and the reference prices it produced swung wildly — settlement values jumped or dropped by amounts unrelated to the day's trading, option writers and hedgers were marked against prices they could not reasonably have anticipated, and confidence in the mechanism collapsed within weeks. In the month the system launched, average daily turnover in equity derivatives fell to an eighteen-month low on the National Stock Exchange and dropped by roughly a third on the BSE, before a partial rebound in September.

The restoration of the thirty-minute VWAP directly addresses the failure. "Participants going ahead will have greater clarity and predictability around expiry settlement prices," said Dhiraj Relli, managing director of HDFC Securities, adding that the change should restore trader confidence and, over time, bring derivatives volumes back toward pre-CAS levels. Eshaan Lazarus, founder of broker 021 Trade, identified the specific pathology the auction created: lower liquidity meant participants with relatively small books could influence closing prices in thinly traded instruments, and reverting to VWAP removes that leverage. Arbitrageurs stand to benefit most directly — the old methodology restores their ability to hedge large positions across cash and derivatives without absorbing auction-window noise.

The economics of the reversal run through the brokerage industry. Jefferies analysts noted Friday that reduced expiry-day volatility should benefit brokers whose order volumes remain below pre-auction levels, and maintained a buy rating on Billionbrains Garage Ventures, the operator of retail platform Groww. Every incremental recovery in derivatives turnover flows directly into transaction revenue for brokers and exchanges alike, which is why the brokerage stocks have treated the announcement as a sector event rather than a technical footnote. The revival of prop-desk activity and the return of traders who sat out the auction months add a second layer of volume recovery on top.

Two qualifications keep the moment in proportion. First, SEBI's broader clampdown on speculative derivatives trading — the position-limit increases, the expiry-week restrictions, the margin escalations that halved retail F&O participation over two years — remains fully in force; Thursday's reversal repairs one mechanism, not the policy. Second, the VWAP method carries its own known vulnerabilities, which is why SEBI replaced it in August; whether those vulnerabilities resurface now that participants know the auction is gone will determine if this reversal sticks or becomes a pendulum. For now, though, the regulator has done the rarer and harder thing in Indian market regulation: admitted, with an announcement rather than a white paper, that an experiment failed — and handed a battered market the predictability it had been asking for.

The reversal also carries a signal beyond the derivatives pit. SEBI spent two years tightening every stage of the retail trading pipeline, persuaded that speculative F&O volumes were a social harm worth suppressing; the volume collapse that followed the August auction showed how much of the remaining activity was professional hedging and arbitrage rather than retail punting, and Thursday's reversal reads as an implicit recognition of that. The regulator's instinct to protect small traders remains intact — the curbs on position sizes and expiry frequency are not going anywhere — but the episode has drawn a boundary around how far structural engineering can be pushed before the plumbing itself fails. Markets tolerate friction; they do not tolerate arbitrary settlement prices, because everything from margin calls to hedge ratios keys off them. In choosing predictability over purity, SEBI has repaired the one thing a derivatives market cannot function without: trust in the number at which positions close. The test arrives with the next quarterly expiry cycle, when volumes will reveal whether the departed traders return as quickly as they left — and whether the episode enters the record as a two-month anomaly or as the costliest detour in the exchange's recent history.

For the exchanges themselves, the stakes are concrete: BSE, whose derivatives franchise is smaller and was hit proportionally harder — volumes fell by a third under the auction — has the most to recover, while the NSE's eighteen-month low prints the depth of the damage even at scale. A durable volume recovery would rebuild the transaction-revenue base that has underwritten both bourses' valuations through the curbs era, and a failure to recover would harden the market's judgement that structural demand for Indian derivatives has permanently reset lower.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID SEBI CHANGE?
Expiry-day derivative settlement reverts to the volume-weighted average price of underlying stocks in the final 30 minutes of trading, replacing the closing auction system launched August 3.
WHY WAS THE CLOSING AUCTION SYSTEM REVERSED?
It caused wild swings in settlement prices, allowed small players to influence closes in thin instruments, and drove derivatives turnover to an 18-month low on the NSE.
WHO BENEFITS?
Arbitrageurs, proprietary desks, option writers and brokers — Jefferies kept a buy on Groww's operator Billionbrains Garage Ventures, expecting volumes to recover.
DID SEBI ROLL BACK ITS WIDER F&O CURBS?
No. The broader restrictions on speculative derivatives trading remain; only the settlement mechanism changed. GLOSSARY: - VWAP: Volume-weighted average price — the average price of a security weighted by traded volume over a period. - Closing Auction Session: A short, concentrated window at market close intended to determine a single official closing price. - Expiry settlement: The process of setting final values for derivatives contracts as they expire. - Arbitrageur: A trader who profits from price differences between related instruments or markets.
