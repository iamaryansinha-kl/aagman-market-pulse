Oil Jumps to $107 as Houthis Hit Saudi Aramco Building in Dhahran

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Brent crude futures rose $1.40, or 1.35%, to $107.35 a barrel, while WTI gained 1.32% to $95.38.
  • •- A Houthi attack on an administrative building at Saudi Aramco's Dhahran headquarters triggered a limited fire, Saudi Arabia's Energy Ministry said.
  • •- Houthis have launched ballistic missiles and drones at Saudi sites for a week; Riyadh's airport was struck four times, including a Saturday attack that killed 13 people.
  • •- Goldman Sachs outlined a scenario where oil rises to $120 a barrel if attacks on Middle East shipping escalate.
  • •- Saudi Arabia increased production last month despite US-Iran war disruptions, OPEC+ said.

Oil prices climbed more than 1 per cent on Wednesday after an administrative building at Saudi Aramco's headquarters in the eastern city of Dhahran was attacked, triggering a limited fire, the kingdom's Energy Ministry said.

Brent crude futures rose $1.40, or 1.35 per cent, to $107.35 a barrel, while US West Texas Intermediate crude gained $1.24, or 1.32 per cent, to $95.38. Brent had slipped 0.9 per cent on Tuesday, while WTI rose 0.4 per cent.

The Dhahran complex is not a peripheral site. It houses a major operations centre for Aramco, which manages an extensive network of onshore and offshore oil rigs, pipelines, refineries and export terminals — making any strike on it a direct test of the kingdom's ability to protect its energy infrastructure.

For the past week, the Houthis have been launching ballistic missiles and drones at key Saudi sites in retaliation for the kingdom's support for government forces in Yemen's renewed civil war. Riyadh's international airport has been struck four times, including a Saturday attack that killed 13 people. On Tuesday, the Houthis warned all employees and civilians near the kingdom's oil facilities to evacuate, describing the sites as a "legitimate target."

The attack came hours after those warnings, and the exchange escalated quickly: shortly after the evacuation order, explosions were heard in Sanaa, and media outlets affiliated with the Houthis said Saudi-backed forces had carried out air strikes on an oil facility in the al-Sabah area.

Markets are now weighing the risk of supply disruptions from the widening Houthi-Saudi fighting against the prospect of increased diesel supplies from Russia. Two other forces are layered into the price. Saudi Arabia increased oil production last month despite disruptions to shipments caused by the US-Iran war, contributing to a sharp rise in OPEC+ output, the group said on Tuesday, and OPEC raised its oil demand forecast for 2027, citing the need to rebuild stocks.

Iran, meanwhile, is adopting new tactics to target ships in the Strait of Hormuz in an effort to keep oil prices elevated and influence the US midterm elections, Reuters reported, citing two senior Iranian officials. That framing — attacks calibrated for political effect rather than military outcome — is what unnerves traders most, because it implies no quick de-escalation path.

Bank research desks are struggling to model the war. JPMorgan analysts said they were unable to forecast how the conflict would ultimately unfold, noting that when the war began they had assumed the US administration would avoid crossing certain economic thresholds. Seven months in, many of those thresholds have been crossed while a clear exit strategy remains absent.

Goldman Sachs has sketched the risk range explicitly. Daan Struyven, co-head of global commodities research at the bank, said recent attacks showed how disruptions to shipping could spread and intensify, and outlined a scenario in which oil prices rise to $120 a barrel if attacks on vessels in the Middle East escalate. If exports return to normal, the bank expects prices to move back towards $80.

Struyven added that risks to shipping have become a major factor driving oil prices, with significant upside potential for crude and expectations that natural gas and refined product prices will rise too. Supply shocks affecting gas and fuels, he said, are larger than those in the crude oil market itself — a distinction that matters for import-dependent economies like India, where the pass-through from fuel prices to inflation is direct and fast.

For India, $107 Brent is uncomfortable arithmetic. Every sustained $10 increase in crude adds pressure to the import bill, the trade deficit and the rupee — the same rupee the Reserve Bank of India has spent the past week defending with a six-measure package of forex curbs. Wednesday's move puts the RBI's inflation fight, already strained by a 5.54 per cent CPI print, under fresh strain, and raises the odds that fuel-price pass-through returns to the policy debate before the December central bank meeting.

The offset, for now, is supply. Saudi Arabia raised production last month even as the US-Iran war disrupted shipments elsewhere, and the resulting jump in OPEC+ output has kept the market from pricing a true shortage. Traders are effectively weighing two opposing forces: a genuine geopolitical risk premium pushing prices up, and surplus production capacity pushing them back down. Wednesday's bid suggests the risk premium is winning the argument.

What happens next hinges on the Houthis' stated targets. Having declared oil facilities a "legitimate target" and demonstrated reach with the Dhahran strike, any follow-on attack on export terminals or the East-West pipeline would test the $120 scenario quickly. Conversely, a pause in strikes — or successful Saudi interception — would let the surplus-supply narrative reassert itself, and prices could retrace a large part of the week's geopolitical premium.

For energy-importing economies, the prudent read is that the geopolitical floor under oil prices has risen. Seven months into the conflict, the thresholds that once anchored analyst models have been crossed repeatedly, and neither the US nor regional players have articulated an exit. Until one appears, every headline from the Gulf will carry a price tag.

GLOSSARY: - Brent crude: The global benchmark price for oil, set by trading of North Sea crude and used to price most of the world's oil. - WTI: West Texas Intermediate, the US benchmark crude price. - OPEC+: The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries plus allied producers including Russia. - Strait of Hormuz: The narrow waterway through which roughly a fifth of global oil supply passes. - Pass-through: The extent to which changes in input costs, like crude oil, translate into changes in consumer prices.
