Shriram Finance Profit Jumps 59% on Lower Funding Costs

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Standalone net profit rose 59.4% year-on-year to Rs 3,677 crore, from Rs 2,307 crore a year earlier, and 6.8% sequentially.
  • •- Net interest income surged 38% to Rs 8,661 crore as interest income rose 15.6% and interest expenses fell 9.9%.
  • •- Net interest margin jumped to 9.52% from 8.19%; management expects around 9% for the next two quarters.
  • •- Assets under management grew 17% to Rs 3.29 lakh crore; the board declared an interim dividend of Rs 6.50 per share.

The cheapest funding environment in years is handing India's shadow banks a windfall, and Shriram Finance is collecting it in full. The country's largest retail-focused non-banking finance company on Friday reported a 59.4% year-on-year jump in standalone net profit to Rs 3,677 crore for the September quarter, from Rs 2,307 crore a year earlier — and, notably, up 6.8% even from an already-strong June quarter. The engine was net interest income, which surged 38% to Rs 8,661 crore, built on a simple but powerful combination: interest income rose 15.6% to Rs 13,669 crore while interest expenses fell 9.9% to Rs 5,008 crore. When a lender's cost of money falls while its loan book grows, the margin in between expands violently — and it did. Net interest margin jumped 133 basis points to 9.52%, from 8.19% a year earlier.

The full profit-and-loss picture was uniformly strong. Operating profit rose 53.3% to Rs 6,809 crore; core operating profit, excluding other income, rose 53.5% to Rs 6,682 crore; total income grew 15.8% to Rs 13,799 crore; and profit before tax climbed 58.6% to Rs 4,932 crore. The cost-to-income ratio improved sharply to 22.52% from 27.76% — evidence that the operating leverage of a growing book is finally showing up in the expense line. One number went the other way, and deliberately: loan losses and provisions rose 41% to Rs 1,877 crore, from Rs 1,333 crore a year earlier and up 28.3% sequentially. That is the company pre-provisioning for the riskier end of its portfolio even as profits boom — a choice that flatters future quarters' comparability at the cost of this one's optics, which absorbed the hit without blinking. It is also the honest way to grow a book this fast: when a lender's AUM is expanding 17%, the prudent assumption is that some of the new lending will sour on a lag, and building the buffer before the market asks for it is what separates a managed cycle from a surprise.

Growth was broad-based across products, with one spectacular outlier. Assets under management grew 17% year-on-year and 4.9% sequentially to Rs 3.29 lakh crore at September-end; the core vehicle and equipment portfolio stood at Rs 2.61 lakh crore, or 79.46% of the total. Within that, gold loans were the fastest-growing segment — up 68.2% to Rs 8,974 crore — followed by passenger vehicle loans up 20% to Rs 71,542 crore, commercial vehicle loans up 19.4% to roughly Rs 1.53 lakh crore, and personal loans up 18% to Rs 12,415 crore. Construction equipment financing was the lone meaningful decliner, shrinking 22.4% to Rs 11,930 crore. The mix says a lot about where India's credit demand actually is right now: small-ticket, consumption-linked, and increasingly secured against assets whose value a lender can verify in a day — gold first among them, given how quickly the metal's price run has made household holdings a viable borrowing base.

Asset quality was a study in mixed signals. The gross stage-three ratio — the closest NBFC equivalent of bad loans — ticked up to 4.65% from 4.57% a year earlier, while the net stage-three ratio improved to 2.31% from 2.49%, as the provision coverage ratio rose to 51.48% from 46.70%. In plain terms: more loans are entering distress, but the company is covering the potential losses far more aggressively than before, so the net exposure to those loans has actually fallen. Capital is abundant — the capital adequacy ratio jumped to 33.24% from 20.68%, supported by MUFG Bank's infusion — and return on assets improved to 3.95% from 2.89%, even as return on equity dipped to 13.19% from 15.10%, the arithmetic consequence of a much larger equity base.

"Our long-term guidance has consistently been around 8.5%, but it will take some time to return to that level. For the next two quarters, we expect NIM to remain around 9%," managing director and CEO Parag Sharma told ET, attributing the profit surge to volume growth and the substantial benefit of lower interest costs. That guidance is worth dwelling on: the company is effectively telling the market that today's supercharged margins are temporary, and it will not chase them by taking on riskier loans as funding costs normalise. The board, meanwhile, declared an interim dividend of Rs 6.50 per share, with October 29 as the record date.

The quarter lands at an interesting moment for the RBI's tightening narrative — the same central bank that has been warning about inflation and hiking rates has, by its own liquidity operations, helped engineer the lower funding costs this quarter monetised. Shriram Finance's print is, in that sense, both a company story and a macro one: the NBFC complex's earnings power is now visibly a function of the cost of money, and this quarter the money was gloriously cheap. The question the next two quarters must answer is how much of that 9.52% margin survives the turn everyone now expects.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DROVE SHRIRAM FINANCE'S 59% PROFIT JUMP?
Net interest income rose 38% to Rs 8,661 crore as interest income grew 15.6% while interest expenses fell 9.9%; NIM expanded to 9.52% from 8.19%, and operating profit rose 53.3%.
IS THE MARGIN SUSTAINABLE?
Management says no: long-term NIM guidance is around 8.5%, and it expects roughly 9% for the next two quarters, implying gradual normalisation from the current 9.52%.
HOW WAS ASSET QUALITY?
Mixed: gross stage-three rose to 4.65% from 4.57%, but net stage-three improved to 2.31% as provision coverage rose to 51.48%; loan loss provisions jumped 41% to Rs 1,877 crore. GLOSSARY: NET INTEREST MARGIN (NIM) — The difference between what a lender earns on loans and pays on its borrowings, as a percentage of assets; 9.52% versus 8.19% means each rupee lent earned substantially more. STAGE-THREE ASSETS — Loans where repayment is significantly overdue or default has occurred; the gross ratio counts them before provisioning, the net ratio after. PROVISION COVERAGE RATIO — The share of stressed loans a lender has already set aside money against; a rise to 51.48% means half the risk is pre-funded. COST-TO-INCOME RATIO — Operating costs as a percentage of income; a fall from 27.76% to 22.52% signals improving operating efficiency.
