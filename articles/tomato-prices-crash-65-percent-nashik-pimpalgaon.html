Tomato Prices Crash 65% in a Week at Nashik's Pimpalgaon Market

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Wholesale prices at Pimpalgaon APMC, one of India's largest tomato markets, fell from Rs 321 to Rs 111 per 20-kg crate in eight days — a 65% crash.
  • •- Retail prices in Nashik halved to Rs 20 per kg from Rs 40 a week earlier, with traders expecting further correction.
  • •- Daily arrivals of 3 lakh to 3.75 lakh crates flooded the market after heat accelerated ripening.
  • •- Poor-quality tomatoes sold for as little as Rs 1 per kg while premium produce fetched Rs 16.55 per kg.
  • •- Demand was hit by the Navratri lull, weak exports and self-sufficiency in consuming states UP, Bihar and MP.
  • •- Growers already battling drought conditions and rising cultivation costs face a double blow.

At the Pimpalgaon Agricultural Produce Market Committee yard in Nashik — one of India's largest wholesale tomato markets — the arithmetic turned brutal in eight days. The average wholesale rate for a 20-kilogram crate collapsed from Rs 321 on October 12 to Rs 111 on Monday, a decline of roughly 65 per cent that translates to Rs 5.55 per kilogram for the farmer. At that level, a grower is recovering barely a third of what the same crate fetched little over a week earlier, and for many the crate price now sits below the cost of picking, grading and hauling it to the yard. Quality spread the pain unevenly: crates sold anywhere between Rs 20 and Rs 331 depending on condition, with poor-quality tomatoes going for as little as Re 1 per kilogram while premium produce managed Rs 16.55. In Nashik's retail markets, the kitchen staple was selling at Rs 20 per kilogram on Monday, down from about Rs 40 a week earlier — a 50 per cent drop that one retailer said still has further to run as wholesale declines feed through with the usual lag of a day or two.

The cause is a textbook supply avalanche. According to APMC officials, daily arrivals at Pimpalgaon surged to between 3 lakh and 3.75 lakh crates over the past week, a flood that badly outstripped demand and dragged prices to what the trade calls rock-bottom. A market that clears perhaps a lakh and a half crates on a normal October day was absorbing two to two-and-a-half times that volume; perishable supply meeting fixed demand can only clear at lower and lower prices until the marginal crate goes unsold. The trigger was weather: unusually high temperatures accelerated the ripening of standing crop across the Nashik belt, compressing what would normally be several weeks of staggered arrivals into a few days of simultaneous supply. Tomatoes do not wait for better prices; once ripe, they move. The result is the recurring Indian agricultural nightmare — a bumper arrival in the same week that demand goes missing.

Demand, in fact, offered no cushion at all. The Navratra period slowed wholesale activity as several markets remained shut, removing a chunk of institutional buying at precisely the wrong moment. Export demand, a usual release valve for gluts, was weak — and with freight costs still elevated, even the traditionally price-sensitive Gulf demand has thinned for perishables that must arrive fresh. And three of the country's largest consuming states — Uttar Pradesh, Bihar and Madhya Pradesh — are currently meeting their requirements from local supplies, cutting their dependence on Nashik fruit that typically travels a thousand kilometres or more to reach their mandis. Traders said prices could fall further if arrivals do not slow.

The timing makes the blow heavier than the price chart shows. Nashik growers entered this season already coping with drought conditions and rising cultivation costs — input, labour and transport expenses that do not fall when the mandi rate does. That asymmetry is the double blow in the story: costs that are sticky upward, prices that are violently sticky downward. A farmer who harvested at Rs 16 per kilogram's worth of expectation eight days ago is now being quoted Rs 5.55 for the same crate, against input bills priced for the higher number. Where the grower has outstanding crop loans, the price collapse can push repayment conversations with lenders into uncomfortable territory within weeks, not months. The Indian farmer's recurring tragedy is not poverty of output but poverty of timing — the crop is fine; the calendar is not. And unlike cereals covered by minimum support prices, horticulture has no such floor: the mandi auction is the only price there is.

The Pimpalgaon episode is also a reminder of how little has changed structurally. APMC yards still clear perishables through spot auctions with no hedging layer, no warehouse receipt mechanism that works for soft horticulture, and no serious processing capacity to absorb gluts — India processes only a fraction of its tomato output, so excess supply cannot be diverted into paste and pulp at scale. Cold-chain coverage remains thin precisely where production concentrates, meaning the farmer's only storage option is the plant itself, which is already perishing. Government price-stabilisation funds exist for exactly these weeks and routinely arrive after the damage. Retailers will benefit for a few weeks, and urban consumers celebrating cheaper tomatoes into Diwali will barely register the farmgate side of the trade — a familiar asymmetry in Indian food inflation, where the farmer's crash and the consumer's discount are separated by middlemen who rarely pass the full benefit either way. Until the chain between field and mandi gains storage, processing and futures-market depth, the Rs 1-per-kilogram tomato will keep returning to Nashik like a monsoon that arrives only to flood.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
HOW MUCH DID TOMATO PRICES FALL?
Wholesale rates at Pimpalgaon APMC dropped 65% in eight days, from Rs 321 to Rs 111 per 20-kg crate; Nashik retail prices halved to Rs 20 per kg.
WHY DID PRICES CRASH?
Heat accelerated ripening, pushing daily arrivals to 3-3.75 lakh crates, while Navratri market closures, weak exports and local self-sufficiency in UP, Bihar and MP crushed demand.
WHAT ARE FARMERS FACING?
A double blow of drought conditions and rising cultivation costs on top of the price collapse, with poor-quality fruit selling at Re 1 per kg.
WILL RETAIL PRICES FALL FURTHER?
Traders expect further retail correction in the coming days as wholesale declines feed through with a lag. GLOSSARY: - APMC: Agricultural Produce Market Committee, the regulated wholesale market where farmers sell produce. - Arrivals: The quantity of produce reaching a wholesale market for sale on a given day. - Mandi: A wholesale market for agricultural goods in India. - Glut: An excess of supply over demand that drives prices sharply lower.
