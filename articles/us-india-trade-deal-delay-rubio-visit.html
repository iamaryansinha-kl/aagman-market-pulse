What's Holding Up the US-India Trade Deal as Rubio Heads to India

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Rubio begins a six-day trip with stops in Uzbekistan, Mumbai and Jamnagar — bypassing Delhi.
  • •- The February framework cut reciprocal tariffs from 25% to 18%; the deal still has not been signed.
  • •- US court challenges struck down the reciprocal tariff regime, weakening the value of Washington's offer.
  • •- Agriculture, US energy purchases and tariff parity with Vietnam remain the sticking points.
  • •- Finance Minister Sitharaman said talks have hit a "plateau"; USTR Greer said an announcement is not imminent.
  • •- India's goods exports to the US jumped 21% to $8.3 billion in August despite the impasse.

The itinerary tells you what the communique will not. US Secretary of State Marco Rubio lands in India this week on the second visit of the year, and he is skipping New Delhi entirely. His stops are Mumbai, the financial capital, and Jamnagar — home to the world's largest refining complex and, not incidentally, the most visible symbol of the energy trade at the centre of the two countries' disagreements. The trip, part of a six-day swing that begins in Uzbekistan, will focus on investment and energy. It will also carry, everywhere Rubio goes, the unresolved question of the trade deal both governments have been promising for the better part of a year.

The February framework is where the story starts. Under it, Washington would lower reciprocal tariffs on India from 25 per cent to 18 per cent, and India would reduce duties across a range of industrial and agricultural goods. Months of negotiation have not produced a signature, and one reason is a legal earthquake under the American side of the bargain: US court challenges struck down the reciprocal tariff regime that formed the framework's basis. "The February framework had offered to reduce India's reciprocal tariff in return for substantial Indian concessions," trade expert Ajay Srivastava noted. "Once that tariff regime was struck down, the value of the US offer became unclear." India, in effect, negotiated against a tariff that no longer legally exists — which is either a diplomatic windfall or a foundation of sand, depending on which ministry you ask.

The sticking points on India's side are the ones that have been sticking all year. Agriculture remains the third rail: New Delhi is unwilling to expose its farmers to greater American competition, and every draft that brushes against dairy or farm access has to be priced against domestic politics. Energy flows the other way — Washington wants India to buy more US energy, a demand that sits awkwardly beside the sanctions architecture that has governed India's Russian crude purchases and the waiver Trump signed on Friday. And India has held a firm line on relative economics: its final tariff rate must be competitive with the rates received by regional manufacturing rivals, Vietnam above all. Trade minister Piyush Goyal said this week that the first tranche is in its final contours and legal drafting, but would not be operationalised until India secured an advantage over its peer countries — the demand stated plainly, as a condition rather than an aspiration.

The public tone from both capitals has cooled in step with the calendar. In June, US ambassador Sergio Gor said negotiators were working on the "last one percent" of the pact. Last week, India's finance minister Nirmala Sitharaman described the talks as having hit a "plateau," with little room left for concessions, and US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer said an announcement was not "imminent." Three months turned the last one percent into a plateau, which is what happens when the easy clauses are all signed and every remaining one is either a farm constituency or a tariff benchmark.

And yet the underlying trade is behaving as if the deal were already signed. India's goods exports to the United States jumped 21 per cent in August to $8.3 billion against the same month a year earlier — when India faced 50 per cent tariffs — and the US remains the destination absorbing India's surplus at the exact moment West Asia's war is taxing every other part of the trade balance. That surge is the strongest argument in the deal's favour: both economies have demonstrated, under duress, that the commercial relationship expands even without the paperwork. It is also the strongest argument against urgency. If exports rise at 50 per cent tariffs and 18 per cent tariffs alike, the political cost of delay is lower for both governments than the political cost of the concessions required to finish — which is precisely what a plateau looks like from the inside.

Rubio's visit threads the needle both sides now need. Investment and energy are deliverables that require no farm votes and no tariff schedules; Jamnagar lets the energy conversation happen at the facility that embodies it, away from Delhi's television cameras. The trade deal itself will not be signed in Mumbai. But the fact that America's chief diplomat is arriving with the file marked urgent — while India's exports to his country post their best month of the year — suggests both capitals understand the plateau is affordable for months, not years. Deals signed under that kind of mutual patience tend to hold. Deals signed under artificial deadlines tend to be reopened. The current pace, however maddening to watch, is probably the one that produces the durable version.

There is one number that disciplines both sides' patience. The August export surge — 21 per cent to $8.3 billion — is running against a 50 per cent tariff memory, which means every month of impasse that does not damage trade is a month that proves the relationship's commercial floor. Washington can afford to wait for the farm clauses it wants; New Delhi can afford to wait for the parity it has demanded; and the exporters capturing the American demand in the meantime are the quiet lobby for precisely the status quo both capitals publicly deplore. That is the peculiar geometry of the current plateau: each side's best evidence for holding is the other's evidence that waiting costs nothing. The deal will get signed when one of those equations breaks — an election, a court ruling, a commodity shock — and Rubio's luggage this week suggests both sides are still calculating which break arrives first.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHY HAS THE US-INDIA TRADE DEAL BEEN DELAYED?
Court challenges struck down the US reciprocal tariff regime underpinning the February framework, and disputes remain over farm access, US energy purchases and tariff parity with Vietnam.
WHERE IS RUBIO TRAVELLING IN INDIA?
He visits Mumbai and Jamnagar, bypassing New Delhi, focusing on investment and energy during the trip.
WHAT DID INDIAN OFFICIALS SAY ABOUT THE TALKS?
Piyush Goyal said the first tranche is in final legal drafting but will only be operationalised once India gets a competitive advantage over peer countries; Nirmala Sitharaman said talks have hit a plateau.
HOW IS TRADE HOLDING UP WITHOUT A DEAL?
India's goods exports to the US rose 21% to $8.3 billion in August, even without a signed agreement. GLOSSARY: - Reciprocal tariff: An import duty set to mirror the treatment a country applies to American goods. - Tariff parity: Matching the duty rates granted to competitor countries so exporters are not disadvantaged. - Legal drafting: Converting agreed negotiating terms into binding treaty language. - Trade plateau: A negotiation state where remaining disputes are politically difficult and no new concessions are available.
