Indian Bank Books Rs 1,388 Crore Gain From NSE Stake Sale in Q2

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The bank booked a Rs 1,388 crore one-time gain from the NSE IPO's offer-for-sale window.
  • •- Its NSE holding fell to 0.28% from 0.34%.
  • •- Non-interest income surged 50% quarter-on-quarter to Rs 3,953 crore.
  • •- It also took a Rs 123 crore mark-to-market loss on held-for-trading instruments.
  • •- Rival PSU banks saw treasury income fall as bond yields rose.

The National Stock Exchange's long-awaited public listing is starting to show up in bank earnings statements — and nowhere more dramatically than at Indian Bank. The Chennai-headquartered state-owned lender reported a one-time gain of Rs 1,388 crore from selling part of its NSE stake through the offer-for-sale window during the exchange's initial public offering, a bonanza that single-handedly powered a 50 per cent quarter-on-quarter surge in the bank's non-interest income to Rs 3,953 crore in the September quarter.

The arithmetic of the sale is a tidy illustration of how much latent value sat on PSU bank balance sheets. Indian Bank held 0.34 per cent of the NSE before the IPO; after paring through the offer-for-sale, its residual holding stands at 0.28 per cent. That small trim — six basis points of one of the world's most profitable exchanges — was enough to generate a gain larger than many mid-sized lenders' entire quarterly net profit. "The NSE listing also helped mark-to-market gains," managing director Binod Kumar told ET, suggesting the listed price re-rated the value of the stake the bank continues to hold.

The gain arrived at a useful moment. Indian Bank simultaneously reported a Rs 123 crore mark-to-market loss on instruments kept in its held-for-trading portfolio, as the rise in bond yields this quarter took its bite out of treasury books across the banking system. The NSE bonanza more than absorbed that loss, turning what could have been a soft quarter for other income into a strong one.

The contrast with fellow state-owned lenders is the story within the story. Canara Bank's other income fell 14 per cent quarter-on-quarter to Rs 5,772 crore, with treasury earnings down 53 per cent at Rs 499 crore. Uco Bank's other income dropped 47.4 per cent to Rs 888 crore. The divergence has nothing to do with operational quality and everything to do with a single line item: which banks happened to own a piece of the NSE, and how much of it they chose to sell.

That dynamic will echo through the next several quarters of PSU bank results. A clutch of state-owned lenders held small NSE stakes accumulated in the exchange's early years, when the banking system underwrote the institution's rise. The listing has converted those holdings into both realised gains, for those who sold into the IPO, and a revalued asset, for those who did not. Indian Bank's numbers this quarter are the template: a one-time kicker to non-interest income, a flattering quarter-on-quarter comparison, and a residual stake now marked closer to its true market worth.

For investors reading Q2 results across the PSU banking space, the analytical task is to separate the NSE effect from the underlying business. A 50 per cent jump in non-interest income says little about loan growth, margins or asset quality; it says a great deal about which banks held lottery tickets that have now paid out. The cleaner comparison this quarter is the fee, forex and core treasury performance beneath the one-time items — and there, the higher bond yields that generated Canara's 53 per cent treasury decline are the same force that clipped Indian Bank's held-for-trading book. Excluding the NSE gain, the operating environment for treasury income was difficult across the sector.

MD Binod Kumar's emphasis on the mark-to-market benefit is worth pausing on, because it hints at the second-order effect: banks that retained their residual NSE stakes now carry them at values dramatically higher than the historical cost on their books, which flatters capital ratios and provides future optionality — more sales, or collateral, or simply a richer balance sheet. Indian Bank kept 0.28 per cent, worth multiples of what it once was. The exchange that the public sector helped build in the 1990s is now, quarter by quarter, paying the public sector back.

There is a broader valuation point buried in the numbers. Public sector banks have traded for years at price-to-book multiples well below their private peers, a discount that reflected asset-quality history as much as anything else. But their balance sheets carry equity stakes — in exchanges, in clearing corporations, in insurance and asset-management joint ventures — accumulated over decades at negligible historical cost. Each listing that re-rates one of these holdings converts dormant book value into visible capital, and Indian Bank's quarter is the cleanest demonstration yet of how that mechanical rerating works: sell a sliver, book a gain, mark the rest higher, and watch the capital ratio improve without a single new equity issuance.

None of which changes the underlying earnings question. When the one-time NSE gains wash out of PSU bank results over the coming quarters, what remains is the operating business — loan growth, deposit franchises, margins in a rate environment that has not been kind to treasury books, and credit costs that remain the sector's swing factor. The market will eventually value the NSE windfalls correctly, as windfalls. What it pays a multiple for is the recurring engine, and on that metric the NSE bonanza buys Indian Bank time and capital, but not a premium.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHERE DID INDIAN BANK'S RS 1,388 CRORE GAIN COME FROM?
From selling part of its NSE stake through the offer-for-sale window during the exchange's IPO; its holding fell to 0.28% from 0.34%.
HOW DID THE GAIN AFFECT Q2 INCOME?
Non-interest income rose 50% QoQ to Rs 3,953 crore, even as the bank took a Rs 123 crore mark-to-market loss on held-for-trading instruments.
HOW DID OTHER PSU BANKS FARE?
Canara Bank's treasury earnings fell 53% to Rs 499 crore and Uco Bank's other income dropped 47.4%, as higher bond yields hurt treasury books without an NSE offset.
DOES THE GAIN REFLECT BETTER OPERATIONS?
No — it is a one-time item; underlying loan growth, margins and asset quality tell the real story. GLOSSARY: - One-time gain: A profit from a non-recurring event, such as an asset sale, that inflates a single quarter's earnings. - Offer for sale (OFS): A mechanism for existing shareholders to sell stakes to the public during an IPO. - Mark-to-market: Accounting for securities at current prices, so gains and losses flow through earnings. - Held-for-trading portfolio: Securities bought to sell short-term, valued at market prices with changes hitting profit.
