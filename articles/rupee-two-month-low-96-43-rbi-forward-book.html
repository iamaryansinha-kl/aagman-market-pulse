Rupee Hits 2-Month Low Of 96.43 As RBI Weighs Forward Book

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/The rupee fell 0.15% to 96.43 per dollar on Tuesday, its weakest level since July, before paring losses as traders reckoned the RBI sold dollars
  • 02/Persistent foreign selling of local assets is pressuring the currency; the record low is 96.96 hit in May
  • 03/Spot sits near July lows while the 1-year outright forward at 99.70 prices far more weakness than analyst polls that see 97.50 in 12 months

The rupee fell 0.15% to 96.43 per dollar on Tuesday, October 6. That is its weakest level since July.

The currency pared some losses through the session. Traders reckoned the Reserve Bank of India stepped in to sell dollars, as it has on a near-daily basis.

Persistent foreign selling of local assets is doing the damage. Portfolio outflows have kept steady pressure on the rupee for weeks.

The RBI's almost daily dollar sales have anchored the currency. Without them, traders say, the slide would likely be steeper.

Tuesday's low leaves the rupee about half a percent above its record low of 96.96. That record was hit in May.

The more interesting story is in the forward market. The 1-year outright forward was quoted around 99.70 on Tuesday.

That is a wide gap. Forward markets are pricing far more weakness than the analyst community expects.

A Reuters poll of economists and analysts points to only a modest decline. On average, they see the rupee at 97.50 over the next 12 months.

None of the polled analysts expect the rupee to pass 100 per dollar in the next year. BofA, UOB and Barclays forecast 99 by end-September 2027.

The forwards disagree. A 1-year outright at 99.70 implies the market is hedging for a much weaker rupee than any poll participant predicts.

Why the divergence matters: it shapes how the RBI manages its intervention. And that intervention is constrained by a swollen forward book.

A DBS note, cited by traders, says the RBI will prefer to time its dollar sales. The goal is to rationalise the use of the reserves buffer.

The bank also flagged a second concern. Untimed sales risk widening the ballooned short FX forward book.

Here is the mechanics in plain terms. India's foreign exchange reserves were boosted recently by one-off policy measures.

Those measures drew over $140 billion in capital inflows. The RBI absorbed much of this through swaps rather than plain spot purchases.

In a swap, the RBI takes dollars today but owes dollars back at a future date. That obligation shows up as a forward liability on its balance sheet.

The result: the net forward book has expanded to about $200 billion by end-August. That is the swollen position the DBS note refers to.

Every dollar the RBI sells now changes the mix of its reserves and its forward obligations. Timing sales lets it smooth the currency without letting the forward book balloon further.

This is why traders watch the RBI's daily behaviour as much as the spot rate. The central bank's reaction function, not just foreign flows, is setting the floor under the rupee.

There is also a policy event on the calendar. The RBI's rate decision is due on Wednesday, October 7.

A 25-basis-point hike is widely anticipated, according to the report's context. The rupee's path will still depend more on flows and intervention than on any single rate call.

For now, the spot market is near July lows and the forward market is near 100. The gap between the two is the signal investors are trading.

GLOSSARY: Spot rate: The current exchange rate at which the rupee trades against the dollar for immediate delivery — the headline number, at 96.43 on Tuesday. Forward book: The central bank's accumulated future dollar obligations from swap transactions; India's net forward book stood at about $200 billion by end-August. Outright forward: A contract that locks in an exchange rate for a future date; the 1-year outright at 99.70 signals where the market prices the rupee a year out. FX reserves: Foreign currency assets held by the RBI, built up partly by one-off policy measures that drew over $140 billion in capital inflows. Intervention: Central bank action in the currency market — here, near-daily dollar sales by the RBI to cushion the rupee's fall. Portfolio outflows: Foreign investors selling local assets and repatriating funds, which creates sustained demand for dollars and pressure on the rupee.
