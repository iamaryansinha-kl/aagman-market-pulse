India Trade Deficit Widens to $30.21 Billion in September on Oil

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The merchandise trade deficit widened to $30.21 billion in September from $26.86 billion in August.
  • •- Imports rose to $74.61 billion from $70.67 billion; exports grew 23% YoY to $44.40 billion.
  • •- The gap was driven by energy costs linked to the US-Iran conflict and Strait of Hormuz disruptions.
  • •- Exports were broad-based: petroleum products, electronics, engineering goods, meat, dairy and handicrafts.
  • •- Gold imports fell to $1.8 billion; services exports were estimated at $36.35 billion.
  • •- The US remained India's largest export market, with April-September shipments up to $50.81 billion.

India's merchandise trade deficit widened to $30.21 billion in September, from $26.86 billion in August, as imports raced ahead of exports on the back of elevated energy costs — a number that lands just above economists' expectations and squarely inside the oil shock that has dominated the country's macro narrative this month.

The mechanics are visible in the two headline figures. Imports climbed to $74.61 billion from $70.67 billion in August, while exports rose only marginally on a month-on-month basis to $44.40 billion from $43.81 billion — though the year-on-year comparison is far kinder, with exports up 23.04 per cent. The gap between the two, at $30.21 billion, was broadly in line with the $30.4 billion economists had pencilled in, according to a Reuters poll; the surprise, if any, is how quickly the energy bill has scaled.

The villain is familiar. The escalating conflict involving the United States and Iran has kept Brent crude above $100 a barrel, and attacks around the Strait of Hormuz have intensified over the past ten days — with Tehran saying it is escalating strikes in response to the US blockade of its ports and tougher sanctions imposed last month. As the world's third-largest oil importer, India has absorbed the shock through every channel available: a wider trade gap, a rupee that breached 96 last week before the central bank's countermeasures, and an RBI that raised interest rates earlier this month to contain the inflationary pass-through before unveiling its dollar-window and derivatives curbs to steady the currency.

The export side of the ledger, however, is the story's quiet bright spot. Growth was broad-based across petroleum products, meat, dairy and poultry, electronic goods, handicrafts and engineering goods — the kind of cross-sector strength that suggests competitiveness rather than a one-off commodity windfall. Trade Secretary Rajesh Agrawal read the numbers as showing "India's strength across sectors," and the pipeline of market access is deepening: the trade agreement with New Zealand takes effect next week, the pact with Britain is already operational, and the broader agreement with the European Union is moving toward implementation.

Automobile exports added a separate layer of confirmation. Shipments rose 22.5 per cent year on year to 684,481 vehicles in September, according to SIAM, led by two-wheelers — up 26.8 per cent to 544,797 units — even as passenger-vehicle shipments slipped 2.2 per cent. Services trade continued to provide a partial offset to the goods deficit, with services exports estimated at $36.35 billion against imports of $19.44 billion.

Gold, meanwhile, retreated as a pressure point. Imports fell to $1.8 billion from $2.3 billion in August, when jewellers had front-loaded inventory ahead of the festive season; over the first half of FY27, gold imports are down 27.32 per cent year on year at $19.27 billion — a notable decline given the metal's traditional role in India's import bill, and one that gives policymakers some breathing room even as the oil bill climbs. The US remained India's largest export destination, with merchandise shipments rising to $50.81 billion in April-September from $45.82 billion a year earlier.

For the rupee and the rate path, the arithmetic is now clear. A $30-billion monthly goods gap requires roughly $1.4 billion of financing every working day — comfortably coverable by services surpluses, remittances and portfolio flows in normal times, but a strain when foreign investors are selling. The RBI's measured response this month has bought stability; Thursday's data explains why it felt compelled to act at all. The deficit number also frames the week's trade diplomacy — the first phase of the US pact finalised and drafting underway — as macroeconomically urgent, not merely political.

The composition beneath the headline offers both comfort and warning. Comfort, because the export surge is volume-and-variety driven rather than a single commodity spike, and because the services surplus plus remittances still cover most of the goods gap in the current account; warning, because the deficit with China separately crossed $100 billion in the first nine months of the year, and because every month Brent spends above $100 adds to an import bill that already dwarfs last year's. Analysts will also note that gold's 27 per cent H1 decline is doing quiet work on the other side of the ledger — households substituting out of bullion is an adjustment channel India has used in every oil shock of the past half-century, and it is functioning again.

The September print also extends a pattern that has taken hold since the conflict escalated: monthly merchandise gaps running in the high twenties of billions of dollars, each one financed by a mix of services earnings, remittances and capital flows that works only as long as global risk appetite holds. New Zealand's entry next week, the UK's operational pact and the EU agreement in progress are, in that sense, not diplomatic trophies but structural hedges — each one widening the set of buyers for the exports that must now do the heavy lifting of paying for expensive oil.

WHAT WAS INDIA'S TRADE DEFICIT IN SEPTEMBER?
The merchandise trade deficit widened to $30.21 billion from $26.86 billion in August, broadly in line with economists' expectations of $30.4 billion.
WHAT DROVE THE WIDENING?
Imports rose to $74.61 billion on elevated energy costs linked to the US-Iran conflict and Hormuz disruptions, while exports reached $44.40 billion, up 23% YoY.
WHICH EXPORTS GREW FASTEST?
Growth was broad-based — petroleum products, electronics, engineering goods, meat, dairy and handicrafts; auto exports rose 22.5% to 684,481 vehicles.
HOW DID SERVICES AND GOLD TRADE DO?
Services exports were estimated at $36.35 billion against $19.44 billion of imports; gold imports fell to $1.8 billion from $2.3 billion in August. GLOSSARY: - Merchandise trade deficit: The gap between the value of goods a country exports and the goods it imports. - Strait of Hormuz: The chokepoint through which a fifth of global oil supply transits; disruptions raise freight and insurance costs. - SIAM: Society of Indian Automobile Manufacturers, the industry body that compiles vehicle sales and export data. - Services exports: Earnings from services — software, consulting, tourism, remittance-linked flows — which offset part of the goods gap.
