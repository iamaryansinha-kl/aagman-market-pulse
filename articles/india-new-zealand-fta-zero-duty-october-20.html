India-New Zealand FTA Takes Effect With Full Zero-Duty Access

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The FTA comes into force at 12.01 am on October 20, the first time India has secured full duty-free access across all tariff lines from day one of an FTA.
  • •- New Zealand will provide 100% duty-free market access to Indian exports; earlier duties of up to 10% are eliminated.
  • •- Bilateral trade is targeted to grow from $2.4 billion to about NZ$7 billion (nearly Rs 35,000 crore) by 2030, with $20 billion of New Zealand FDI envisioned over 15 years.
  • •- India will give tariff concessions on 70% of tariff lines (~95% of current trade value), keeping dairy, most farm goods, copper, aluminium and gems on the exclusion list.
  • •- New Zealand opens 118 services sectors and a 5,000-visa skilled-worker pathway; customs clearance of 48 hours for cargo is committed.
  • •- Bilateral trade actually slipped 11% to $1.15 billion in FY26, leaving a small $22.6 million deficit for India.

At one minute past midnight on October 20, India will wake up to a trade milestone it has never had before: a partner opening every single tariff line, duty-free, from the very first day. The India-New Zealand free trade agreement, announced after years of negotiation, comes into force on Dussehra with what Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal called a simple but sweeping promise — all Indian goods, from leather and footwear to apparel, textiles, marine products and engineering goods, enter the island country with zero duty. For Indian exporters who previously paid duties of up to 10 per cent, the disadvantage disappears overnight.

The headline numbers carry ambition. Goyal said the pact targets lifting bilateral trade in goods and services from roughly $2.4 billion to about 7 billion New Zealand dollars — nearly Rs 35,000 crore — by 2030, and framed an even larger flow behind it: $20 billion of foreign direct investment from New Zealand into India over the next fifteen years. He made the announcement at a flag-off ceremony for a small excavator built by JCB India, headed for New Zealand as the agreement took effect; the company currently exports 25 per cent of its total production and was publicly given a target of 50 per cent, with New Zealand becoming its 140th export destination. It was a deliberately unglamorous symbol for an agreement whose selling point is breadth rather than spectacle — a construction machine from a British-owned, India-built supply chain, shipping into a market of five million people as proof that the pact is meant to be used by ordinary exporters, not just celebrated.

On the market-access ledger, India conceded less than the optics suggest. New Zealand gets tariff concessions on 70 per cent of India's tariff lines, which sounds generous until you see the carve-outs: dairy — milk, cream, whey, yoghurt and cheese — is fully excluded, as are onions, chana, peas, corn and almonds, sugar, edible fats and oils, arms and ammunition, and gems, jewellery and precious metals. Copper and aluminium articles are protected too, as are animal products other than sheep meat. New Zealand's famous apples, kiwifruit and Manuka honey get in only through quotas with minimum import prices and safeguards. The design protects the farm lobbies that have killed Indian trade deals before — a lesson drawn directly from the abandoned RCEP negotiations and the bruising politics of the UK and US talks. About 29.97 per cent of tariff lines sit on the exclusion list, yet because of what is on it, the politics hold. Every recent Indian trade negotiation has taught the same lesson: an FTA that spooks the dairy and onion lobbies dies in Delhi long before it reaches a foreign capital, and this text has clearly internalised that constraint.

The services chapter may end up mattering more than the goods chapter for India's economy. New Zealand has committed to open 118 services sectors and sub-sectors to Indian providers — information technology, professional services, telecommunications, construction, education, financial services and tourism among them. Indian professionals get real mobility: intra-company transferees can stay up to three years, contractual service providers and independent professionals up to twelve months, and a dedicated temporary employment pathway carries a quota of 5,000 visas at any time, covering IT, engineering, construction, education, healthcare — and, in a distinctly Indian touch, yoga instructors, chefs, music teachers and Ayush practitioners. Business visitors get 90 days in any twelve months. For a services superpower whose trade agreements have historically over-indexed on goods, the asymmetry runs in India's favour this time, and the visa pathway gives the chapter teeth that services commitments often lack.

The quiet risk in the story is the baseline. Bilateral trade actually declined about 11 per cent in 2025-26 to $1.15 billion, from $1.3 billion the year before; India's exports were $566.51 million against imports of $589.1 million, a $22.59 million deficit. New Zealand Trade and Investment Minister Todd McClay's framing — that 95 per cent of New Zealand's current exports will see tariffs eliminated or substantially cut once fully implemented — reminds you whose exporters get the bigger immediate windfall. The agreement's own plumbing tries to fix the friction that holds small trade back: customs clearance within 48 hours for cargo and 24 hours for express and perishable consignments, digital certification and self-certification of rules of origin for eligible status holders. Commerce Secretary Rajesh Agrawal's post-announcement line — that the job now is to translate the opportunity into actual benefits — is the honest summary of where the deal stands. The architecture is done, the tariffs are gone, and the target is nearly three times the current trade value inside four years. Nobody has ever accused India's trade ministry of aiming low, and in a season dominated by a stalled US negotiation and tariff anxiety, a fully operational FTA with a developed economy is also diplomatically valuable proof that India's trade diplomacy still works when the counterpart is willing.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHEN DOES THE INDIA-NEW ZEALAND FTA TAKE EFFECT?
At 12.01 am on October 20, 2026, giving all Indian goods immediate zero-duty access to New Zealand across every tariff line.
WHAT IS THE TRADE TARGET?
Bilateral trade is targeted to rise from about $2.4 billion to NZ$7 billion (nearly Rs 35,000 crore) by 2030, with $20 billion of New Zealand FDI into India over 15 years.
WHAT DID INDIA EXCLUDE?
Dairy, most sensitive farm products, sugar, edible oils, arms, gems and jewellery, and copper and aluminium articles; sheep meat gets zero duty, while apples and kiwifruit enter via quotas with minimum import prices.
WHAT ABOUT SERVICES AND VISAS?
New Zealand opens 118 services sectors to Indian providers, and a 5,000-visa quota allows skilled Indian workers stays of up to three years. GLOSSARY: - Free trade agreement (FTA): A pact between countries to reduce or eliminate tariffs and other barriers on trade. - Tariff lines: Individual product categories in a customs schedule, each with its own duty rate. - Rules of origin: Criteria used to determine where a product was made, needed to claim FTA benefits. - Minimum import price (MIP): A floor price below which imports cannot enter, used as a safeguard.
