US 10Y Yield Hits 5.34% 24-Year High: Nifty, Rupee Slide

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/The US 10-year Treasury yield rose as high as 5.34% on Thursday, October 1, 2026, its highest level since early 2002 and above its 2007 peak.
  • 02/The benchmark US yield posted its biggest quarterly rise of this century in Q3 2026, intensifying a global bond sell-off that hit Indian stocks, bonds and the rupee.
  • 03/The Sensex recovered from a 1,200-point fall to close 571 points lower, while traders expect the RBI to sound less relaxed on rates at its upcoming policy decision.

The US 10-year Treasury yield climbed to its highest level since early 2002 on Thursday, October 1, 2026. The yield rose as high as 5.34% during the session, crossing its 2007 peak and deepening a global bond sell-off.

The move capped a brutal quarter for US bonds. According to Reuters, the 10-year Treasury posted its biggest quarterly rise of this century in the third quarter of 2026.

The sell-off has been driven by concerns over inflation, higher crude prices and the possibility that US interest rates may stay elevated for longer than markets had hoped.

The fallout landed hard on Indian markets. The BSE Sensex fell as much as 1,200 points during intraday trade, wiping out investor wealth worth nearly Rs 10 lakh crore at the lows.

Indian equities recovered some ground by the close. The Sensex settled 571 points lower, while the Nifty ended the session at 22,422.

The US dollar index (DXY) rose to a three-month high, supported by higher US Treasury yields. A stronger greenback typically spells trouble for emerging markets.

The rupee weakened to 95.98 per dollar during the session. State-run banks were likely selling dollars to limit losses near the 96 level, traders said.

The logic of the rout is straightforward. Higher US yields make dollar assets relatively more appealing than emerging-market equities and debt, pulling capital away from markets such as India, Indonesia, Brazil and South Africa.

A stronger dollar also squeezes emerging economies through other channels. It pressures EM currencies, raises imported inflation and makes foreign-currency debt more expensive to service.

India carries an additional vulnerability. Elevated crude prices can widen the country's current account deficit and add further pressure on the rupee.

Indian government bonds felt the heat as well. Traders expect the benchmark 6.94% 2036 bond yield to trade in the 7.18%-7.26% range in the near term.

That bond closed at 7.1879% on Wednesday, a two-and-a-half-year high. The yield rose 24 basis points in September and about 44 basis points over the quarter.

The Reserve Bank of India now faces a harder script. An RBI policy decision is due soon, and higher global yields make it more difficult for the central bank to sound relaxed on interest rates.

Markets are already adjusting. Investors are pricing in further tightening over the next year as global yield pressures constrain the RBI's room to manoeuvre.

There is also a valuation channel at work. Rising US yields reduce the premium investors are willing to pay for equities, because future earnings are discounted at a higher rate.

That is why growth stocks, financials and other rate-sensitive sectors typically face the first round of selling whenever Treasury yields spike.

For Indian investors, the signal from US bonds matters more than the day's index level. Until US yields stabilise, equities, bonds and the rupee are likely to remain under pressure.
