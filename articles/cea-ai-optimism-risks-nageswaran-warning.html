Who Pays if AI Optimism Is Wrong? CEA Nageswaran Has an Answer

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The CEA said finance ministries, central banks and households will pay if AI optimism proves wrong — and part of the bill even if it proves right.
  • •- Risk one: entry-level jobs are vanishing, breaking the career ladder's first rung.
  • •- Risk two: an MIT study found "cognitive debt" — weaker brain connectivity among heavy AI users.
  • •- Risk three is AI safety; the fourth is financing — a US data-centre buildout needing over $10 trillion by 2032.
  • •- The fifth risk concerns public finances, where governments are absorbing AI's costs before its revenues arrive.

One of India's most senior economic officials has just asked the question the technology industry has been avoiding. Chief Economic Adviser Anantha Nageswaran this week laid out a structured warning about artificial intelligence, and his framing was deliberately uncomfortable: "Finance ministries, central banks, and households will pay the bill if today's optimism on AI proves wrong. They may pay part of it even if it proves right." It is a formulation worth pausing on — not a prediction of failure, but an observation about who underwrites the experiment either way.

The first risk Nageswaran catalogued is the one closest to India's economy: the hollowing of entry-level work. Firms are hiring fewer juniors, he noted, citing a study across 41 countries where the junior share of the workforce fell in 23 of the 31 economies that could be measured. In the United States, employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed jobs has fallen about 19 per cent behind older workers in the same roles. The CEA's point is generational rather than cyclical: the people who will check AI's work tomorrow are trained on the first rung today, and if that rung disappears, the damage compounds silently for a decade. For a country whose services-led growth model depends on precisely those entry-level rungs — the TCS fresher batch, the BPO intake, the support desk — the stakes, as he put it, "are much higher" for developing economies.

The second risk concerns human capability itself. Nageswaran cited a four-month MIT Media Lab study in which brain activity and connectivity weakened among participants who relied on an AI model to write essays; in the first session, 83 per cent of ChatGPT users could not quote a single sentence from an essay they had submitted minutes earlier. The researchers call it "cognitive debt" — the outsourcing of thinking that feels like productivity and bills itself as augmentation. For an education-obsessed economy, the prospect that the most-used study tool of the current generation is also measurably degrading the skill it is meant to assist lands with particular force.

The third risk is safety, stated without elaboration. The fourth is financing, and here the CEA's numbers deserve wide circulation. The US data-centre buildout may need over $10 trillion by 2032 — roughly 3.6 per cent of American GDP per year — and this year, for the first time, the large cloud firms will spend more on capital expenditure than they generate in cash. The gap is increasingly filled by debt. That is the sentence beneath the AI trade's entire architecture: the buildout is no longer self-funding, and the transition from cash-flow-funded optimism to debt-funded optimism is precisely when finance ministries and central banks become the sector's uninvited partners. When the CEA says they may pay "part of it even if it proves right," this is what he means — the debt has to be serviced regardless of whether the intelligence arrives as advertised.

The fifth risk concerns public finances, and it completes the circle: governments are being asked to fund the infrastructure, absorb the employment transition, and underwrite the energy demand of AI before any of the technology's promised fiscal dividends materialise. Add the week's market action — US equities ending sharply lower on AI jitters even as Indian IT posted its best week since 2008 — and the CEA's framework reads less like scepticism and more like a map. The optimism may be justified; the bill is still itemised.

For Indian policymakers, the speech's practical content is a warning against importing the buildout's financing assumptions. India is being courted as an AI-infrastructure destination — data centres, chips, energy — and the temptation to replicate the American leverage model with public balance-sheet support is real. Nageswaran's argument implies the opposite posture: let the private cost of capital price the risk, keep the sovereign out of the venture, and focus policy on protecting the entry-level employment ladder that the technology is demonstrably eroding elsewhere. The country that supplies the world's first rungs should be the last to sacrifice its own.

The timing amplifies the message. This was the week Nifty IT posted its biggest rally since 2008 on the conviction that Indian services are AI's winners, while Wall Street sold off on the suspicion that American capital expenditure is AI's bubble — both cannot be wholly right, and the CEA's framework explains how both can be partly wrong. If the buildout's debt-financed trillion-dollar infrastructure delivers returns, India gains as the services layer; if it does not, the losses concentrate in the balance sheets Nageswaran named — treasuries, central banks, households — while the employment damage arrives on his first rung. Either way, the speech argues, the bill has already been partially issued.

Historically, such warnings from the CEA's office carry weight because they arrive before the consensus moves. The last time an occupant of that chair challenged a dominant narrative was during the post-pandemic stimulus debates, when caution about inflation proved correct long before markets priced it. Nageswaran is not forecasting an AI crash; he is itemising the liabilities of the AI boom with the patience of someone who expects to be asked about them later. In a week when the technology's market narrative swung violently between euphoria and doubt, a calm inventory of who pays was arguably the most radical document to come out of the Indian government.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID THE CEA WARN ABOUT AI?
Anantha Nageswaran said finance ministries, central banks and households will pay the bill if AI optimism proves wrong — and part of it even if it proves right — citing five risks.
WHAT ARE THE FIVE RISKS?
Threats to entry-level jobs, degradation of human capability ("cognitive debt"), safety, the debt-heavy financing of the buildout, and public finances.
WHAT DID THE MIT STUDY FIND?
Brain activity and connectivity weakened among heavy AI users; 83% of ChatGPT users could not quote a sentence from essays they had submitted minutes earlier.
HOW BIG IS THE AI FINANCING GAP?
The US data-centre buildout may need over $10 trillion by 2032 — about 3.6% of GDP a year — and big cloud firms now spend more on capex than they generate in cash. GLOSSARY: - Cognitive debt: Measurable weakening of brain activity and recall from over-reliance on AI assistance, per MIT Media Lab research. - Capital expenditure: Money spent on long-lived assets like data centres, as opposed to operating costs. - Entry-level exposure: The degree to which junior, first-rung jobs are automatable — highest precisely where careers begin. - Public finances: Government budgets — revenue, spending and debt — which the CEA says will absorb AI's costs before its returns.
