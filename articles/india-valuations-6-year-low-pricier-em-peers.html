India Valuations at 6-Year Low but Still Pricier Than EM Peers

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • Key points unavailable

Mumbai: India's equity market is cheaper than it has been in six years. Whether that makes it a bargain is a separate question — and the answer depends on who you ask.

The dollar-denominated MSCI India Index trades at around 18.5 times its 12-month forward price-to-earnings ratio, the lowest level since May 2020.

That is a long way down. The measure hit a record high of 24.54 times in September 2024, when the Sensex and Nifty were at their all-time peaks, and stood at 22.5 times at the start of 2026.

The compression has been brutal. In dollar terms, the Sensex and Nifty are down 18-19% so far in 2026, while MSCI India has fallen 15.4%. Only MSCI China, down 20%, has done worse among major markets; most others are up for the year. The decline has been driven by a familiar cluster of pressures: sustained foreign portfolio outflows, a rupee hovering near record lows, crude above $115 a barrel and a US 10-year Treasury yield back around 5%.

Yet foreign investors are not rushing back. The reason is relative, not absolute: India's valuation is still roughly double that of its emerging market peers. For a global allocator measuring every market against every other, a discount alone is not a reason to buy — the question is whether India's growth premium justifies its price premium, and that debate has sharpened as North Asian markets deliver superior returns this year.

The MSCI Emerging Markets Index trades at 8.7 times forward earnings, down from 13.3 times at the beginning of the year and below its 10-year average of 12.05 times. India, even after its correction, trades at a premium of more than 100% to that benchmark.

The comparison with developed markets is similarly unflattering to the cheapness argument. MSCI US trades at around 18.8 times forward earnings, roughly in line with India despite US markets gaining 12% this year. Put differently: an investor moving money out of India this year has not been fleeing expensive assets — they have been swapping a falling market for rising ones at a similar multiple.

Taiwan and Japan trade at 16.8 times and 15.4 times forward earnings respectively — both below India — and Taiwan has gained 57% in dollar terms this year, powered by semiconductor demand from the global AI boom.

China trades at just 9.2 times forward earnings, versus its 10-year average of 11.5 times.

Some fund managers say the headline multiple flatters Indian valuations. "The Nifty forward PE looks cheap only if you believe the forward EPS," said Vivek Iyer, partner and CIO at Rational Equity Asset Management. "With crude where it is, we think consensus estimates still have cuts ahead, so the true multiple is higher than the headline." The distinction matters: a forward P/E is only as good as the earnings forecasts beneath it, and those forecasts were built before oil climbed above $115 and the rupee weakened past 94 to the dollar.

There is also the question of where the de-rating has actually happened. Iyer describes the recent decline as a large-cap de-rating: mid- and small-cap stocks are still trading at a 50-70% premium to their historical medians.

"The broader market has not reset at all. The reset is incomplete," he said. The pain has been concentrated in crude-sensitive and rate-sensitive large caps — oil marketing companies, paints, autos, aviation, cement, consumer discretionary and lenders — which is also where foreign selling has been concentrated, because that is where $100-plus crude poses the greatest earnings risk.

India's premium to emerging markets has historically been defended on the strength and stability of corporate earnings growth. That argument still has defenders, particularly those who note that India's earnings volatility has been lower than most peers across multiple cycles. "We would avoid prescribing a fixed India-EM premium as fair value," said Saurabh Patwa, head of Equity and Portfolio Manager at Quest Investment Managers. "A meaningful premium is justified where businesses deliver superior earnings growth with durability."

But in the near term, the competition for global capital has intensified. AI and data-centre investments are creating stronger growth opportunities in markets such as South Korea and Taiwan, pulling the marginal emerging market dollar toward North Asian semiconductors.

The path back for Indian equities likely runs through crude oil. A sustained decline in oil prices would ease the inflation and current account concerns driving foreign selling, stabilise the rupee and remove the main reason earnings estimates are being cut. It would also relieve the rate-sensitive sectors — lenders, autos, cement, consumer discretionary — where both the de-rating and the earnings risk have been concentrated, and where any recovery in foreign flows would likely begin.

Until then, the market offers an unusual configuration: six-year-low headline valuations, an incomplete reset beneath the surface, and a premium to peers that still assumes earnings durability the macro environment has yet to confirm.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
HOW CHEAP IS THE INDIAN MARKET NOW?
MSCI India trades at about 18.5 times 12-month forward earnings, the lowest since May 2020 and below its 10-year average of 19.7 times. It is down from a record 24.54 times in September 2024 and 22.5 times at the start of 2026.
WHY AREN'T FOREIGN INVESTORS BUYING THE DIP?
India still trades at roughly twice the MSCI Emerging Markets multiple of 8.7 times forward earnings. Fund managers also argue forward earnings estimates have further to fall given $115 crude, so the true multiple is higher than the headline. Foreign selling has been concentrated in crude- and rate-sensitive large caps.
HOW DOES INDIA COMPARE WITH OTHER MARKETS?
MSCI US trades at 18.8 times forward earnings (up 12% this year), Taiwan at 16.8 times (up 57% on AI semiconductors), Japan at 15.4 times (up 17%), and China at 9.2 times (down 20%). Only China has fallen more than India in dollar terms this year.
WHAT WOULD MAKE INDIAN STOCKS GENUINELY CHEAP?
A sustained fall in crude oil prices would ease inflation, current account and rupee pressures, and halt the earnings downgrades. Until then, the headline multiple understates the effective valuation because consensus estimates have cuts ahead. GLOSSARY: FORWARD P/E: The price-to-earnings ratio using forecast earnings for the next 12 months, rather than trailing profits. It reflects what investors are paying for expected future earnings. MSCI INDIA / MSCI EM: Dollar-denominated indices compiled by Morgan Stanley Capital International that foreign investors use to compare markets. MSCI EM is the benchmark for emerging market equities. DE-RATING: A fall in the valuation multiple investors are willing to pay for a stock or market, independent of earnings changes. A de-rating from 24.5x to 18.5x means prices fell even where earnings held up. PEG RATIO: Price-to-earnings divided by expected earnings growth rate, used to judge whether a multiple is justified by growth. A high PEG signals an expensive market even where the P/E looks moderate. GLOBAL CAPABILITY CENTRE (GCC): An overseas unit set up by a multinational to handle technology, data and operations work — a structural source of IT services demand, and competition for traditional outsourcing.
