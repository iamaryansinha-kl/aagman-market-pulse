PM GatiShakti at Five: Goyal Maps Multimodal Next Phase

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- PM GatiShakti National Master Plan completed five years on October 13, 2026, the anniversary of its October 13, 2021 launch.
  • •- Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said the platform now covers 58 central ministries and departments and all 36 states and Union Territories.
  • •- The GIS-based system holds more than 23,493 data layers as of October 12, 2026, and offers 230 approved public datasets.
  • •- Seven infrastructure engines are integrated: roads, railways, ports, waterways, airports, mass transport and logistics.
  • •- The next phase deepens multimodal connectivity, last-mile links and data-driven planning across districts and social sectors.

The PM GatiShakti National Master Plan turned five on October 13, 2026, and the government used the milestone to lay out where the flagship infrastructure-planning platform goes next. Union Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal said the initiative will continue to support multimodal connectivity, faster infrastructure development and greater logistics efficiency as it enters its sixth year.

In a social media post marking the anniversary, Goyal said the platform had transformed how India plans infrastructure. "The PMGatiShakti National Master Plan turns 5 today," he wrote, crediting Prime Minister Narendra Modi for envisioning the programme. His core claim: the initiative has shifted infrastructure development in India from a fragmented approach to a coordinated, technology-driven, whole-of-government framework.

That coordination claim is measurable. According to government data, the GatiShakti platform now spans 58 central ministries and departments, and all 36 states and Union Territories feed into the same planning system. The platform was designed to bring infrastructure planning across ministries, states and Union Territories onto a common Geographic Information System, or GIS, based backbone, so that projects are planned on shared data rather than in departmental silos.

The scale of the underlying data has grown sharply. As of October 12, 2026, the platform contained more than 23,493 data layers. Its public platform provides access to 230 approved datasets that support site suitability analysis, connectivity mapping, infrastructure alignment planning and compliance checks. For project developers, that means a power plant, a highway stretch or an industrial corridor can be sited against rail links, water sources, land use patterns and environmental constraints on a single screen.

Seven infrastructure engines sit inside the framework: roads, railways, ports, waterways, airports, mass transport and logistics infrastructure. The platform also folds existing national programmes into the same planning map, including Bharatmala for highways, Sagarmala for ports, inland waterways development and the UDAN regional air connectivity scheme.

Goyal outlined six principles that guide the platform: integrated development, multimodal infrastructure, last-mile connectivity, expedited land acquisition, minimised clearances and reduced ecological impact. Those principles map directly onto the two chronic complaints about Indian infrastructure projects — land acquisition delays and inter-ministerial clearance overlaps — which the platform was built to compress.

The minister said the initiative has expanded beyond national-level infrastructure planning to cover states, districts and social sectors. The next phase, he indicated, will lean on the same architecture to strengthen inter-ministerial coordination and data-driven decision-making, enabling faster project execution, better logistics efficiency and more inclusive regional development.

The five-year milestone lands at a busy moment for Indian infrastructure. The government has been pushing integrated planning to improve how goods move between transport modes, a core input for the broader goal of lowering logistics costs relative to GDP. Dedicated freight corridors, port-led development under Sagarmala, inland waterway cargo and regional airports all feed into the same multimodal logic that GatiShakti is meant to coordinate.

The platform's practical value lies in catching conflicts before money is spent. A highway alignment that runs through a protected zone, a rail siding that misses an industrial park by a few kilometres, or a port expansion without last-mile rail connectivity are exactly the failures that shared GIS layers are designed to surface at the drawing-board stage. Officials have repeatedly described the platform as a decision-support tool rather than a construction programme: it does not build projects itself, but it changes how projects are chosen, sequenced and cleared.

Industry has generally received the framework positively, since a common map reduces the information asymmetry between project promoters and approving agencies. The public availability of 230 approved datasets extends some of that benefit to smaller developers and state-level planners who lack in-house geographic data teams.

As GatiShakti enters its sixth year, the emphasis on districts and social sectors suggests the platform's use cases will keep widening beyond transport infrastructure. Rural connectivity projects, social-sector facilities and regional development programmes can in principle be planned on the same layers that currently guide highways and ports.

For businesses and investors, the practical significance of the platform has grown with its dataset library. Site-selection decisions that once required separate consultations with multiple agencies can now be screened against public datasets before capital is committed. State governments use the same maps to align their own infrastructure priorities with central schemes, reducing the risk of duplicated or stranded assets.

Goyal's framing of the next phase stayed consistent with the original design: a single, shared map on which the centre, states and private developers plan in the same coordinate system. Whether the platform's coordination gains translate into visibly faster project completion remains the test for the second half of the decade, and the government's own anniversary messaging suggests it expects the payoff to compound quietly, project by project. For now, the government is counting five years of institutionalised planning and signalling that multimodal connectivity, last-mile links and data-driven execution stay at the top of the infrastructure agenda.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT IS PM GATISHAKTI?
PM GatiShakti is the National Master Plan for infrastructure planning, launched on October 13, 2021. It integrates infrastructure planning across central ministries, states and Union Territories on a common GIS-based platform.
HOW MANY MINISTRIES AND STATES USE THE PLATFORM?
The platform covers 58 central ministries and departments and all 36 states and Union Territories, according to government data cited at the five-year mark.
WHAT ARE THE SEVEN INFRASTRUCTURE ENGINES UNDER GATISHAKTI?
The seven engines are roads, railways, ports, waterways, airports, mass transport and logistics infrastructure, with schemes such as Bharatmala, Sagarmala, inland waterways and UDAN integrated into the same planning map.
WHAT DATA DOES THE PLATFORM HOLD?
As of October 12, 2026, the platform contained more than 23,493 data layers, with 230 approved datasets available publicly for site suitability analysis, connectivity mapping, alignment planning and compliance checks. GLOSSARY: - PM GatiShakti: India's National Master Plan integrating infrastructure project planning across ministries and states on a shared GIS platform. - GIS (Geographic Information System): Digital mapping technology that layers geographic and infrastructure data for planning decisions. - Bharatmala: The national highways and road development programme folded into the GatiShakti framework. - Sagarmala: The port-led development programme covering port modernisation and connectivity, integrated into GatiShakti. - UDAN: The regional air connectivity scheme linking smaller cities into the national transport network. - Multimodal connectivity: Integration of two or more transport modes, such as rail, road, ports and waterways, into a single logistics chain.
