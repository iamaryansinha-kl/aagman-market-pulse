Brent Crude Hits $110 as Strait of Hormuz Attacks Widen

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Brent touched $110 and settled at $109.86, up 3.7%; WTI rose 3.5% to $101.41.
  • •- WTI gained more than 10% for the week; Brent rose nearly 5%.
  • •- Iran-backed attacks hit at least nine tankers in the Strait of Hormuz since Sunday.
  • •- Iran sank the container ship GFS Pride on Wednesday; the crew was evacuated.
  • •- Houthi missiles targeted Saudi Arabia, including the Yanbu oil export terminal.
  • •- Goldman Sachs sees $120 if shipping disruption escalates; JPMorgan warns no exit strategy is visible.

Oil just broke the level that makes every oil-importing finance ministry flinch. Brent crude futures touched $110 a barrel on Friday — crossing that threshold for the first time since May — before settling at $109.86, up 3.7 per cent on the day. US West Texas Intermediate gained 3.5 per cent to $101.41. For the week, WTI rose more than 10 per cent and Brent nearly 5 per cent, and the drivers were uniformly hostile: a widening campaign against shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, missiles raining on Saudi Arabia, and the collapse of the energy ceasefire President Donald Trump had tried to broker between Russia and Ukraine.

The most strategically significant development is in the Gulf. At least nine tankers and cargo ships have come under attack since Sunday as Iran seeks to disrupt oil exports through the Strait of Hormuz — the chokepoint through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil passes. US Central Command said Iran sank the Singapore-registered container ship GFS Pride on Wednesday; the crew was safely evacuated. The International Maritime Organization reported three seafarers injured and two missing across this week's attacks. Insurance rates for Gulf transit are repricing in real time, and shippers are already rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope at significant cost and delay.

Saudi Arabia absorbed the week's other front. The kingdom intercepted several missiles over the Red Sea on Friday, including one headed toward Yanbu — a key oil export terminal on the Red Sea coast — and destroyed three drones over Riyadh and its southern regions. An Aramco administrative building in Dhahran was hit on Tuesday, and the Houthis' October 10 attack on Riyadh's international airport killed 12 people and injured more than 300. Attacks of this frequency on Saudi and Emirati energy infrastructure were the nightmare scenario of 2019; they are now a weekly baseline.

The Russia-Ukraine dimension removed the supply-side fallback. The energy ceasefire Trump sought to broker quickly fell apart — Moscow continued strikes on Ukrainian infrastructure, and President Volodymyr Zelenskyy pledged renewed attacks on Russian refineries in retaliation. Ukrainian strikes on Russian refining capacity have been a persistent force behind the surge in refined-product prices: US diesel averaged $6.33 a gallon on Friday, more than 70 per cent higher than a year earlier, a figure with direct consequences for American inflation and, therefore, for how long the Federal Reserve can stay on hold.

For India, $110 Brent lands on an economy that has just spent six weeks defending the rupee. The arithmetic is unforgiving: India imports roughly 88 per cent of its crude, and every sustained $10 increase in the oil price adds meaningfully to the import bill, widens the trade deficit, and pressures the current account — the same external balance the RBI has been spending nearly $60 billion of reserves to protect. The narrowing August current-account deficit reported this week was calculated at oil prices materially below today's; next month's print will not be so lucky. The rupee's hard-won recovery below 95 will face its first real stress test, and the central bank's rate-hike-and-reserves defence strategy just got more expensive on both fronts.

Analyst framing splits between scenarios rather than levels, which tells its own story about uncertainty. Goldman Sachs' commodities team, led by Daan Struyven, outlined a path to $120 a barrel if attacks on vessels escalate, against a reversion toward $80 if export flows normalise — a $40 range that renders most budget and inflation models decorative. JPMorgan's assessment was grimmer in tone: seven months into the conflict, many of the economic thresholds the bank had expected Washington to protect "had been crossed, while a clear exit strategy remained elusive." Shipping risk, Struyven noted, has become a major driver of oil prices, with refined products and natural gas even more exposed than crude itself.

The market's other signal worth reading is what did not rally. US equities ended the week sharply lower amid AI jitters, and the dollar softened on fading Fed-hike bets — the classic safe-haven pattern failed to fire, suggesting investors are treating the oil spike as a supply shock that tightens financial conditions rather than a geopolitical risk that merely rotates capital. Supply shocks are the stagflationary kind: they raise prices while lowering real income, which is precisely why central banks from Mumbai to Washington will be watching next week's tanker traffic through Hormuz more closely than any inflation print.

The weekend's geopolitical calendar offers no obvious de-escalation path. Iran's campaign has been escalating rather than tapering — from harassment to ship-sinking in five days — and the Houthi front has reactivated against Saudi targets simultaneously, a combination that suggests coordination rather than coincidence. Ukraine's refinery-strike pledge adds a second supply threat anchored in a different conflict entirely, meaning the oil market now prices two independent war premia that would each be disruptive alone. Goldman's $120 scenario requires only that the current tempo of attacks persist; it does not require escalation. The $80 scenario, by contrast, requires all three fronts to quiet at once — a taller order by the week.

For now, the honest summary is that the oil market has moved from pricing risk to pricing damage. Ships have been sunk, terminals have been hit, and diesel is 70 per cent more expensive in the world's largest economy than it was a year ago. Whatever diplomatic off-ramps exist, none are currently being taken, and the actors with the most leverage — Tehran, Riyadh, Moscow, Washington — are all behaving as if higher prices are either tolerable or useful. That is not a backdrop in which $110 holds for long in either direction; the only question is which scenario fires first, and the weekend's shipping traffic will be the tell.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHY DID OIL PRICES SURGE?
Iran attacked at least nine ships in the Strait of Hormuz, Houthi missiles hit Saudi Arabia including the Yanbu export terminal, and the Russia-Ukraine energy ceasefire collapsed with Ukraine pledging refinery strikes.
WHAT ARE THE KEY PRICE LEVELS?
Brent settled at $109.86 after touching $110; WTI closed at $101.41, up more than 10% on the week.
WHAT DOES GOLDMAN SACHS FORECAST?
$120 a barrel if shipping disruptions escalate; a slide toward $80 if export flows normalise, with refined products and gas more exposed than crude.
WHAT DOES THIS MEAN FOR INDIA?
A bigger oil import bill, pressure on the trade and current-account deficits, and a tougher test for the rupee and the RBI's reserve-heavy defence strategy. GLOSSARY: - Strait of Hormuz: The narrow waterway between Iran and Oman through which about a fifth of global oil exports transit. - WTI: West Texas Intermediate, the US benchmark crude, typically cheaper than Brent. - Supply shock: A sudden restriction in supply that raises prices while reducing real economic output — the difficult kind of inflation for central banks. - Yanbu: Saudi Arabia's key Red Sea oil export terminal.
