India's Forex Reserves Fall $8.58 Billion to $726 Billion

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Reserves fell $8.578 billion to $726.027 billion in the week ended October 9.
  • •- The previous week had seen a steeper $12.952 billion drop to $734.606 billion.
  • •- Reserves peaked at a record $785.706 billion in the week ended September 4.
  • •- Foreign currency assets fell $7.14 billion; gold fell $1.32 billion.
  • •- The drawdown follows the RBI's intervention to support the rupee and the fading of a record NRI deposit inflow.

India's foreign-exchange reserves are being spent on the rupee's defence, and the weekly numbers now show it plainly. The Reserve Bank of India reported on Friday that reserves fell by $8.578 billion to $726.027 billion in the week ended October 9 — the second consecutive weekly decline, following a $12.952 billion drop to $734.606 billion in the prior week. From the all-time high of $785.706 billion touched in the week ended September 4, the kitty has now shrunk by nearly $60 billion in just over a month.

The composition of the fall is as instructive as its size. Foreign currency assets, the largest component, declined by $7.142 billion to $597.605 billion — a drop that reflects both dollar sales by the RBI and valuation changes as the euro, pound and yen moved against the greenback. Gold reserves fell $1.319 billion to $105.094 billion, largely a price effect rather than a transaction. India's special drawing rights with the IMF dipped $109 million to $18.548 billion, and the reserve position with the IMF declined $10 million to $4.78 billion.

To understand why the drawdown is happening, rewind to how the peak was built. The September surge to $785.7 billion was driven by massive inflows through a special concessional swap facility for deposits from the Indian diaspora, through which banks raised a record sum of nearly $133 billion — the same programme that produced the $83 billion-plus of non-resident deposits behind this month's record capital-account surplus. In effect, the RBI borrowed dollars from the diaspora at the top of the market; now it is spending a portion of the proceeds to keep the rupee orderly while oil prices are elevated and global yields are high.

That framing matters, because a $60 billion slide from a record peak can be read two ways. The alarmist reading is that India is burning reserves to defend an unsustainable level of the rupee. The calmer reading — supported by this week's current-account data showing the deficit narrowing to $5.2 billion in August — is that the central bank is smoothing volatility from a position of unusual strength, deliberately spending down an artificially inflated stock toward a more normal one. The rupee posted its best week in seven months even as reserves fell; that combination is what successful intervention looks like from the outside.

The arithmetic is still worth watching. At the current pace of roughly $10 billion a week, the September inflow buffer would be exhausted in about six weeks, though in practice the pace should slow as the rupee stabilises and the oil-company dollar facility reduces spot-market demand. The RBI has other tools it deployed earlier this month — tighter currency-derivative rules to squeeze speculative positioning, and the FM's just-announced push for rupee trade settlement, which would reduce dollar demand at the margin structurally rather than tactically.

For markets, the reserves number is now the single clearest gauge of how much currency stress remains beneath the surface calm. A falling reserve line alongside a recovering rupee says the central bank is choosing to pay for stability; a falling reserve line alongside a weakening rupee would say it is losing control. Friday's data shows the former. The $726 billion that remains is still the world's fourth-largest hoard, comfortably above any plausible stress threshold — but the direction of travel is now the story, and the next few weekly prints will show whether the RBI's war of attrition against the dollar shortage is tapering or intensifying.

There is a precedent for reading too much into these numbers in either direction. In 2022, when reserves fell from $642 billion toward $530 billion during the Fed's aggressive tightening, the same two readings competed — and the calm reading ultimately prevailed, with the rupee stabilising once the global cycle turned. Today's starting position is stronger by almost $200 billion, the banking system is flush with the NRI deposit proceeds, and the current-account deficit, at $5.2 billion in August, is narrower than it was then. The vulnerability is different in kind rather than degree: a prolonged period of crude above $100 would test the oil-company swap facility's capacity, and a disorderly move in US yields would pressure the valuation component of the stock further.

What the data does rule out is the fantasy that September's reserve pile was a permanent addition to India's defences. It was borrowed — literally, through the swap window — and it is now being repaid in the currency of market stability. The honest accounting is that India entered this storm with roughly $726 billion of usable ammunition plus a domestic bond market deep enough to absorb the financing of any remaining gap. That remains one of the strongest external positions among major economies; it is simply no longer the impregnable one the September headlines briefly, and somewhat misleadingly, suggested.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
HOW MUCH DID RESERVES FALL?
$8.578 billion to $726.027 billion in the week ended October 9, after a $12.952 billion fall the previous week.
WHY ARE RESERVES DECLINING?
RBI dollar sales to support the rupee amid elevated oil prices and high global yields, plus valuation effects; the decline follows an artificial peak built on record NRI deposit inflows.
HOW FAR HAVE RESERVES FALLEN FROM THE PEAK?
Nearly $60 billion from the record $785.706 billion hit in early September.
SHOULD INVESTORS WORRY?
The fall alongside a strengthening rupee suggests managed smoothing, not distress; $726 billion remains the world's fourth-largest reserve stock, but the weekly pace bears monitoring. GLOSSARY: - Forex reserves: Foreign assets held by the central bank — dollars, other currencies and gold — used to back the currency and pay external obligations. - Foreign currency assets (FCAs): The currency-denominated portion of reserves, sensitive to both transactions and exchange-rate moves. - Special drawing rights (SDRs): An international reserve asset created by the IMF that supplements member countries' official reserves. - Special concessional swap: An RBI facility that lets banks swap foreign-currency NRI deposits for rupees, boosting reserves at the margin.
