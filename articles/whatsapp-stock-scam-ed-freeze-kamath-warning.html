WhatsApp Stock Scam: ED Freezes Rs 200 Crore, Kamath Sounds Alarm

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The Enforcement Directorate froze trading accounts holding shares worth about Rs 200 crore in an alleged stock manipulation scheme.
  • •- Searches covered 29 premises across six cities including Mumbai, Nashik, Ahmedabad, Gandhinagar, Surat and Bhavnagar under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act.
  • •- WhatsApp groups such as 'Multibagger Stocks' pushed daily buy recommendations between late December 2025 and February 2026.
  • •- The scam ran through preferential allotments by two listed companies between April 2023 and March 2024, with allottees dumping shares into the induced buying.
  • •- Zerodha CEO Nithin Kamath warned that such schemes could become more frequent as the market cycle advances.
  • •- Kamath suggested investors sanity-check tips using AI tools before acting on them.

The Enforcement Directorate's Mumbai zone moved last week against what may be the most 2026 of financial crimes: a pump-and-dump scheme run through WhatsApp, with a side hustle in X posts and SMS. The agency conducted searches at 29 premises across six cities — Mumbai, Nashik, Ahmedabad, Gandhinagar, Surat and Bhavnagar — under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act, and froze trading accounts holding shares worth approximately Rs 200 crore. The anatomy of the scam, as laid out by the ED, is a case study in how modern manipulation actually works: not boiler rooms, but group chats.

The scheme had two phases. Between April 2023 and March 2024, two listed companies issued preferential allotments of shares — and the ED says a substantial portion of the money behind those allotments came from a small, connected circle of individuals, firms and lenders rather than genuine outside investors. That is the pump's fuel: quietly acquired, heavily concentrated stock at prices nobody else could get. The second phase was the promotion. WhatsApp groups operating under names such as 'Multibagger Stocks' circulated daily buy recommendations to retail investors between late December 2025 and February this year, run from Bhavnagar using SIM cards obtained in the names of other people in Haryana and Mumbai, with promotional traffic also flowing through X and SMS. The ED's own summary is blunt: the stocks were promoted to generate buying interest, prices and volumes rose during the promotion window, and the preferential allottees "took advantage of this increased buying interest and dumped a part of their shareholding to a large number of investors."

The retail-facing warning came Tuesday from the most credible messenger available. Nithin Kamath, founder and CEO of Zerodha — the brokerage whose own customer base skews precisely toward the first-time investors these schemes target — posted on X that such schemes remain a live concern in 2026 and called them one of the silliest ways for investors to lose money, with the warning that they could become more frequent as the market cycle advances, pointing to a rise in pump-and-dump operations run through WhatsApp groups and influencers promoting both stocks and IPOs. His framing was quotable precisely because it is old advice in new packaging: "It's become a cliché, but nowhere is 'if it's too good to be true, it probably is' truer than in the stock market." Kamath's practical suggestion was of its moment — before acting on any stock tip, he said, run it past an AI tool such as Claude or ChatGPT to test whether the story makes sense. A free sanity check, he argued, can save an investor a significant amount of money. That a brokerage CEO is prescribing AI as the retail investor's first line of defence says as much about the scale of the problem as any enforcement action.

The episode lands at an awkward intersection of trends, each feeding the other. Retail participation in Indian equities has exploded over this market cycle, and with it the economics of scamming: a single coordinated WhatsApp campaign can reach lakhs of first-time investors who have never seen a full bear market and whose only framework for a 30 per cent monthly return is that someone, somewhere, must be earning it. Demat account additions running at record pace mean the pool of potential marks refreshes faster than any awareness campaign can cover. Influencer-driven promotion of IPOs adds a second channel that sits in a regulatory grey zone — not quite investment advice, not quite advertising, but functioning as both. The ED's Bhavnagar-to-Haryana SIM trail is a reminder that the operators understand exactly how thin the enforcement perimeter is, and the six-city search footprint suggests this case will widen before it narrows.

For the market's plumbing, the Rs 200 crore freeze is less a headline number than a signal. Preferential allotments have long been the preferred vehicle for exactly this manipulation pattern — cheap stock acquired privately, promoted publicly, dumped gradually — and the ED's decision to trace the allotment funding to a connected lending circle indicates investigators are now working backwards from the share register rather than waiting for price charts to scream. That methodical backwards-trace is what separates this action from the periodic token crackdowns of past cycles: if the funding network behind the allotments gets mapped, the next operator will inherit a meaningfully higher cost of doing business, and the allottee-side gatekeepers on company boards will face questions they have historically avoided. Kamath's warning that these schemes multiply late in cycles has historical support: the 2018 and 2021 peaks both produced waves of tip-group busts after the damage was done. The retail investor's defence remains almost embarrassingly simple — no legitimate multibagger arrives by forwarded message — but as long as bull markets mint new participants faster than regulators mint new warnings, the 'Multibagger Stocks' groups will keep finding an audience.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID THE ED FREEZE?
Trading accounts holding shares worth about Rs 200 crore, in an investigation of an alleged WhatsApp-driven stock manipulation scam spanning 29 premises across six cities.
HOW DID THE SCAM WORK?
Two listed companies issued preferential allotments funded by a connected circle; WhatsApp groups such as 'Multibagger Stocks' then promoted the stocks so allottees could dump shares onto retail buyers.
WHAT DID NITHIN KAMATH SAY?
He warned investors to treat easy-money tips as scams, said such schemes may grow as the market cycle advances, and suggested checking tips with AI tools before acting.
WHAT SHOULD INVESTORS WATCH FOR?
Daily buy calls in messaging groups, unsolicited IPO promotions, price spikes without fundamentals, and any tip that promises quick or guaranteed returns. GLOSSARY: - Pump-and-dump: A scheme where promoters inflate a stock's price through misleading promotion, then sell their own holdings into the rally. - Preferential allotment: Private placement of shares to select investors, typically at negotiated prices. - Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA): India's primary anti-money-laundering law, enforced by the ED. - Freezing of accounts: A legal order blocking transactions in bank or trading accounts pending investigation.
