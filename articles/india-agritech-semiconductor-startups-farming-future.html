India's Agri-Tech Future Hinges on Semiconductors: Why Chip Startups Are the Missing $150B Link

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/India imports 85-90% of its semiconductors, leaving agri-tech startups dependent on costly foreign chipsets despite agriculture contributing 16-18% to GDP.
  • 02/The India Semiconductor Mission offers a $10 billion (₹76,000 crore) incentive outlay, while the Design Linked Incentive scheme provides up to ₹30 crore per startup for chip design.
  • 03/Watch for ISM 2.0 in Budget 2026 and whether domestic semiconductor startups can close the gap before the $150 billion market target by 2030.

India's agriculture sector contributes 16-18 per cent to GDP and employs nearly 42-45 per cent of the workforce, according to the International Fund for Agricultural Development (IFAD). Despite this scale, the sector battles low productivity, climate shocks, and poor resource use.

Artificial Intelligence is already reshaping Indian farming. From pest detection to yield prediction and weather forecasting, AI enables data-driven decisions. NITI Aayog estimates AI adoption could add $1.7 trillion to India's economy by 2035 if scaled aggressively across sectors including agriculture.

Drones are another fast-emerging enabler. India's drone market is projected to hit $11 billion by 2030, capturing 12.2 per cent of the global market. The Drone Rules 2021 and rising startup adoption are powering crop monitoring, precision spraying, and farm mapping at scale.

But there is a structural vulnerability beneath this visible progress. India imports 85-90 per cent of its semiconductor requirements, as confirmed by the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology. Every AI model, drone sensor, and smart irrigation system depends on chips — and nearly all of them come from abroad.

This import dependence raises costs and limits scalability for domestic agri-tech startups building deep-tech solutions. A drone startup developing autonomous crop-spraying hardware or an AI firm building soil sensors still sources chipsets from foreign suppliers, squeezing margins and exposing supply chains to geopolitical risk.

The government launched the India Semiconductor Mission with a $10 billion (₹76,000 crore) incentive outlay to build domestic fabrication and design capacity. The Design Linked Incentive (DLI) scheme, part of the broader Semicon India Programme, offers up to ₹30 crore per startup for semiconductor design and prototyping support.

As of July 2025, MeitY had approved 23 chip design projects worth $91.6 million. Over 278 academic institutions and 72 startups gained access to Electronic Design Automation (EDA) tools and IP cores through the DLI scheme, signaling early ecosystem traction.

India's semiconductor market is projected to reach $150 billion by 2030. Investor interest is accelerating: Indian semiconductor startups raised $28 million in 2024, up from just $5 million in 2023, according to industry data. Notable deals include $8 million for Mindgrove Technologies and $6 million for FermionIC.

Preet Sandhu, AVPL International & Promoter at Startup Stairs, writes that the real missing link in agri-tech is not innovation but integration. AI provides intelligence and drones deliver data, but semiconductors power both. Without this foundational layer, the ecosystem risks remaining fragmented and import-dependent.

Sandhu's incubator has committed ₹4 crore to support startups in AI, drone, and semiconductor domains through the Startup League and incubation programmes. The focus extends beyond capital to mentorship, infrastructure, and market access — elements he calls critical for scaling deep-tech in India.

Budget 2026 introduced India Semiconductor Mission 2.0, expanding support to equipment, materials, design IP, supply chains, and R&D centres. The government also allocated ₹4,500 crore to modernise the Semiconductor Laboratory (SCL) in Mohali, assuring it will not be privatised.

India's agricultural future will be defined by the technologies that enhance it, not land alone. The shift to intelligent, data-driven farming is inevitable. But for this transformation to be self-reliant and sustainable, India must build a domestic semiconductor backbone — and startups must lead that charge.
