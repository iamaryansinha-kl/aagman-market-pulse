Fed Veterans Urge Warsh To Focus On Crisis Tools Over Balance Sheet Cuts

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/Former Fed officials urge incoming Chair Kevin Warsh to focus on crisis-readiness tools rather than cutting the $6.7 trillion balance sheet size.
  • 02/Jeremy Stein called the balance sheet an "optical political football"; shifting to shorter-term Treasuries matters more than the headline number.
  • 03/Charles Evans said returning to 2007 levels of $800 billion is unrealistic given today's banking system and liquidity needs.

Former Federal Reserve officials have advised incoming Fed Chair Kevin Warsh to prioritise establishing clear principles for deploying the central bank's balance sheet during future economic and financial shocks. The veterans argued that the debate should focus on the composition and purpose of holdings rather than their absolute size.

The Fed's balance sheet currently stands at about $6.7 trillion. This consists largely of U.S. Treasury and mortgage-backed securities accumulated during years of quantitative easing and pandemic-era stimulus measures.

Jeremy Stein, a former Fed governor, said political attention around the balance sheet had turned it into an "optical political football." He noted that Warsh may feel pressure to demonstrate progress by reducing the balance sheet, but argued that shifting holdings toward shorter-term Treasury bills would likely matter more than simply cutting the total size.

Warsh has been confirmed by the U.S. Senate for a four-year term and is expected to be sworn in later this week. He has made balance-sheet reduction a key part of his proposed overhaul of the central bank.

Although Warsh initially supported quantitative easing during the 2007-2009 financial crisis while serving as a Fed governor, he later became increasingly critical of prolonged bond-buying programs. He argued they distorted markets and contributed to inflationary pressures.

Charles Evans, former Chicago Fed president, expressed skepticism over how much the Fed could realistically reduce its balance sheet. He noted that when he joined the Chicago Fed in 2007, the balance sheet was roughly $800 billion. He said returning to those levels was unrealistic given today's banking system and liquidity requirements.

Warsh had previously suggested the balance sheet could potentially be reduced by around $2.5 trillion. Evans questioned whether such targets were achievable without major regulatory overhauls. He described proposals aimed at significantly lowering reserve needs as highly ambitious initiatives.

Randall Kroszner, another former Fed governor, said the central bank should establish clearer guidelines in advance regarding what assets it buys and for what purpose. He pointed to the Bank of England's intervention during the 2022 UK government bond market turmoil as an example of effective communication.

Kroszner noted that the Fed's pandemic-era asset purchases gradually evolved from market-support operations into broader economic stimulus measures. This created confusion about the central bank's objectives and made it harder to explain when and why interventions should end.

Former officials at the conference broadly agreed that future debates over the Fed's balance sheet should focus less on headline numbers. The priority should instead be transparency, market functioning, and the role of asset purchases during periods of financial stress.
