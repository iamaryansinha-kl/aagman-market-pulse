World Bank Raises India FY27 Growth Forecast to 7.1%

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/World Bank lifted India's FY2026-27 growth forecast to 7.1% from 6.6%, a 50-basis-point upgrade
  • 02/The revision follows April-June quarter GDP growth of 7.8%, which beat expectations
  • 03/Risks flagged include another energy shock, a strong El Niño and elevated inflation into 2027

The World Bank has raised India's growth forecast for the financial year 2026-27 to 7.1%. The previous estimate was 6.6%, meaning the upgrade is 50 basis points. The revision was published on October 6, 2026.

The lender also lifted its 2026 growth forecast for South Asia as a whole to 6.9%. That is 60 basis points above its earlier estimate.

India is expected to remain the region's key driver of growth. Even so, the forecast implies a slowdown from the 8.6% growth recorded in 2025-26.

The revision follows India's April-June quarter performance. GDP grew 7.8% in the first quarter of FY26, stronger than economists had expected.

That data point appears to have shifted the World Bank's assessment. The economy is absorbing the energy shock better than earlier models assumed.

The World Bank's Chief Asia Economist, Franziska Ohnsorge, spoke to Reuters about the upgrade. She noted that higher energy prices have so far not derailed consumption in South Asia.

Her explanation rests on three buffers. Government measures shielded consumers, remittances stayed strong, and domestic demand proved resilient.

The numbers support the divergence she describes. Inflation has risen, yet consumption has held up. Ohnsorge called the gap between the two "quite striking."

There is a caveat embedded in that observation. In emerging markets, the usual lag between an inflation shock and its growth effect is around 18 months.

In other words, the consumption story may not be settled. The full growth cost of higher energy prices could still be working through the system.

The World Bank expects inflation to remain elevated into 2027. The Reserve Bank of India, for its part, expects inflation to average 5% in the current financial year.

Three risks headline the bank's warning list. The first is another prolonged energy shock. South Asia is heavily dependent on energy imports, so a fresh spike in oil or fuel prices would hit the region hard.

The second risk is a strong El Niño. Agriculture is central to both rural demand and food inflation in India.

The last El Niño occurred in 2023. That year, the monsoon came in at 95% of its long-period average.

A weak monsoon pressures farm output and rural incomes. That combination can feed directly into food prices.

The third risk is a correction in global financial markets. Such corrections typically tighten external financing conditions for emerging economies.

Beyond the macro forecasts, the World Bank also urged South Asia to accelerate artificial intelligence adoption. The region's firms lag global peers on this front.

About 23% of Indian firms reported using AI. The comparable figure for US firms is 43%.

The upgrade fits into a broader pattern. Other institutions have recently revised India's growth outlook upward as well.

Moody's, S&P Global Ratings and the Asian Development Bank have all issued similar upgrades. The ADB's forecast stands at 7%.

Taken together, the revisions describe an upgrade cycle. Data keeps coming in stronger than forecasters expected, and forecasts keep being revised up.

The tension at the centre of the story is the lag. Consumption is holding now, but the bank's own framework says the growth cost of an inflation shock can take around 18 months to appear.

For now, the 7.1% figure is the strongest signal yet that forecasters see India's economy as more resilient than they did three months ago. Whether that holds will depend on energy prices, the monsoon, and how long inflation stays elevated.

GLOSSARY: EL NIÑO: A climate pattern marked by unusually warm ocean temperatures in the equatorial Pacific. In India it is associated with weaker monsoons, which hurt agriculture, rural demand and food inflation. LONG-PERIOD AVERAGE (LPA): The 50-year average rainfall benchmark used by the India Meteorological Department to assess whether a monsoon season is normal, below normal or above normal. BASIS POINT: One hundredth of a percentage point. A 50-basis-point upgrade to a growth forecast means the estimate rose by 0.5 percentage points. FISCAL YEAR (FY27): India's financial year, running from April to March. FY27 refers to April 2026 through March 2027. REMITTANCES: Money sent home by citizens working abroad. A key source of household income and foreign exchange for South Asian economies. ENERGY SHOCK: A sudden, sustained rise in oil, gas or fuel prices. South Asia imports most of its energy, so such shocks widen trade deficits and raise costs across the economy.
