RBI Open to More Steps to Curb FX Speculation, Malhotra Says

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra said the central bank is open to further measures to curb speculative forex trading.
  • •- Malhotra said recent restrictions should not be seen as capital controls and are aimed at genuine hedging rather than speculative bets.
  • •- About one-third of forex trades were being closed or canceled on the same day they were entered, the governor said.
  • •- The rupee outperformed Asian peers on Tuesday, rising 0.8% to 95.01 per dollar after Monday's 1% gain.
  • •- Offshore, dollar-rupee non-deliverable forwards eased 0.1% to 95.36 on Wednesday.

The Reserve Bank of India would consider further measures, if needed, to curb speculative trading in the foreign exchange market, Governor Sanjay Malhotra said in an interview late on Tuesday, signalling that the central bank's week-long defence of the rupee is far from over.

"The RBI is determined to do whatever is necessary to ensure orderly movement in the foreign exchange market," Malhotra said. He was emphatic that the recent steps should not be read as capital controls, framing them instead as an effort to make the forex market serve genuine hedging needs rather than speculative bets.

The governor offered a striking statistic to justify the crackdown. "Over the past few months, we have observed a very high proportion of trades being canceled and rebooked on the same day," he said, adding that the RBI had observed cases where about one-third of trades were closed or canceled on the day they were entered into. That pattern — rapid-fire churn rather than position-taking — is the speculative behaviour the new rules are designed to squeeze out.

The intervention is already working on the price. The rupee outperformed Asian peers on Tuesday, rising as much as 0.8 per cent to 95.01 per dollar, following Monday's 1 per cent gain. In offshore markets on Wednesday, dollar-rupee non-deliverable forwards eased as much as 0.1 per cent to 95.36, extending the currency's recovery from the record lows that triggered the central bank's response.

The extended curbs built on a weekend package of measures, including a special window for oil firms to meet their dollar demand and broader restrictions on derivative transactions, all aimed at the same target: speculative positioning that had driven the rupee toward record lows as foreign funds sold. The RBI has also been aggressively intervening in the spot market to defend the local unit, according to Bloomberg.

The currency battle sits on top of an already-tightening policy stance. Earlier this month, the central bank's Monetary Policy Committee raised its benchmark repurchase rate by 25 basis points to contain accelerating inflation — the September consumer price print came in at 5.54 per cent, a two-year high — and to support a weakening rupee amid a foreign fund selloff. Tuesday's intervention marks the first time in recent memory that rate hikes and direct market restrictions have been deployed in the same month.

Malhotra's framing matters as much as his measures. By insisting the steps are not capital controls, the governor is drawing a line that markets care deeply about: India is defending the currency's trading mechanism, not closing it. Capital controls would spook foreign investors in a way that forex market hygiene rules do not, and the distinction buys the RBI room to do more if speculation persists.

The test comes in the next fortnight. If same-day churn falls and the rupee holds below 96, the package will be judged surgical. If offshore forwards re-widen and speculative pressure rebuilds, the governor has already told markets what to expect: whatever is necessary.

The measures Malhotra is defending were layered in over ten days. They include restricting rupee non-deliverable forwards to hedging-only use, widening a 20 per cent cash-reserve requirement to banks' own forex hedges, barring the rebooking of cancelled rupee derivative positions, cutting the no-underlying threshold for such trades from $100 million to $5 million, and opening a special dollar window for public-sector oil companies to meet their import bills. Individually each rule is technical; together they close most of the avenues through which leveraged speculation on the rupee had been rebuilt after earlier rounds of intervention.

The same-day churn the governor described has a simple logic. A trader who books a dollar-rupee position in the morning and cancels it by afternoon pays almost nothing to maintain the appearance of hedging while running an unhedged speculative book overnight elsewhere. Requiring such trades to be backed by genuine underlying exposure — and blocking their rebooking once cancelled — removes the free option. The RBI's data showing one-third of trades dying the same day they were born is, in that sense, a measurement of how much of the market's activity was churn rather than commerce.

There is also a monetary-policy dimension that makes this episode unusual. The rate-setting committee raised the repo rate earlier this month even as the currency measures rolled out, meaning the RBI is tightening through both price and quantity channels at once. Historically the bank has preferred one lever at a time; running both simultaneously signals how seriously it views the combination of a 5.54 per cent inflation print, a rupee down sharply over the year, and portfolio outflows from foreign investors.

What investors will watch now is the 95 level. The rupee's rebound to 95.01 puts it roughly six per cent stronger than its recent record lows, a recovery built almost entirely on regulatory force rather than changed fundamentals. If foreign fund selling resumes — and oil at $107 a barrel keeps the dollar demand pipeline full — the RBI's willingness to act further will be tested in the market, not just in interviews. For now, the governor has drawn his line clearly: speculation is the enemy, hedging is not, and the toolbox stays open.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID THE RBI GOVERNOR SAY?
Sanjay Malhotra said the RBI is determined to do whatever is necessary for orderly forex markets and is open to further steps against speculation, while stressing the measures are not capital controls.
WHAT SPECULATIVE BEHAVIOUR DID THE RBI OBSERVE?
The RBI found a very high proportion of trades canceled and rebooked the same day, with about one-third of trades in some cases closed or canceled on the day they were entered.
HOW HAS THE RUPEE RESPONDED?
It rose 0.8% to 95.01 per dollar on Tuesday after a 1% gain on Monday, and offshore NDFs eased to 95.36 on Wednesday.
WHAT ELSE HAS THE RBI DONE?
It extended curbs on derivative transactions, opened a special dollar window for oil firms, intervened aggressively in the spot market, and raised the repo rate by 25 basis points earlier this month. GLOSSARY: - Capital controls: Government or central bank restrictions on the flow of capital in and out of a country. - Non-deliverable forward (NDF): An offshore currency derivative settled in dollars, used to bet on or hedge currencies with trading restrictions. - Repurchase rate (repo): The RBI's benchmark short-term lending rate, raised to contain inflation. - Offshore market: Currency trading conducted outside the home country's jurisdiction. - Hedging: Using derivatives to protect against currency movements, as distinct from betting on them.
