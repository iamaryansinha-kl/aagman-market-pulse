Sovereign Investors With $29 Trillion Pivot to Energy Assets, Flag Dollar Fears

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/Invesco survey of 90 sovereign wealth funds and 54 central banks shows 80% view energy security as the top resilient investment
  • 02/61% of central banks say US debt levels hurt the dollar's long-term reserve status, up sharply from 20% in 2024
  • 03/One-third of institutions plan to boost gold holdings; 29% expect a weaker dollar in five years

Sovereign wealth funds and central banks managing $29 trillion in assets are turning to energy assets and infrastructure. An Invesco survey published on Monday revealed the shift.

The survey covered 90 sovereign wealth funds and 54 central banks. Respondents are prioritizing portfolios that can "take a hit and still hold it together."

Trade tariffs, closed shipping channels, and wars in Ukraine and the Middle East are driving the reassessment. Investors want resilience, not just returns.

Some 80% of those polled said energy security and energy transition infrastructure were the most credible investments for portfolio resilience. Infrastructure now accounts for 9% of sovereign wealth fund assets in 2026.

The race to build energy-hungry AI infrastructure added to the appeal, Invesco found. Real assets are replacing bonds as a diversification tool.

Concerns about the dollar were "widespread and deepening." 61% of central banks said US debt levels negatively impact the dollar's long-term position as a reserve asset, up from just 20% in 2024.

While the US-Israeli war with Iran has lifted the dollar 3% this year, analysts warn US policy uncertainty and high debt could weaken it long term. 29% of respondents said the dollar's reserve-currency status will be weaker in five years, up from 12% in 2022.

Several institutions are reviewing reliance on US-based custodians and clearing infrastructure. One European central bank has already replaced its US custodian.

"In a world of inflation shocks, geopolitical fragmentation and more concentrated markets, investors are rethinking old assumptions about diversification," Invesco head of research Benjamin Jones said. "Resilience is becoming a hard requirement, not a nice-to-have."

One-third of those polled said they intended to boost gold holdings. The lack of a credible dollar alternative means any shift will likely be incremental.
