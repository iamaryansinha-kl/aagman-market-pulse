India 10-Year Bond Falls 10th Straight Week, Worst in 30 Years

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The benchmark 6.94% 2036 bond ended Friday at 7.4740%, versus 7.4596% previously — the tenth consecutive weekly decline, the worst streak in thirty years.
  • •- The yield rose 9 basis points this week, after surging 62 basis points in the previous nine weeks, the steepest rise since 1998.
  • •- The RBI raised the repo rate to 5.5% earlier this month and shifted to "calibrated tightening"; ICICI Securities Primary Dealership sees the policy rate at 6% by February.
  • •- A December hike of 25 basis points is "a done deal"; a bigger 50 basis-point move is possible depending on data.

India's bond market has not had a good week since August — literally. Government bonds fell for a tenth straight week on Friday, their worst consecutive decline in three decades, as elevated crude oil prices and bets on a hawkish central bank kept sellers in control of every auction and every rally attempt. The benchmark 6.94% 2036 bond ended the week at 7.4740%, against 7.4596% in the previous session; the yield rose 9 basis points this week, on top of a 62 basis-point surge over the previous nine weeks that marks the steepest rise since 1998. Add the two together and the benchmark yield has climbed roughly 71 basis points in ten weeks — a repricing of India's sovereign risk at a speed traders under the age of forty have never seen. In a market where yields move inversely to prices, that is a decade's worth of damage compressed into a quarter.

The proximate driver is oil. Brent crude hovered around $115 a barrel on Friday — its highest in nearly six months — as geopolitical tensions raised fears of further disruption to global energy supplies, and India imports close to 90% of its oil requirements. Every dollar added to crude widens the import bill, pressures the rupee, darkens the inflation outlook and weakens public finances — four channels through which an oil shock transmits directly into bond yields, and all four are currently open. The currency and equity markets have already repriced this reality; the bond market is now doing so for the tenth week in a row.

The monetary backdrop compounds it. The Reserve Bank of India earlier this month raised the benchmark repo rate to 5.5% and shifted its stance to "calibrated tightening" — language that explicitly keeps further hikes on the table if price pressures entrench. The minutes of that meeting, released subsequently, only hardened the market's read, and ICICI Securities Primary Dealership now forecasts the policy rate touching 6% by February. Its framing of the December meeting is blunt: "While a 25 bps hike in December is a done deal, a bigger 50 bps hike could make sense tactically depending on incoming data and global developments." When a primary dealer starts pricing a 50 basis-point move as tactically reasonable, the bond market's job is to sell first and ask questions later.

The most striking feature of this sell-off is how completely it has been absorbed. Overnight indexed swap rates ended the week largely unchanged — the 1-year OIS at 6.58%, the 2-year at 6.65% and the liquid 5-year at 6.8150% — which says the market's rate path is already pricing nearly all of the bad news. Derivatives that do not blink at a tenth consecutive weekly decline are telling you something important: investors believe the story, they believe the central bank's reaction function, and they are positioned accordingly. That is the strange equilibrium of October 2026: bond yields at thirty-year-record losing streaks, yet derivatives calm, because everyone agrees on why it is happening and roughly when it stops. The risk to that consensus is equally clear — a crude spike through $115 into genuinely uncharted territory, or an RBI that decides 50 basis points is not a tactical option but a necessity.

For borrowers, the pass-through has begun in earnest. The government's own borrowing costs reprice every week this streak extends, and since the sovereign sets the floor for every other rupee borrower in the economy, corporate issuers and state governments will discover the new cost of funds at their next auctions. Banks, insurers and mutual funds holding long-dated government paper have already marked weeks of losses on their bond portfolios. For the RBI, the streak is a live experiment in how much tightening the economy can absorb while growth forecasts still rise — the central bank itself has nudged FY27 GDP growth to 7.1%. Somewhere between those two facts — a bond market pricing pain and an economy still forecast to grow at 7% — the next quarter of Indian macro will be decided, and the December policy meeting is where the two stories meet.

The historical framing deserves emphasis. Bond market veterans measure regimes in decades, and a ten-week losing streak with a 71 basis-point backup in the benchmark has no parallel in the modern Indian market — the last comparable episode dates to 1998, when yields were a different animal and the RBI a different institution. What makes 2026's version distinctive is its provenance: this is not a balance-of-payments scare or a fiscal-crisis trade, but a slow-motion repricing driven by energy economics and a central bank that has signalled, clearly and repeatedly, that it will tolerate the pain rather than rescue the market. For allocators who built portfolios around the assumption that Indian yields had a ceiling, the last ten weeks have been an expensive education.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
HOW LONG HAS THE BOND SELLOFF RUN?
Ten consecutive weeks — the worst losing streak for Indian government bonds in thirty years. The benchmark 2036 bond's yield has risen 71 basis points in that span, the steepest rise since 1998.
WHAT IS DRIVING IT?
Crude oil near $115 a barrel (India imports about 90% of its oil) and a hawkish RBI that has shifted to "calibrated tightening" after raising the repo rate to 5.5% earlier this month.
WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?
ICICI Securities Primary Dealership expects the policy rate at 6% by February, with a 25 basis-point December hike "a done deal" and a possible 50 basis-point move if data deteriorates.
WHY DOES CRUDE OIL HIT INDIAN BONDS SO DIRECTLY?
India imports close to 90% of its oil, so higher crude widens the trade deficit, pressures the rupee, and worsens the inflation outlook — a combination that forces bond yields up and narrows the RBI's room to stay patient. GLOSSARY: REPO RATE — The RBI's benchmark short-term lending rate to banks; raising it tightens money and pushes bond yields up. BASIS POINT — One-hundredth of a percentage point; a 9 basis-point weekly rise in a bond yield is a significant move for a benchmark sovereign. OVERNIGHT INDEXED SWAP (OIS) — A derivatives contract reflecting where markets expect the policy rate to be over a horizon; flat OIS during a selloff means the pain is already priced. CALIBRATED TIGHTENING — An RBI stance signalling rate hikes remain on the table, with the pace dependent on incoming inflation data.
