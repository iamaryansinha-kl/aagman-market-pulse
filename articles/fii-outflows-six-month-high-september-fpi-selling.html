FPI Outflows Hit Six-Month High in September: Rs 23,676 Crore Sold

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/FPIs sold Rs 23,676 crore of Indian equities through September 19, 2026, the heaviest monthly outflow since March 2026
  • 02/The selling snapped a two-month inflow streak: FPIs had bought Rs 11,045 crore in July and Rs 10,231 crore in August
  • 03/DIIs absorbed the selling with net purchases of about Rs 36,219 crore in the same period; the Nifty fell roughly 3% from its August-end close of 24,080

Foreign portfolio investors have turned aggressive sellers of Indian equities in September 2026. NSDL data shows net outflows of Rs 23,676 crore through the exchanges in the month up to September 19.

That makes September the heaviest month of foreign selling since March 2026. It also marks a sharp reversal from the brief recovery seen over the summer.

FPIs had returned as buyers in July with inflows of Rs 11,045 crore. They added another Rs 10,231 crore in August, raising hopes that the selling wave had ended.

Those hopes have now fizzled. Within the first four trading days of September alone, foreign investors had already pulled out Rs 7,443 crore.

The monthly picture for 2026 has been brutal. January saw sales of Rs 34,152 crore, followed by inflows of Rs 12,950 crore in February.

March brought the record: a massive Rs 1.15 lakh crore outflow as the US-Israel-Iran conflict escalated. Selling continued through April (Rs 71,203 crore), May (Rs 50,188 crore) and June (Rs 35,174 crore).

The July and August inflows were the only break in an otherwise relentless exit. September's renewed selling confirms the outflow cycle is still intact.

Several forces are driving the exits. Brent crude is hovering around $107 a barrel, a headwind for India's import bill.

US bond yields are another magnet. The US 10-year Treasury yield is near 5.24%, close to a two-decade high, making risk-free dollar assets hard to ignore.

The rupee has added to the problem, breaching 96 per dollar in late September. Currency depreciation erodes dollar returns for foreign funds even before stock losses.

Valuations complete the case against India. Indian equities still trade at a premium to Asian peers, and foreign investors have judged that premium hard to justify in a high-yield world.

There is also a structural shift in how foreign capital engages India. FIIs have increasingly favoured primary-market exposure over the secondary market, preferring IPOs to listed stocks.

The cushion has come from domestic institutional investors. DIIs bought roughly Rs 36,219 crore in the month to September 19, extending a consistent buying streak.

That domestic bid has limited the damage. The Nifty was down about 3% from its August-end close of 24,080 over the same stretch, far milder than the foreign selling would normally produce.

The cumulative numbers underline the scale of the exit. FPI secondary-market outflows for 2026 stood at roughly Rs 2.4 lakh crore by late September, per multiple trackers.

Foreign ownership of NSE-listed companies has fallen to about 15%, a 15-year low. Domestic investors now carry a structurally larger share of the market than at any point in recent memory.

CONTEXT: The 2026 exodus is best understood as a rotation, not a verdict on India alone. Global capital has been chasing AI and semiconductor exposure in North Asia, where Taiwan's market is up roughly 40% in dollar terms this year, South Korea's Kospi has gained around 62%, and Japan's Nikkei is up about 18%. India's Sensex and Nifty, by contrast, have fallen roughly 13-14% over the period. Add elevated crude, a near-5.24% US 10-year yield offering risk-free dollar returns above 5%, and a rupee that has slipped past 96 per dollar, and the arithmetic for a foreign fund manager becomes straightforward. Since 2020, the market's microstructure has also changed: DII and retail SIP flows now arrive every month regardless of global mood, which is why foreign selling no longer produces the crashes it once did.

IMPACT: Near term, persistent FPI selling keeps index-level pressure and volatility elevated, particularly in FII-heavy sectors such as banks and IT where earnings-downgrade risk is highest. There is also a feedback loop to watch: heavy selling weakens the rupee further, and a weaker rupee trims dollar returns, which can trigger more selling. The reversal would likely need a combination of a crude correction, a Fed pause that pulls US yields down from two-decade highs, and a valuation reset that narrows India's premium to Asian peers. Until then, domestic flows remain the market's principal support, and the Nifty's relatively mild 3% slide despite Rs 23,676 crore of selling shows that support is real. The risk is what happens if DII buying ever slows while foreign exits continue.
