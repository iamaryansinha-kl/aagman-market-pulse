FIIs Sold Rs 6.13 Lakh Crore of Indian Equities Since Tax Hike

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Foreign investors have net sold Indian equities worth around Rs 6.13 lakh crore since July 2024, when capital gains taxes were raised.
  • •- Outflows were Rs 2,774 crore in the remainder of 2024, Rs 1.66 lakh crore in 2025, and Rs 4.47 lakh crore in 2026 so far.
  • •- In July 2024, the short-term capital gains tax was raised from 15% to 20% and the long-term rate from 10% to 12.5%.
  • •- Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman said on Tuesday that discussions on LTCG and securities transaction tax are ongoing.
  • •- Analysts say a tax rejig could trigger sharp foreign inflows, though views differ on the scale.
  • •- FIIs remained net sellers on Tuesday, offloading Rs 1,305 crore even as benchmarks rallied 1.5%.

Foreign investors have net sold Indian equities worth around Rs 6.13 lakh crore since July 2024, when the government overhauled how profits from the stock market are taxed, according to data tracked by brokerages — a two-year drain that has become the central argument in the growing debate over whether India should roll back its capital gains regime.

The timeline tells the story. In July 2024, following the general elections, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman announced the hike: the tax on short-term capital gains, on profits booked within a year, rose from 15 per cent to 20 per cent, while the long-term capital gains rate on equities went from 10 per cent to 12.5 per cent. Analysts warned at the time that the changes would spook foreign investors. The warning came true, slowly at first and then in a flood.

Foreign investors sold Rs 2,774 crore between the announcement and the end of 2024 — a modest reaction, suggesting many were waiting to see whether the policy would stick. It did, and the selling accelerated: Rs 1.66 lakh crore in 2025, and Rs 4.47 lakh crore in 2026 so far, fuelled by a stack of additional headwinds including Donald Trump's tariff flip-flops, the Middle East war, a weakening rupee and soaring bond yields. What began as a tax-driven reassessment became a full-scale reassessment of Indian equity risk.

The policy debate has now reopened from the top. On Tuesday, at the Bloomberg New Economy Forum, Sitharaman said discussions around the long-term capital gains tax and the securities transaction tax were ongoing, and that the debate on transaction taxes and whether different asset classes should be treated similarly had been well aired. The comments triggered a sharp rally on Dalal Street, with the Sensex and Nifty jumping more than 1.5 per cent each. Notably, foreign investors were not convinced: they remained net sellers on Tuesday, offloading Rs 1,305 crore according to provisional NSE data.

Analysts see real potential in a reversal. Siddhartha Khemka, head of research at Motilal Oswal Financial Services, said a reduction in capital gains taxes could spark sharp FII inflows, improve investor sentiment and support equity market participation. Gurmeet Chadha, managing partner and chief investment officer at Complete Circle Consultants, went further: if the finance minister reduces LTCG and STT, he argued, India could attract over $150 billion of FII and FDI flows in the next three years, surpassing even the FCNR deposit drive — and, he added, the capital gains and STT collections would actually go up as volumes rise. "This is the power of capital markets. It's a win win," he wrote on Wednesday.

The legal and structural case is equally pointed. Punit Shah, partner at Dhruva Advisors, said there is a reasonable case for rationalising the LTCG regime, potentially including abolition of the long-term gains tax on listed equities and a reduction in the holding period required for long-term treatment. He noted that the securities transaction tax was originally introduced in lieu of capital gains tax, so layering both makes equity transactions expensive and less attractive, directly discouraging foreign capital.

There are cautions. Pranay Aggarwal, director and CEO of Stoxkart, said that while abolition could improve post-tax returns and encourage longer-term equity participation, the extent of any increase in retail investment or foreign inflows would depend on broader market conditions, valuations and global liquidity — and the government would need to weigh revenue implications against indirect economic benefits.

That revenue trade-off is the heart of the matter. The 2024 tax hike was, at the time, a bet that higher rates on a booming market would raise more money than they cost in lost foreign flows. With Rs 6.13 lakh crore of net selling on the books and a market capitalisation several lakh crores below where it might otherwise have been, that bet looks increasingly expensive. Whether the finance ministry reaches the same arithmetic will shape the Budget — and possibly the next leg of the market.

The selling has also quietly changed who owns Indian equities. Domestic institutional investors and retail investors, funnelled through monthly systematic investment plans, have absorbed a large share of what foreigners sold — the reason Indian indices have fallen far less than the gross outflow numbers imply. But there is a ceiling to that substitution: SIP inflows are sticky but not infinite, and the foreign overhang hangs over every rally, as Tuesday showed when FIIs sold Rs 1,305 crore into a 1.5 per cent surge.

The STT wrinkle adds to the grievance. The securities transaction tax was originally introduced in 2004 in lieu of capital gains tax — a simple levy collected at source in exchange for exempting gains. Successive governments layered capital gains tax back on top, so equity investors now pay both a turnover tax on every trade and a gains tax on profits. For high-turnover foreign funds, that double incidence is arithmetic they can calculate to the basis point — and several have said, publicly and privately, that it shapes where Asia allocations go.

What happens next hinges on the Budget calendar and the political economy of the moment. Tax rationalisation would forego known revenue in the near term for uncertain flows later — a difficult sell when the fiscal deficit is under scrutiny. But with FII selling accelerating, the rupee under pressure and the RBI tightening into an oil shock, the cost of the status quo is no longer theoretical. The Rs 6.13 lakh crore question is whether New Delhi decides that cheaper equity capital is worth more than the revenue line it replaced.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
HOW MUCH HAVE FIIS SOLD SINCE THE 2024 TAX HIKE?
Foreign investors have net sold about Rs 6.13 lakh crore of Indian equities since July 2024 — Rs 2,774 crore in the rest of 2024, Rs 1.66 lakh crore in 2025 and Rs 4.47 lakh crore in 2026 so far.
WHAT CHANGED IN JULY 2024?
The short-term capital gains tax on equities was raised from 15% to 20%, and the long-term capital gains tax from 10% to 12.5%.
WHAT DID THE FINANCE MINISTER SAY?
Nirmala Sitharaman said on Tuesday that discussions on LTCG and securities transaction tax are ongoing, fuelling hopes of a tax rejig in the Budget.
COULD A TAX CUT BRING FIIS BACK?
Analysts believe so. Gurmeet Chadha estimates over $150 billion of FII and FDI flows in three years if LTCG and STT are reduced, though others caution that global liquidity and valuations also matter. GLOSSARY: - FII: Foreign Institutional Investor, an overseas fund investing in Indian securities. - LTCG: Long-term capital gains tax, levied on equity profits held beyond one year. - STCG: Short-term capital gains tax, levied on equity profits booked within one year. - STT: Securities Transaction Tax, a levy on every equity exchange transaction in India. - FCNR: Foreign Currency Non-Resident deposits, raised from Indians overseas to bolster dollar reserves.
