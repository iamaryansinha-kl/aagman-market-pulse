GoDaddy Challenges Delhi Court Ruling on Fake Websites, Warns It Could 'Make Internet Less Safe'

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/GoDaddy challenges Delhi court ruling on fake websites, arguing it could make the internet less safe for legitimate businesses
  • 02/Court blocked 1,100 websites and barred domain sellers from offering privacy protection by default; required buyer details release within 72 hours
  • 03/India received over 2.4 million cyber fraud complaints worth $2.4 billion in 2025; matter to be heard on 16 July

GoDaddy, the world's largest internet domain seller, has challenged a 2025 Delhi court order aimed at limiting fake websites. The company warned that the ruling could make the internet less safe for genuine businesses and have worldwide consequences.

The legal battle stems from petitions filed by more than 20 companies in 2019. These companies included Amazon, McDonald's, Microsoft, Xiaomi and Colgate-Palmolive. They sought court intervention against fake websites that were misusing their brands.

The court blocked 1,100 websites in its ruling. The judge observed that fake websites were "engines for large-scale deception."

The court announced stringent measures for domain sellers. It barred them from offering free privacy protection by default to buyers. It prohibited domain sellers from registering domain names that are variations of protected brand names. It also required domain sellers to release the buyer's details to anyone with a "legitimate interest" within 72 hours.

GoDaddy has challenged the December 2025 ruling before a larger bench of the Delhi High Court. The company argued that the order could severely affect legitimate businesses whose names resemble those of well-known brands.

GoDaddy said stopping privacy-by-default features would result in public disclosure of name, address, telephone and email of legitimate website owners. This would expose them to "foreseeable privacy and security risks" such as stalking and harassment.

The company also noted that as domain names operate worldwide, the court's ruling could compel the domain seller to regulate website addresses globally.

Regarding the 72-hour deadline to release registration details, GoDaddy argued it lacks the means to determine whether a requester has a legitimate interest in accessing a domain registrant's details.

In one appeal document, the company called the order "commercially destabilising." It noted the ruling could force domain companies to "exit India."

GoDaddy's rivals, including Arizona-based Namecheap and Netherlands-based Hosting Concepts, have also challenged the ruling.

GoDaddy argues that weakening the privacy feature would contradict India's data protection law and the European Union's GDPR. Both require a "privacy by default" approach.

Despite the court order, GoDaddy continues to advertise its service as offering "free privacy protection forever." It states that it redacts customers' names, addresses, phone numbers and email addresses from the public directory.

India is seeing soaring demand for smartphones and internet use. However, the increasing demand is coinciding with a growing problem of online fraud. Last year, the central government received over 2.4 million complaints of alleged cyber fraud, worth $2.4 billion.

Earlier this year, Union Home Minister Amit Shah said that every 37 seconds, at least one person falls prey to cybercrime. He warned that inaction could turn the menace into a "national crisis."

The matter is scheduled to be heard on 16 July.
