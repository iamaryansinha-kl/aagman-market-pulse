FM Flags $100 Billion China Trade Gap, Cites Non-Tariff Barriers

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- India's trade deficit with China has exceeded $100 billion this year.
  • •- The FM called China's use of non-tariff barriers the "biggest hurdle" in bilateral trade.
  • •- Indian generic medicines, beef and vegetables face phytosanitary and other restrictions despite "immense" potential.
  • •- India has refrained from imposing retaliatory non-tariff measures or countervailing duties.
  • •- The FM also called India's ASEAN trade agreements "very skewed" and flagged oil and fertiliser import dependence.
  • •- The remarks came on the sidelines of the IMF meeting in Bangkok.

Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman has put a hard number on India's oldest trade frustration: the deficit with China has crossed $100 billion this year, and in her telling it is growing "exponentially" — even as the diplomatic relationship between New Delhi and Beijing enjoys its warmest phase since the 2020 border confrontation.

Speaking at an interaction with the Atlantic Council on the sidelines of the International Monetary Fund meeting in Bangkok, Sitharaman identified the cause with unusual bluntness. "China uses non-tariff barriers unhesitatingly," she said, calling that "the biggest hurdle in the trade between India and China." She pointed to phytosanitary regulations — measures nominally designed to prevent the spread of plant pests and diseases — as one category of restriction keeping Indian products out, and named the goods with the most to gain: generic medicines, beef and vegetables, all of which she said hold "immense" potential in the Chinese market but cannot get in.

The asymmetry she described is stark. China sells freely into what is now the world's most populous market; Indian exporters face regulatory walls in the opposite direction. Yet New Delhi has deliberately not answered in kind: Sitharaman confirmed India has refrained from imposing retaliatory non-tariff restrictions or countervailing duties on Chinese goods — a restraint that reflects both the cost of escalation and India's continued dependence on Chinese imports across electronics, pharmaceuticals' active ingredients, machinery and solar components. The deficit, in other words, is not merely a trade-policy problem; it is entangled with supply chains Indian industry cannot yet replace.

The timing sharpens the message. External Affairs Minister S Jaishankar said earlier this week that India was not "getting a level playing field in China," and Prime Minister Narendra Modi and President Xi Jinping have been working to stabilise relations after years of tension that followed the deadly 2020 border clash. Sitharaman's Bangkok remarks make clear that warmer diplomacy has not papered over the commercial imbalance — if anything, the widening deficit is being cited as the reason the current thaw needs to produce market access, not just handshakes.

Her frame extended beyond Beijing. The finance minister described India's existing trade arrangements with Southeast Asian economies as "very skewed" against New Delhi, saying India intends to keep its ASEAN agreement but will seek revised terms to address the imbalance — a signal that the ongoing trade-agreement push with partners from New Zealand to the UK and EU will be matched by a harder look at legacy deals. She also returned to a vulnerability this publication has tracked all year: India's dependence on imported crude oil and fertilisers, whose import bill remains "big and growing" and contributes to the foreign-exchange outflows that have pressured the rupee through 2026. The Reserve Bank of India's interventions — dollar sales, a special foreign-exchange facility for state-owned oil companies, tighter currency-derivative rules — have stabilised the currency, but the structural bill for essential imports keeps arriving. It is the connective tissue of this year's economic story: every bilateral complaint, from Beijing to Washington, ultimately surfaces in the currency market, and Bangkok this week was no exception to that rule.

The strategic picture that emerges is a government trying to run three tracks at once: de-escalation with China, renegotiation of trade terms with everyone else, and accelerated domestic capacity-building so that the deficits of the 2020s do not repeat in the 2030s.

Consider the calendar alongside the complaint. India is simultaneously implementing its pact with New Zealand, advancing negotiations with the UK and the European Union, and managing the fallout of its tariff confrontation with the United States — a trade-diplomacy workload unmatched by any other major economy this year. Sitharaman's Bangkok remarks add the missing piece to that map: the deals being signed are only half the agenda, and the other half is fixing the relationships where the imbalance runs the other way. ASEAN's "skewed" terms and China's $100 billion wall of non-tariff barriers are the two biggest entries on that second list.

For Indian exporters, the practical significance is that market access has replaced tariffs as the binding constraint. The goods India is best positioned to sell into China — pharmaceuticals, agricultural products, specialty chemicals — are precisely the categories where regulatory approvals and sanitary rules function as the gatekeeper, which means progress will be measured in registrations and inspections rather than headline summits. None of that will move quickly; phytosanitary negotiations have a pace of their own, and Beijing has never yet liberalised on anyone else's timetable but its own. For the deficit to narrow, either Chinese demand for Indian goods must be unleashed or Indian demand for Chinese goods must shrink — and on current evidence, only one of those two has any political momentum behind it. The $100 billion deficit is the scoreboard for all three tracks. Until Indian goods can actually clear Chinese customs, Sitharaman's message from Bangkok suggests, the deficit will keep compounding — and the diplomatic thaw will keep getting measured against it, quarter after quarter, in both capitals.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID THE FINANCE MINISTER SAY ABOUT CHINA TRADE?
She called China's non-tariff barriers the biggest hurdle in bilateral trade and flagged that India's trade deficit with China has exceeded $100 billion this year.
WHICH INDIAN PRODUCTS FACE BARRIERS IN CHINA?
Generic medicines, beef and vegetables, among others, face phytosanitary and other non-tariff restrictions despite strong potential demand.
HAS INDIA RETALIATED?
No — Sitharaman said India has refrained from retaliatory non-tariff restrictions or countervailing duties on Chinese goods.
WHAT ELSE DID SHE FLAG?
She described ASEAN trade agreements as "very skewed" and cited India's oil and fertiliser import bill as a growing vulnerability contributing to rupee pressure. GLOSSARY: - Non-tariff barriers: Restrictions other than import duties — such as licensing, quotas or sanitary rules — used to limit trade. - Phytosanitary regulations: Measures designed to prevent the spread of plant pests and diseases, sometimes used as de facto trade barriers. - Trade deficit: The gap when a country's imports from a partner exceed its exports to it. - Countervailing duties: Tariffs imposed to offset foreign subsidies or unfair pricing.
