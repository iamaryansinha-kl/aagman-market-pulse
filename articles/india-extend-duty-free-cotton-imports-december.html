India to Extend Duty-Free Cotton Imports Through December

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • Key points unavailable

India is expected to extend duty-free cotton imports for another two months through December, three government and industry sources said on Monday, in a move that could cut input costs for textile mills, curb firm domestic prices and lift imports from the United States, Brazil and Australia.

The world's second-biggest cotton producer had waived the 11 percent customs duty on cotton imports for five months from June 1 through October 31, aiming to augment supplies during the lean season. That window is now set to run at least until the end of December.

The timeline matters because of how the crop cycle works. Domestic cotton prices usually ease once new-season arrivals begin in October. This year they have stayed stubbornly firm. A patchy monsoon has dented output prospects, mills are short of fibre, and the market has refused to soften the way it normally does at harvest time.

After inter-ministerial consultations, the government is expected to issue an order soon extending the duty exemption, the sources said. The finance, commerce and textile ministries did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

The price math explains the pressure. Domestic cotton is trading above Rs 9,000 per 100 kg, comfortably above the government-set minimum support price of Rs 8,717 for the new season. When market prices sit above support levels this early in the marketing year, it signals genuine tightness rather than speculative froth.

The supply-demand gap is wide. India's cotton output is likely to fall below 29 million bales in the 2026-27 season, against consumption demand of more than 34 million bales, according to trade estimates. That roughly 5 million bale shortfall has to be bridged from somewhere, and imports are the only lever that works quickly.

Structural problems keep the deficit recurring. In the absence of newer seed upgrades, farmers continue to use older genetically modified cotton varieties, leaving the crop exposed to pest attacks and stagnant yields. Output has struggled to keep pace with rising demand from a growing textile and apparel sector.

Dipanshi Agarwal, principal analyst at S&P Global, pointed to the tightness indicators: lower domestic output, a stock-to-use ratio of about 31% — the lowest level in five years — and strong textile demand all supported the case for extending the exemption. She cautioned, however, that additional imports could weigh on domestic prices, which is precisely the point from the mills' perspective.

The industry wants more. Chandrima Chatterjee, secretary-general of the Confederation of Indian Textile Industry, said India should remove the import tax for an extended period rather than renew it in two-month increments. In her view, the duty has only raised manufacturers' production costs and eroded competitiveness against Vietnam and Bangladesh in export markets. The stop-start approach, industry executives argue, makes it impossible to plan fibre procurement across a full season.

Trade data show mills are already voting with their wallets. India's cotton imports rose 63% year-on-year to $1.1 billion in the first six months of the current fiscal year from April, according to the trade ministry. That surge happened with the duty-free window open for only part of the period.

There are limits to how much relief imports can deliver. Benchmark US cotton futures are trading above 83 cents per pound, freight costs remain elevated, and the rupee has been weakening — down past 94.9 to the dollar on Monday. A dealer with a global trading house noted these factors could cap import volumes even if the duty-free window is extended. Imported fibre is cheaper without the 11 percent duty, but it is not cheap in absolute terms.

For the government, the extension is a balancing act. Cheaper imports help mills and support textile exports, but they pull domestic prices toward import parity, which farmers will feel at the mandi. With cotton prices already above the MSP, the fiscal cost of procurement operations stays contained for now — a political calculation that can flip quickly if global prices fall further.

The immediate beneficiaries are spinners in Tamil Nadu, Gujarat and Maharashtra, who have been squeezed between firm domestic fibre costs and tepid yarn export demand. Every month of duty-free access trims raw material costs by roughly a tenth on imported volumes, a margin swing that matters in a low-spread business.

The order itself is expected shortly. For an industry that has lobbied for a full duty removal for years, two more months of relief is progress — but not the permanent resolution exporters say they need to compete with Southeast Asian rivals.
