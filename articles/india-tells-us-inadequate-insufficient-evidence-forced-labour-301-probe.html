India Tells US: Inadequate, Insufficient Evidence on Forced Labour 301 Probe

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • Key points unavailable

India is pushing back against US trade allegations.

The government has formally told the United States that the evidence supporting forced labour allegations in the ongoing Section 301 investigation is inadequate and insufficient. The response comes as the two countries negotiate a broader trade agreement, with the forced labour issue emerging as a major sticking point that could derail progress.

Section 301 is a powerful US trade tool. Under the Trade Act of 1974, the US can impose tariffs or other trade restrictions on countries that violate trade agreements or engage in unfair practices. The forced labour probe was initiated following complaints from US labour groups and lawmakers about working conditions in certain Indian industries, particularly textiles and agriculture.

India's response challenges the evidentiary basis. The government has argued that the allegations are based on outdated information, isolated incidents, and reports from non-governmental organisations with political agendas. New Delhi maintains that India has robust labour laws and is actively working to improve enforcement, making broad-based trade sanctions unjustified.

The timing is sensitive. The two countries are in the middle of negotiating a comprehensive trade agreement that could cover everything from tariffs to intellectual property to digital trade. The forced labour issue, if not resolved, could lead to US tariffs on Indian exports, complicating the broader negotiation.

Other countries have faced similar pressure. The US has used forced labour allegations to justify trade restrictions against China, Vietnam, and Bangladesh. In some cases, the threat of tariffs has led to changes in labour practices; in others, it has simply disrupted trade without improving conditions.

The outcome will affect multiple sectors. If the US imposes tariffs under Section 301, Indian textiles, leather goods, and agricultural products could face higher barriers. These are labour-intensive sectors that employ millions of workers, many of them in informal or semi-formal arrangements that the US finds problematic.
