Aegis Logistics to Buy 90% of Tristar Holdings for $809 Million

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Aegis Logistics will acquire an 89.9% stake in Tristar Holdings for an equity consideration of $809 million.
  • •- The deal is executed through its Singapore step-down subsidiary and is expected to close in the fourth quarter of the current fiscal year.
  • •- Tristar, established in 1998 and incorporated in Dubai, operates in more than 30 countries across energy logistics.
  • •- For 2025, Tristar reported revenue of $1.4 billion, EBITDA of $257 million and net profit of $82 million.
  • •- Chairman and MD Raj Chandaria called the deal immediately accretive to profitability.
  • •- Aegis shares rose nearly 3% to Rs 1,430 on the BSE on Wednesday.

Aegis Logistics on Wednesday announced its largest acquisition yet: a definitive agreement to acquire an 89.9 per cent stake in Dubai-based Tristar Holdings for an equity consideration of $809 million, a deal that transforms the Indian company from a domestic operator into a global energy logistics platform.

The transaction is being executed through Aegis Logistics International Pte, the company's wholly owned step-down subsidiary, and was disclosed in an exchange filing on Tuesday. The sellers are Agility Tristar Holdings Ltd., Gulf Investment Corporation and Diamond Holdings Ltd. The acquisition is subject to regulatory approvals and is expected to be completed in the fourth quarter of the current fiscal year.

Tristar is no small bolt-on. Established in 1998 and incorporated in Dubai, the company is an integrated liquid logistics provider offering end-to-end services across maritime logistics, fuel tank storage, road transport, aviation fuelling and fuel distribution, and it operates in more than 30 countries. Its customer base spans the global energy trade — the tankers, terminals and fuel-handling contracts that keep aviation and shipping supplied.

The financials explain the price. For the financial year ended December 31, 2025, Tristar reported consolidated revenue of $1.4 billion, EBITDA of $257 million and net profit of $82 million, according to the filing. That means Aegis is paying roughly ten times EBITDA for a business with established infrastructure, long-term customer contracts and a footprint across three continents — a valuation that looks full but defensible for an asset-heavy platform in a strategic sector.

Raj Chandaria, Aegis Logistics' Chairman and Managing Director, framed the deal as a redefinition of the company. "The transaction will allow Aegis to benefit from greater scale, as well as business and geographic diversification. Furthermore, the acquisition will grant Aegis access to an extensive network of blue-chip customers and premier industry partners," he said, adding that the acquisition would mark the company's transition from a domestically focused operator to an international energy logistics platform, and is expected to contribute to overall profitability immediately.

Tristar Group CEO Eugene Mayne struck a similar note on the other side of the table. "Together, we look forward to building a more competitive and globally connected business, while upholding our unwavering commitment to safety, operational excellence and delivering long-term value to our customers," he said.

The deal logic runs through India's energy security story. Aegis built its domestic franchise on liquefied petroleum gas and liquid logistics terminals — storage and distribution infrastructure that grows with India's fuel consumption. Tristar extends the same model overseas, giving Aegis exposure to international aviation fuelling and maritime logistics, businesses where India-linked trade flows are expanding. The two companies' networks complement rather than overlap, which is why the integration risk looks lower than in most cross-border deals of this size.

Investors greeted the announcement with a modest nod: shares rose nearly 3 per cent to Rs 1,430 on the BSE on Wednesday. The restrained move suggests the market is waiting for detail on financing and the regulatory calendar before re-rating the stock — fair, given that an $809 million cheque is large relative to Aegis' balance sheet and the deal's closing is still two quarters away.

BNP Paribas is acting as exclusive financial advisor to Aegis Logistics, while Norton Rose Fulbright and Veritas Legal are serving as legal counsel.

To understand the leap this deal represents, consider Aegis' domestic franchise. The company built its name on liquefied petroleum gas and liquid terminals along India's coasts and inland waterways — storage and distribution infrastructure that grows in step with the country's fuel consumption. That business is profitable and defensible, but it is bound by India's geography. Tristar breaks that boundary in a single stroke, adding aviation fuelling at international airports, maritime logistics across three continents, and fuel distribution relationships with the global energy majors.

The financial case rests on Tristar's $257 million of EBITDA against an $809 million price — a multiple near ten times, which is full for asset-heavy logistics but not excessive for a platform with contracted cash flows and scarce infrastructure assets. For Aegis shareholders, the accretion claim is testable: Tristar earned $82 million last year, and if that performance simply holds, the acquired earnings will be meaningful against Aegis' own profit base the moment the deal closes.

The transaction also fits a broader pattern of Indian mid-cap consolidation in logistics and energy infrastructure. With global energy trade rerouting around conflict zones and India emerging as a refining and bunkering hub, domestic operators with capital have been buying international capability rather than building it. Aegis is betting that owning the tanks, trucks and fuelling contracts matters more in this environment than renting access to them — a bet that will be validated or refuted by how quickly the integration delivers the promised blue-chip customer cross-selling.

The approvals pipeline is the near-term watch item. A transaction touching energy infrastructure across more than 30 countries will need clearances from competition and sectoral regulators in multiple jurisdictions, which is why management has left itself a full quarter, running to March, to complete the process. Until then, Aegis remains, on paper, a domestic company with a very large foreign cheque outstanding.
