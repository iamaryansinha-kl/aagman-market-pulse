Maharajah Bonds Get TDS Exemption in Boost for NDB Fundraising

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- A CBDT notification dated October 19 exempts interest on Maharajah Bonds from tax deducted at source from FY27 onward.
  • •- The exemption covers both resident investors and foreign institutional investors.
  • •- The New Development Bank plans to raise Rs 25,000 crore through Maharajah Bonds over five years.
  • •- Proceeds will finance sustainable infrastructure, green and social projects in India.
  • •- The move follows NDB's August representation to Irdai seeking access for Indian insurers to invest.
  • •- Insurers could become a key investor base once investment regulations permit.

A small tax change can be worth more than a big speech. On October 19, the Central Board of Direct Taxes issued a notification exempting interest payments on Maharajah Bonds from tax deducted at source for financial year 2026-27 and subsequent tax years — covering both resident investors and foreign institutional investors. The instruments in question are the rupee-denominated bonds issued offshore by the New Development Bank, the BRICS-sponsored multilateral lender, and the exemption arrives precisely as the bank prepares what could become the defining rupee-bond programme of the decade: a plan to raise Rs 25,000 crore over five years to fund infrastructure in India.

TDS sounds like an accounting footnote until you price it. For foreign institutional investors, tax deducted at source on interest creates an immediate cash-flow drag and a compliance trail across jurisdictions; many mandates are structured to avoid it entirely, which quietly shrinks the buyer list for any instrument that carries it. Removing TDS converts Maharajah Bonds from an exotic, tax-friction-heavy product into a conventional fixed-income allocation for global rupee investors — and for resident investors, the notification removes a parallel layer of friction that had made the bonds awkward relative to ordinary domestic debt. In bond-market terms, the exemption lowers the required yield break-even by exactly the friction it removes: a foreign fund no longer needs to be compensated for paperwork, blocked cash and reconciliation overhead before its first rupee of return. The government has, in effect, widened the exit lanes before the traffic arrives.

The context is NDB's deepening India strategy. In August, the Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India disclosed that it had received a representation from the New Development Bank seeking permission for Indian insurers to invest in Maharajah Bonds. That request cuts to the heart of the instrument's design: the bonds are issued offshore but denominated in rupees, and the natural, deepest pools of long-dated rupee money — Indian insurers and pension funds — can only participate if the regulatory plumbing permits it. The CBDT notification removes the tax objection; the investment-regulation question is the one still open, and the Irdai process is now the gate everyone is watching.

For India, the stakes extend beyond one bank's funding plan. The Maharajah Bond label — the rebranded successor to the masala bond experiment of the mid-2010s — is a test of whether the rupee can become a genuine funding currency for multilateral development lending. A BRICS bank borrowing rupees offshore and on-lending them into Indian green, social and infrastructure projects creates a loop with few intermediaries: offshore investors take rupee exposure, India gets project finance without adding to hard-currency external debt, and the currency gains another spoke in its slow internationalisation. The five-year, Rs 25,000 crore envelope gives the programme enough size to build a liquid benchmark curve rather than a series of one-off private placements — the difference between a programme and a gimmick. It would also mark a notable expansion of NDB's on-the-ground presence in India, where the bank's lending has historically run through its Shanghai headquarters with a regional office in New Delhi; a rupee-funded balance sheet anchored to Indian projects makes the India operation structurally deeper, not just numerically larger.

None of this guarantees demand, and the honest caveats are visible. Rupee depreciation remains the foreign investor's core risk, and no TDS exemption touches that; a bond paying 7 per cent in rupees still loses money for a dollar investor if the currency slides 8 per cent over the holding period. India's own record with masala bonds is mixed — issuance volumes never fulfilled the early promise after the 2016 launch, partly for tax-treatment reasons exactly like the one just fixed, and partly because offshore appetite for rupee duration proved shallower than the architects hoped. And the investor base that matters most, domestic insurers, still needs Irdai's blessing before their balance sheets can absorb the supply. But the sequence of moves since August — NDB's representation, the regulator's disclosure, now the tax exemption — reads like a coordinated build-out rather than coincidence. New Delhi wants this market to work.

The near-term test is practical: if Irdai opens the door for insurers, the first NDB Maharajah Bond tranches should find ready demand from institutions hungry for long-tenor, quasi-sovereign rupee paper at a time when the domestic credit market is setting issuance records and AAA spreads remain tight. That demand matters more than symbolism; every crore the NDB raises offshore in rupees is a crore India can spend on transmission lines, metro systems and water projects without adding a dollar of external debt to the sovereign's own ledger. The TDS exemption just made that paper easier to own for everyone else. For a development-finance channel that could steer billions into Indian infrastructure without touching the sovereign's own borrowing programme, that is a modest tax line with an outsized agenda behind it.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID THE CBDT NOTIFICATION SAY?
Interest on Maharajah Bonds paid to eligible bondholders is exempt from TDS for FY2026-27 and subsequent years, covering residents and foreign institutional investors.
WHAT ARE MAHARAJAH BONDS?
Rupee-denominated bonds issued offshore; the New Development Bank plans a Rs 25,000 crore programme over five years to fund sustainable infrastructure, green and social projects in India.
WHY DOES THE EXEMPTION MATTER?
TDS created cash-flow and compliance friction that shrank the buyer base; removing it makes the bonds easier for global and domestic investors to own.
WHO CAN INVEST NOW?
FIIs and residents benefit from the tax change; Indian insurers still need Irdai permission, which NDB sought in August. GLOSSARY: - Tax deducted at source (TDS): Tax collected by the payer before interest or income reaches the recipient. - Maharajah Bonds: Offshore bonds denominated in Indian rupees, named as a successor to masala bonds. - New Development Bank (NDB): A multilateral development bank established by the BRICS nations. - Green and social projects: Borrowings earmarked for environmental or social-impact purposes.
