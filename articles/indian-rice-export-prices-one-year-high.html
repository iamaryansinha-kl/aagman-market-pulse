Indian Rice Export Prices at One-Year High as Crop Tightens

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- India's 5% broken parboiled rice was quoted at $384-389 per ton, up from $378-384 last week — a more than one-year high.
  • •- 5% broken white rice was priced at $385-390 per ton.
  • •- A stronger rupee and firm domestic paddy prices on below-normal rainfall drove the rise.
  • •- Vietnam's prices hit a near two-month high; Thailand's climbed to their highest since September 10.
  • •- The Philippines is expected to resume rice purchases from Vietnam from November.

Indian rice export prices climbed to their highest level in more than a year this week, with the benchmark 5 per cent broken parboiled variety quoted at $384-389 per ton against $378-384 a week earlier — a move that captures, in a single commodity, the two forces currently shaping India's agricultural trade: a rupee that has strengthened sharply this month, and paddy markets tightening on the back of below-normal rainfall.

The rupee's role deserves emphasis because it runs against the usual script. Through most of this year the currency's slide flattered Indian exporters; the last fortnight's recovery, which has taken the rupee from record lows back below 95 against the dollar, has now begun to squeeze them — exporters must raise dollar prices to preserve rupee realisations, and in a competitive global market that prices Indian rice out of some deals. Indian 5 per cent broken white rice was offered at $385-390 per ton this week, comfortably above the levels that prevailed through the summer.

On the supply side, the domestic market is doing its own work. "Paddy prices remain firm in the domestic market due to limited supplies," a Mumbai-based exporter said. "Production has declined in several states because of below-normal rainfall." With the kharif harvest arriving into a market already short of inventory, millers are paying up for raw material, and export quotes are being marked higher to match. The irony is familiar to agricultural economists: the same deficient monsoon that has complicated India's macro picture this year is now lifting rice prices by threatening output.

The regional picture puts India's move in context this week. Vietnamese 5 per cent broken rice rose to $440-450 per metric ton on Thursday, its highest since August 21, with traders reporting that the Philippines will resume purchases from Vietnam from November and an agriculture ministry official confident the country will meet its 8-million-ton export target this year. Thai 5 per cent broken rice climbed to $480 per ton, its highest since September 10, on vessel arrivals and exporter restocking ahead of the off-season harvest's end — though the Thai Rice Exporters Association warned exports could still fall short of this year's 7-million-ton target amid competition from India and Pakistan. In Bangladesh, domestic rice prices kept climbing on higher fuel costs and rising paddy prices, with fine rice at 74-75 taka per kilogram against 72 a week earlier.

For India, the world's largest rice exporter, the pricing dilemma is now acute. The government spent much of the past two years managing the politics of rice — banning exports in 2023 to tame domestic inflation, then easing restrictions as supplies normalised — and the current combination of strong domestic prices and an appreciating currency threatens to slow the export recovery just as buyers like the Philippines re-enter the market. Every dollar-per-ton increase in export quotes narrows India's traditional price advantage over Vietnam and Thailand, whose grades typically command premiums of $50-90 over Indian parboiled.

The food-security lens cuts the other way. Firm paddy prices are good news for India's farmers, whose realisations have lagged input-cost inflation for much of the past two years, and a stronger rupee lowers the cost of imported fuel and fertiliser that feed into production costs. The RBI, which has spent the fortnight defending the currency with rate rises and dollar mobilisation, will also note that an appreciating rupee does exactly this — transmits disinflation through cheaper imports and dearer exports. For consumers from Dhaka to Manila, though, the bill is getting steeper: rice, the world's most important staple, is quietly joining crude oil on the list of things that cost more this October than last.

The policy backdrop makes the moment delicate. New Delhi banned non-basmati rice exports in 2023 to contain domestic food inflation, then spent the following two years selectively easing restrictions as supplies stabilised — a calibration exercise between farmer incomes, consumer prices and export earnings that has never been easy to manage. Today's elevated quotes arrive just as that normalisation process was restoring India's volumes: if dollar prices keep climbing, buyers in Africa and Southeast Asia will accelerate substitution toward Vietnam and Pakistan, and India's hard-won export share will erode precisely when the harvest should be reclaiming it.

The next several weeks are decisive. The kharif crop is arriving now, and if the harvest confirms the production losses that traders are pricing, domestic paddy will stay firm through the winter and export quotes will follow; if arrivals surprise on the upside, today's one-year highs will mark the top of the move rather than its midpoint. The monsoon numbers, the rupee's level and the Philippines' return to Vietnamese buying are the three variables to watch — and for once, India's rice exporters would welcome a weaker currency and a wetter season in the same breath.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHY DID INDIAN RICE EXPORT PRICES RISE?
A stronger rupee squeezed exporter realisations and domestic paddy supplies tightened on below-normal rainfall, pushing 5% broken parboiled rice to $384-389 per ton, a one-year high.
HOW DO INDIAN PRICES COMPARE WITH VIETNAM AND THAILAND?
Vietnam's 5% broken rice rose to $440-450 per ton and Thailand's to $480 — India retains a significant price advantage despite its own increases.
WHAT IS DRIVING VIETNAMESE AND THAI PRICES?
Expected Philippines buying from November and exporter restocking ahead of Thailand's off-season harvest ending.
WHAT DOES THIS MEAN FOR INDIAN FARMERS?
Firm domestic paddy prices support farm realisations, though higher export quotes could slow volumes if buyers switch to competitors. GLOSSARY: - 5% broken rice: Rice in which no more than 5% of grains are broken — a standard international grade for bulk trade. - Parboiled rice: Rice partially boiled in the husk before milling, popular in African and South Asian markets. - Paddy: Unmilled rice — the raw crop farmers sell to mills. - Kharif: India's monsoon-sown harvest season, running from summer planting to autumn arrival.
