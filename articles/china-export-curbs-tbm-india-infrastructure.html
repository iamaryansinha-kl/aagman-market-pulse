China Curbs Tunnel Boring Machine Exports, Hits India Projects

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- China's commerce ministry announced export controls Monday on tunnel boring machines (TBMs) and related items, effective November 18.
  • •- The curbs also cover walking excavators, plateau-type wet shotcrete machines and key dredger components such as slurry pumps, dragheads and cutterheads.
  • •- India exposure: underground metro systems, high-speed rail lines and tunnels in border areas rely on Chinese-linked TBM supply chains.
  • •- Exporters must obtain MOFCOM licences, declare dual-use codes and can see shipments held during verification.
  • •- Beijing previously blocked a German TBM maker operating in China from exporting to India, Commerce Minister Goyal said in 2024.
  • •- A Friday draft plan separately proposed curbs on 14 technology areas including AI, biotech and photovoltaics.

China on Monday added a new instrument to the economic-pressure toolkit it has been pointing at India: export controls on the machines that literally move mountains. The Ministry of Commerce announced restrictions on tunnel boring machines and related items, effective November 18, framed in the now-standard language of safeguarding national security and interests. For India, the practical meaning is immediate — TBMs are the indispensable workhorses of underground metro construction, high-speed rail tunnels and the strategic tunnels being bored in border areas, and Chinese manufacturers and Chinese-based supply chains dominate the global market for them.

The scope runs wider than tunnels. Monday's announcement also covers walking excavators — the all-terrain machines that excavate where wheeled equipment cannot go — and plateau-type wet shotcrete machines, which spray concrete in high-altitude construction. The dredger component list is specified down to the millimetre: slurry pumps with an inlet diameter of at least 0.55 metres and rotational speed no more than 400 rpm, pump casings and impellers of 1.3 metres or more, dragheads on trailing suction hopper dredgers with suction pipes of 0.55 metres or larger, and cutterheads on cutter suction dredgers with outer cutter rings of 2.1 metres or more. The granularity tells you the controls were written by people who know exactly which industries they are squeezing.

The compliance burden is the other half of the weapon. Exporters of the controlled items must apply for licences from MOFCOM under China's Export Control Law and its dual-use regulations, declare the specific export-control codes in customs filings, and — critically — even items not formally controlled but of similar technical specification must be documented with detailed technical parameters. Shipments can be held while customs verifies the declarations. In practice, that converts a licence regime into a slowdown regime: even approved sales move at the pace of Chinese bureaucratic discretion, and unapproved ones simply do not happen.

India has seen this specific movie before. In 2024, Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal told German Vice Chancellor Robert Habeck that Beijing was not allowing a German company manufacturing TBMs in China to export them to India — a striking early example of China's willingness to hold third-country supply chains hostage when the end user was Indian. Monday's formal controls generalise that precedent from an anecdote into policy. The timing lands amid an already strained bilateral commercial relationship: India's infrastructure pipeline — metro expansions in a dozen cities, rail and highway tunnels, port dredging — has built meaningful dependence on Chinese heavy machinery over two decades of cost-driven sourcing, and the Make-in-India heavy-equipment sector has not yet closed the capability gap for large-diameter TBMs.

Consider what a TBM actually is, and why the chokepoint is so hard to replace. A large-diameter machine is a bespoke, hundred-million-dollar piece of engineering — cutterheads, main bearings, slurry or earth-pressure balance systems — with a global supplier bench that has consolidated sharply over the past two decades as Chinese manufacturers, several of them state-backed, undercut European incumbents on price and delivery times. Once a country's contractors have standardised on those fleets, switching suppliers mid-project means new tooling, retrained crews and re-specified spare parts, measured in years rather than months. That installed-base economics is what makes an export control so potent: Beijing does not need to halt every shipment, only to make each one uncertain enough that project timelines and financing models start to bend.

Nor is this the end of the list. On Friday, MOFCOM released a draft plan proposing export restrictions on 14 technology areas spanning artificial intelligence, biotechnology and photovoltaic solar power — sectors where China holds chokepoint positions and where India has strategic ambitions of its own. Taken together, the two announcements sketch a doctrine: control the machines and the technologies, not just the finished goods, and let every infrastructure-dependent economy feel the leverage. New Delhi's response options run from accelerated domestic TBM manufacturing programmes and Japanese or European supplier diversification, to quietly absorbing delays on projects where no alternative supplier exists at comparable cost. None of them are fast, and all of them are expensive — which is precisely the point of the exercise from Beijing's side.

There is a strategic-geometry dimension as well. Many of the tunnels in India's construction queue are not urban metro boxes but connectivity links in the Himalayan frontier — roads and rail passages that carry both civilian freight and military logistics. An export control aimed at tunnel boring machinery therefore lands hardest exactly where India's strategic infrastructure is most sensitive, and where delays are measured not in commuter inconvenience but in years of capability lag along contested borders. Beijing knows this; the specificity of the controlled items reads like a map of India's project pipeline. Whether New Delhi treats November 18 as a deadline to negotiate, a reason to subsidise domestic heavy-machinery capacity, or simply the latest data point in a long decoupling will shape the infrastructure decade. The six-week window to decide is the most concrete thing Monday's announcement delivered.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT EXACTLY DID CHINA RESTRICT?
Export controls on tunnel boring machines and related items, walking excavators, plateau-type wet shotcrete machines, and dredger components including slurry pumps, dragheads and cutterheads meeting specified size thresholds, effective November 18.
WHY DOES THIS MATTER FOR INDIA?
India's underground metros, high-speed rail lines and border-area tunnels depend on TBM supply chains linked to China, and port projects use the controlled dredger components.
HOW DO THE CONTROLS WORK?
Exporters need MOFCOM licences under China's dual-use export rules, must declare control codes and technical parameters, and shipments can be held during customs verification.
HAS THIS HAPPENED BEFORE?
Yes — in 2024 India said China blocked a German TBM manufacturer operating in China from exporting machines to India; the new rules formalise that kind of restriction. GLOSSARY: - Tunnel boring machine (TBM): A massive cylindrical machine that excavates tunnels with a rotating cutterhead, used for metros, rail and road tunnels. - Dual-use items: Goods and technologies with both civilian and military applications, subject to export licensing. - Slurry pump: Heavy-duty pump that moves sand-and-water mixtures in dredging operations. - MOFCOM: China's Ministry of Commerce, which administers export controls.
