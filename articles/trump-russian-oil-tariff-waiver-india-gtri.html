Trump's Russian Oil Tariff Waiver Buys India Time, Not Safety

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Trump waived up to 100% tariffs on buyers of Russian oil on October 16.
  • •- The waiver was issued under the Graham Act, a sanctions law enacted September 18.
  • •- GTRI warned the waiver can be withdrawn at any time and used as trade leverage.
  • •- The US itself began buying Russian diesel on October 9; China has tariff protection until January 10.
  • •- Russia's share of India's oil imports fell from 51% in July to 44% in August.
  • •- GTRI urged India to keep buying oil on commercial terms and avoid permanent concessions.

The tariff bullet that had been aimed at India's energy trade turned out to be a blank — for now. US President Donald Trump on October 16 waived threatened tariffs of up to 100 per cent on countries buying Russian oil, stepping back from penalties that the newly enacted Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026 had authorised since September 18. For India, the world's largest buyer of discounted Russian crude, the relief is real; the question is what it costs, and the Global Trade Research Initiative's answer on Saturday was pointed: time, not safety.

"For India, the waiver buys time but offers no lasting certainty," said GTRI founder Ajay Srivastava. "India should protect its energy security, buy oil on commercial terms and prepare for renewed US pressure. Temporary tariff relief is no basis for permanent trade concessions." The mechanics support his caution: the waiver invokes Section 115 of the Act, has no fixed expiry date, and can be withdrawn at will. Under the underlying framework, the law still provides for a 500 per cent tariff increase on Russian goods and an additional 100 per cent duty on goods from major buyers of Russian oil making new purchases from October 18, 2026. The US Trade Representative will continue monitoring Russian energy purchases, which means the threat apparatus stays intact — only the trigger finger has relaxed.

GTRI's more uncomfortable argument is about why Washington stepped back, and what that reveals. Under an October 9 agreement with Russia, the US decided to buy Russian diesel to address domestic shortages and high fuel prices; penalising other countries for the same purchases the American government is making would invite obvious charges of double standards. China, meanwhile, secured protection from fresh tariff action until January 10 under the arrangement reached during Xi Jinping's US visit last month. Srivastava's conclusion is blunt: "The message is clear — US interests determine when penalties apply, while the ability to retaliate helps secure protection. China can push back. India risks repeated pressure unless it demonstrates that coercion carries a cost."

The waiver lands on an oil trade that has already been reshaping itself under the threat. Russia's share of India's crude imports fell from 51 per cent in July to 44 per cent in August, with purchases rotating toward the United States — despite higher prices — and the UAE. Whether that shift was commercial, diplomatic or anticipatory barely matters now; the direction is visible to Washington, and the waiver's implicit logic is that India has already begun to comply. The risk GTRI flags is that this becomes the negotiating template: pressure applied, behaviour adjusted, relief granted, threat retained.

For the ongoing India-US trade negotiations — where the first-phase deal sits in legal drafting after February's framework cut tariffs from 50 per cent to 18 per cent — the waiver cuts both ways. It removes an immediate 100 per cent landmine from the table, but it also demonstrates that the mine can be re-laid at any time, giving Washington a persistent source of leverage precisely when the hardest concessions are being discussed. Srivastava's advice to New Delhi is correspondingly hard-edged: retain flexibility in oil sourcing, refuse to convert reversible relief into permanent market access, and remember that the Act, the monitoring and the tariff authority all remain in force.

The energy-security arithmetic reinforces the warning. India's import bill is already swollen by elevated crude prices, and the month's trade data showed the oil bill as one of the deficits that refuse to shrink. Buying Russian crude at a discount has been one of the few reliable offsets; losing that option to an American legislative instrument — however temporarily suspended — would hand a foreign parliament permanent influence over India's energy mix. The waiver is good news for refiners this week. GTRI's point is that strategy cannot be built on a week. "Washington threatens penalties, then retreats when enforcing them becomes inconvenient," the think tank noted — which is exactly why the threat never really went away.

The episode also carries a wider lesson for how India reads American trade policy in the months ahead. The Graham Act's architecture — enormous headline tariffs, a presidential waiver valve, and standing monitoring by the USTR — is designed for episodic pressure rather than settled policy. Each waiver resets the clock without removing the machinery. For New Delhi's negotiators, the practical implication is that energy purchasing, tariff schedules and the pending trade deal are now a single interconnected file, where movement on one dimension can be — and evidently is being — used as leverage on the others. GTRI's prescription of diversification and demonstrated retaliation capacity is, in effect, a recommendation to unbundle them.

For now, refiners will buy, ships will load, and the 44 per cent Russian share of India's imports may drift further down regardless of the waiver's status. The discount arithmetic that made Russian crude attractive has narrowed as Moscow's pricing has hardened, and the US-UAE barrels arriving in the mix carry their own diplomatic discount. The trade data over the next two months will show whether the waiver merely ratified a rotation already underway — or whether it froze one, with the bill postponed rather than cancelled.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID TRUMP WAIVE?
Threatened tariffs of up to 100% on countries buying Russian oil, issued on October 16 under the Graham Act passed on September 18.
CAN THE WAIVER BE REVERSED?
Yes — it has no fixed expiry and can be withdrawn at any time; the underlying 500% tariff on Russian goods and 100% duty framework remains in force.
WHY DID THE US STEP BACK?
GTRI cites US purchases of Russian diesel since October 9, tariff protection for China until January 10, and the risk of derailing India trade talks.
HOW HAS INDIA'S RUSSIAN OIL BUYING CHANGED?
Russia's share of India's oil imports fell from 51% in July to 44% in August as purchases shifted to the US and UAE. GLOSSARY: - Graham Act: The Lindsey O. Graham Sanctioning Russia and Iran Act of 2026, authorising punitive tariffs against Russia and its oil buyers. - Section 115: The provision allowing the president to waive the Act's tariffs. - Offer-for-sale structure: How tariff threats function as leverage — present, enforceable, but not currently fired. - GTRI: Global Trade Research Initiative, a New Delhi-based trade policy think tank.
