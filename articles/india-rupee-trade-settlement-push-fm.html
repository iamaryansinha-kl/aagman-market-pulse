India Pushes Rupee Settlement for Currency-Starved Partners

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- India wants import payments from hard-currency-starved partners settled in rupees.
  • •- The FM framed it as a way to keep essential-goods trade flowing through balance-of-payments crises.
  • •- She cited wars, geopolitics and slow multilateral responses as the backdrop.
  • •- India is also pushing for more bilateral trade deals and reviewing the ASEAN pact.
  • •- The idea builds on RBI's 2022 rupee settlement framework and the Russia trade precedent.

On the same Bangkok stage where she spent the morning complaining about China's trade walls, Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman sketched the constructive counterpart of India's currency diplomacy: an active push to settle trade in rupees with countries that are running out of dollars. "One of the things that we in India want to do is to settle in rupee, whatever the payment is got to be done by those countries which import goods from us," she said at an Atlantic Council interaction on the sidelines of the IMF-World Bank annual meetings.

The rationale is practical rather than ideological. When countries lose access to hard currency — through wars, commodity shocks, debt spirals or the closure of global funding markets — trade does not stop being necessary; it stops being financeable. Essential imports queue up at central banks while the IMF process grinds through assessment programmes that, as Sitharaman pointedly noted, have historically taken three to four years to respond. "Rupee and local currency settlements are something which we want to do for the benefit of countries which are starved of hard currencies but yet need to import some goods," she said. "It is a method we have understood and are working on it."

The mechanism she describes already has scaffolding. The RBI issued its rupee trade settlement framework in July 2022, and India's oil trade with Russia since the Ukraine war has functioned as its largest live experiment — with instructive complications, from the rupee-rouble rate puzzle to the accumulation of rupee balances Russian exporters could not easily deploy. Those frictions explain why the idea has spread slowly; every bilateral arrangement must solve the question of what the surplus-rupee-holding partner does with the proceeds. India's pitch in Bangkok — that its own export goods are the answer — is an attempt to turn that weakness into the mechanism's selling point: hold rupees, spend them on Indian exports.

For India, the benefits run in both directions. Exporters gain a payment channel that keeps orders alive when counterparties' dollar access fails; the rupee gains incremental international usage and, with it, a thicker offshore ecosystem of deposits and trade credit; and New Delhi deepens commercial relationships with precisely the countries — in Africa, South Asia, the Middle East and Central Asia — where China's renminbi-denominated lending has been building influence for a decade. Currency internationalisation is, in the end, a market-share contest, and Sitharaman's remarks mark India's most explicit bid yet to compete in it.

The context she provided is the same multipolar trade map she has been drawing all week. Geopolitical tensions and wars have left many countries in a debt trap and balance-of-payments crisis, she said, and multilateral institutions have been too slow to help — which is why India is "pushing for more meaningful bilateral relationships" and why its trade-agreement calendar now spans the US, UK, EU, Canada and New Zealand simultaneously. She repeated that the ASEAN pact is "very skewed against India" and should continue in a "slightly refashioned manner," and defended the 2019 walkout from RCEP as a conscious choice by a country that had nothing to gain from sixteen-economy integration on those terms.

The honest caveat is scale. Rupee settlement remains a sliver of India's trade, and the rupee itself is not fully convertible, which caps how far the arrangement can go before foreign holders of Indian currency demand deeper capital markets in which to park it. The FM's framework acknowledges the limit without conceding it: for hard-currency-starved partners, even a partial, bilateral, goods-anchored rupee channel is better than none. The 2020s have taught emerging markets that the dollar plumbing can be switched off faster than multilateral help arrives; India's answer — build a second pipe, however modest — is a strategy, not a slogan, and Bangkok's audience heard it stated plainly for the first time.

The initiative also has a defensive dimension worth naming. Every rupee invoice settled is a dollar of demand that does not hit India's own external account at the margin — a consideration that resonates after a month in which the RBI has spent reserves defending the currency, mobilised non-resident deposits and tightened derivatives rules. The rupee internationalisation agenda and the reserve-management agenda are the same agenda viewed from opposite ends: one reduces the need for dollars at settlement, the other stockpiles them for when they are needed anyway. A country running the second strategy competently, as this week's balance-of-payments numbers suggest India is, earns the credibility to attempt the first.

Who the early partners might be is a question the FM did not answer, but the candidates are visible: neighbours with chronic dollar shortages, African economies priced out of hard-currency markets, and buyers of Indian essentials — food, medicine, refined fuel — whose trade finance has dried up. Each would bring small volumes at first. The significance of Friday's remarks is directional: India has decided that currency diplomacy is worth stating out loud, at the IMF's own podium, as a permanent feature of its trade statecraft rather than an improvisation for one sanctioned supplier.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID THE FM PROPOSE ON RUPEE SETTLEMENT?
India wants countries that import Indian goods but face hard-currency shortages to settle payments in rupees, so essential trade can continue through balance-of-payments crises.
WHY NOW?
Wars, geopolitics and debt traps have left partners short of dollars, and the FM noted multilateral responses have taken three to four years — too slow for countries needing to import essentials.
DOES A MECHANISM ALREADY EXIST?
Yes — the RBI's rupee trade settlement framework from July 2022, with India-Russia oil trade as the largest live example, though surplus-rupee usage remains a practical challenge.
WHAT ELSE DID SHE SAY ON TRADE POLICY?
She reiterated that the ASEAN agreement is skewed against India and should continue in refashioned form, and defended staying out of RCEP. GLOSSARY: - Hard currency: Widely accepted global currencies like the US dollar and euro, used for most international trade. - Balance-of-payments crisis: When a country cannot finance its external payments without emergency support. - Rupee trade settlement: Invoicing and paying for trade in Indian rupees instead of a third currency like the dollar. - RCEP: The Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership, a 16-economy Asian trade deal India exited in 2019.
