Yes Bank Falls 20% After SC Rules AT-1 Bond Write-Off Illegal

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The Supreme Court declared Yes Bank's write-off of Rs 8,415 crore of AT-1 bonds during its 2020 rescue illegal.
  • •- Shares plunged as much as 20% intraday and were down over 14% in afternoon trade.
  • •- The ruling went against the positions of the RBI and the central government, which had appealed.
  • •- The bonds comprised Rs 3,000 crore raised in 2016 and Rs 5,415 crore raised in 2017.
  • •- The precise balance-sheet impact will depend on the detailed judgment and its implementation.

Six years after Yes Bank's AT-1 bondholders watched Rs 8,415 crore of their investment permanently written down to zero, India's Supreme Court has ruled the write-off itself was illegal — and the bank's stock paid the price on Thursday, plunging as much as 20 per cent intraday as the market began pricing a liability that had been considered extinguished.

The ruling is a landmark in one of Indian banking's ugliest chapters. In March 2020, as Yes Bank buckled under bad loans and governance failures, the RBI placed it under moratorium and stitched together a reconstruction scheme in which State Bank of India and a consortium of other lenders injected fresh capital to keep the bank alive — a rescue remembered on Dalal Street as the moment India's fourth-largest private lender was saved from collapse by its rivals. As part of that scheme, the bank's Additional Tier-1 bonds — Rs 3,000 crore issued in December 2016 and another Rs 5,415 crore issued in October 2017 — were written down to zero. AT-1 bonds are perpetual instruments that pay higher coupons precisely because they carry loss-absorption triggers; investors can, in theory, lose everything.

Bondholders — individuals and institutions who argued they had been sold the instruments as near-fixed-income products — sued, contending the write-off was unlawful even though the bank itself had been saved. In January 2023, the Bombay High Court agreed, holding that the bank's administrator under the reconstruction scheme lacked the authority to extinguish the bonds, and noting pointedly that the final reconstruction scheme itself contained no explicit provision writing down AT-1 bonds. Yes Bank, the RBI and the central government all appealed; the Supreme Court stayed the High Court's order while it heard the case. On Thursday, the apex court let that stay lapse into a final verdict — the write-off was illegal, and the position taken by the regulator and the government lost.

The immediate market question is what the bank now owes. The detailed judgment and its implementation will determine whether Yes Bank faces repayment obligations on the bonds, in what form, and over what timeline — a sum of Rs 8,415 crore that, if it lands back on the balance sheet, would meaningfully alter the lender's capital arithmetic. That uncertainty is what the stock was pricing on Thursday: a fall of as much as 20 per cent at its worst, still down over 14 per cent in afternoon trade at Rs 18.21, even on a day when the benchmark indices rallied more than 1,500 points. A liability of that size, carried into the bank's common equity, would pressure regulatory capital ratios that the market had comfortably assumed were settled — which is why the damage to the share price, severe as it was, stayed within a band rather than turning into a full-scale reassessment of the bank's viability.

The systemic questions run deeper than one bank's stock. AT-1 bonds exist so that bank failure is absorbed by investors before depositors or taxpayers — a design the RBI and the government defended in court, arguing the instruments were governed by contractual terms permitting loss absorption when a bank approaches non-viability. If courts can unwind such write-offs years after the fact, the pricing and appetite for every Indian bank's AT-1 and tier-2 capital instruments will be reassessed, raising funding costs for the sector at the margin. Banks across the system have raised AT-1 capital for years on the assumption that the loss-absorption trigger, once pulled, was final; the Supreme Court has now shown it is reviewable. The ruling also, unusually, placed the judiciary's reading of a rescue scheme above the regulator's — a precedent bondholders elsewhere will study closely.

For Yes Bank specifically, the ruling arrives at an awkward moment in an otherwise improving story. The lender has spent the years since the rescue shrinking its bad-loan book, rebuilding deposits and returning to profitability under new management — a rehabilitation that had, until Thursday, largely buried the rescue-era liabilities in the market's memory. The judgment does not undo that operational progress, but it reopens a financial question from the rescue era that equity holders had assumed was closed, and bondholders — having won the principle after more than six years of litigation — will now move to quantify exactly what the bank owes them.

The human dimension of the case deserves its own accounting. A substantial share of the AT-1 paper had been sold to individual investors, many of whom later said the instruments were marketed to them as high-yield fixed-income products with no meaningful explanation of the loss-absorption clauses embedded in their terms. That mis-selling grievance — the bondholders argued in court that their investments were misrepresented — is what powered a legal fight that outlasted the bank's near-death experience, its reconstruction and its slow recovery, and it is why Thursday's verdict reads as much as a consumer-protection landmark as a banking one.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID THE SUPREME COURT RULE?
That Yes Bank's write-off of Rs 8,415 crore of AT-1 bonds during its 2020 reconstruction was illegal, upholding the Bombay High Court's 2023 decision.
WHY DID YES BANK SHARES CRASH?
The ruling potentially revives a Rs 8,415 crore liability on the bank's balance sheet; the stock fell as much as 20% intraday, down over 14% in afternoon trade.
WHO ELSE LOST IN THE RULING?
The RBI and the central government, which had appealed against the High Court verdict alongside the bank.
WHAT HAPPENS NEXT?
The detailed judgment and its implementation will determine repayment obligations; bondholders are expected to seek restitution. GLOSSARY: - AT-1 bonds: Perpetual debt instruments banks issue to bolster capital; they pay higher coupons but can be written off if a bank approaches non-viability. - Reconstruction scheme: An RBI-designed rescue plan, under which SBI and other lenders recapitalised Yes Bank in 2020. - Moratorium: A temporary suspension of a bank's operations imposed by the regulator to prevent a run. - Write-off: Cancelling a liability or asset — here, reducing bond principal to zero.
