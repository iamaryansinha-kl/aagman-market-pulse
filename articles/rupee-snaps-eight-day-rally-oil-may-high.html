Rupee Snaps Eight-Day Rally as Oil Hits Highest Since May

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The rupee closed down 0.4% at 94.8175 per dollar on Thursday, ending an eight-session winning streak.
  • •- Brent crude climbed 3.3% to $112.40 per barrel, its highest since May, on escalating Middle East conflict worries.
  • •- Early support from chunky Jio Platforms IPO inflows and RBI defensive measures faded as oil prices climbed.
  • •- MSCI's All-World index fell 0.4%, its largest daily decline in two weeks, as tech shares sold off across Asia and Europe.
  • •- A policy debate has opened over the RBI's defence of the currency: the IMF says let the exchange rate move, while a former RBI deputy governor warns that overshooting can threaten financial stability.

After eight consecutive sessions of gains — a streak built on trade-deal optimism, heavy IPO inflows and the Reserve Bank of India's defensive buying — the rupee finally met a force it could not shrug off. The currency swung from its strongest level in over six weeks during the day to close down 0.4% at 94.8175 per dollar on Thursday, snapping its longest winning run in recent months in a single volatile session. The culprit was crude: Brent climbed 3.3% to $112.40 per barrel, its highest since May, as worries about the Middle East conflict deepened and global markets turned risk-off.

The intraday path told the story. Early in the session, chunky foreign inflows from the Jio Platforms IPO added to the tailwinds from the RBI's recent dollar-purchase curbs, and the currency pushed to its strongest level in more than six weeks. Those gains evaporated in the latter half as oil prices climbed, with traders pointing to broad-based dollar demand locally alongside a wider retreat from risk across global markets. MSCI's All-World index fell 0.4%, its largest daily drop in two weeks, with tech shares under pressure from Seoul and Tokyo to Amsterdam and Paris, and equity futures pointing to a weak start on Wall Street. The synchronised nature of the sell-off mattered for the rupee: in risk-off episodes, emerging-market currencies tend to be sold as a bloc by global funds, and India's currency — despite its domestic flow strengths — trades with that pack until the forced selling exhausts itself, which is why the afternoon session unwound the morning's gains so quickly.

The oil risk premium is the hinge on which the rupee now swings, and it is being priced as durable. ING argued in a note that the sizeable risk premium embedded in crude prices is unlikely to fade until clear signs of de-escalation emerge and reassure investors that regional supply will remain uninterrupted. Oil flows from the Gulf have held up so far — the disruption is in the price rather than in the barrels — but the market is behaving as though that could change with one headline. For India, which imports nearly 90% of its crude requirements, this is not an abstract risk: it is the single largest external vulnerability for the currency, and the reason the rupee's October recovery has stalled precisely where oil re-entered the picture.

The episode has also sharpened an unusually public policy debate about how the RBI should respond. Krishna Srinivasan, a senior IMF official, said on Wednesday that India should allow the dollar-rupee exchange rate to move as the first line of defence — the standard Fund view that letting the currency absorb external shocks is cheaper than burning reserves to resist them. Michael Debabrata Patra, the former RBI deputy governor, offered the counterargument the same week: exchange rates tend to overshoot, he told Reuters, and overshooting can create financial stability risks of its own. It is a genuinely live question rather than an academic one: faster depreciation would help exporters and make Indian assets cheaper for foreign buyers, but it would also raise the local-currency cost of oil and widen the imported component of inflation just as the central bank is trying to stamp out price pressures. The two positions also map onto two different fears — the IMF worries about the fiscal and reserve cost of defending an indefensible level, while the former central banker worries about the corporate debt and balance-sheet damage that a disorderly slide could inflict on a country where many borrowers still carry unhedged foreign-currency exposure. That disagreement — flexibility versus stability — now sits at the centre of rupee policy, with the currency carrying a Middle East war premium, a mega-IPO bid providing temporary support, and a central bank that has already shown, through its dollar-purchase curbs, that it is willing to engineer the exchange rate rather than merely smooth it.

For now, the market's verdict is that the eight-day rally was borrowed time. The rupee's recovery from its recent lows was real — powered by trade optimism, IPO demand and RBI defence — but every leg of it depended on crude staying calm. With Brent back at May highs and the risk premium intact until there is visible de-escalation, the burden of proof has shifted: the currency now needs evidence that the oil shock is fading, rather than evidence that domestic flows are strong. Until that arrives, rallies to six-week highs will keep meeting sellers at the oil desk, and the debate between the IMF's flexibility camp and the RBI's stability camp will matter more to the exchange rate than any single day's flows. Traders will also be watching the IPO calendar itself: the Jio Platforms issue is providing genuine, temporary dollar demand, but subscription windows close, and once those inflows stop, the currency will be left alone with the oil bill again — which is precisely when the eight-day streak became a one-day reversal.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHY DID THE RUPEE FALL ON THURSDAY?
Rising oil prices were the main driver — Brent climbed to $112.40, its highest since May, reviving concerns about India's import bill. Broad dollar demand and a global risk-off mood added pressure.
WHAT ENDED THE RUPEE'S WINNING STREAK?
The currency had gained for eight straight sessions on trade-deal optimism, Jio Platforms IPO inflows and RBI measures. Thursday's oil spike overwhelmed those supports.
WHAT IS THE POLICY DEBATE OVER THE RUPEE?
The IMF's Krishna Srinivasan says India should let the exchange rate move as the first line of defence, while former RBI deputy governor Michael Patra warns that currency overshooting can create financial stability risks.
HOW DOES THE MIDDLE EAST CONFLICT AFFECT INDIA'S CURRENCY?
India imports nearly 90% of its crude. When conflict fears lift oil prices, India's import bill and dollar demand rise, putting direct pressure on the rupee. GLOSSARY: - Risk premium: The extra price buyers pay for an asset to compensate for perceived danger — here, the war markup in oil. - Dollar-purchase curbs: RBI restrictions designed to limit the build-up of dollar positions that pressure the rupee. - Overshooting: When an exchange rate moves beyond the level justified by fundamentals, typically during periods of stress.
