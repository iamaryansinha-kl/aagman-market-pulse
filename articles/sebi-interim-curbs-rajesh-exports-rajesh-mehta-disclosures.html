Sebi Confirms Curbs on Rajesh Exports Over Disclosure Gaps

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Sebi has confirmed interim directions against Rajesh Exports and promoter Rajesh Mehta, extending curbs first imposed in June.
  • •- Mehta remains restrained from buying, selling or dealing in the company's securities until further orders.
  • •- The regulator cited unresolved concerns over financial misrepresentation, incomplete disclosures and routing of company funds through personal accounts.
  • •- A forensic audit by BDO India covered April 2020 to March 2024 and found gaps in consolidated revenue disclosures.
  • •- Rajesh Exports argued confidentiality and Swiss law barred disclosure of some records; Sebi rejected the explanation.
  • •- The company's consolidated revenues were far higher than standalone figures of its Swiss subsidiary Valcambi.

The Securities and Exchange Board of India on Tuesday hardened its stand against one of India's largest gold refiners, confirming and continuing interim restrictions on Rajesh Exports and its promoter Rajesh Mehta in a case that stretches from trade receivables to Swiss gold refineries — and that the regulator says the company has failed to convincingly answer.

In a confirmatory order dated October 14, Sebi said Mehta will continue to be restrained from buying, selling or otherwise dealing in the securities of Rajesh Exports, directly or indirectly, until further orders. The company itself has been directed to continue making true, fair, accurate and complete disclosures of its financial statements, related-party transactions and other material information under securities laws.

The order extends directions first issued on June 3 this year. The examination began with a complaint alleging potential financial misrepresentation in the company's books, particularly large trade receivables that had remained outstanding for more than two years. Sebi's investigation covered the period from April 1, 2020 to March 31, 2024, and BDO India Services was appointed as forensic auditor in December 2024.

Rajesh Exports is a listed gold refiner and gold products manufacturer that also sells in India through wholesale and retail channels, including the Shubh Jewellers brand. Its group structure spans overseas subsidiaries and step-down entities, notably Global Gold Refineries AG and Valcambi SA in Switzerland — and it is the revenue of those foreign entities that sits at the heart of the dispute.

The regulator's core finding concerns consolidated revenue disclosures. Sebi said Rajesh Exports disclosed substantially higher consolidated revenues by including amounts attributed to overseas entities, but did not provide the party-wise break-ups, sales registers, purchase registers, invoices or purchase orders needed to verify them. A large gap exists, the order noted, between the standalone revenue of Valcambi SA and the much higher revenue reflected at the consolidated levels of Global Gold Refineries and Rajesh Exports. The company argued that Valcambi recognised only value addition or processing charges as revenue while GGR booked the gross value of gold transactions plus processing charges; Sebi said the explanation did not fully reconcile the difference with supporting records.

The company's defence leaned heavily on confidentiality. Rajesh Exports cited confidentiality obligations, data-protection requirements and Swiss law restrictions for not furnishing certain information. Sebi rejected that position outright, stating that private confidentiality arrangements cannot be used to bypass regulatory obligations — and that the material on record did not show the company had taken all legally permissible steps to provide what the regulator sought.

The order also flagged the routing of company funds through personal accounts, possible misrepresentation, incomplete disclosures and non-cooperation with the investigation. Sebi said the noticees had not satisfactorily addressed these concerns even after written submissions, a personal hearing and additional documents. Notably, the regulator framed the scope of this round narrowly: whether the earlier prima facie findings had been rebutted, and whether the interim directions should be modified, revoked or confirmed. Its answer was confirmation.

The company had not furnished information in a complete and usable form to enable independent verification of financial data covering consolidated operations, underlying accounting records and details relating to foreign subsidiaries, Sebi said. The direction to maintain accurate disclosures therefore stays in force alongside the trading restraint on the promoter.

For investors, the case is a reminder of how difficult consolidated numbers can be to police when group structures cross jurisdictions. Gold refining generates enormous gross revenue flows — metal in, metal out — and accounting choices about gross versus net recognition can swing reported topline by orders of magnitude. Sebi's insistence on registers, invoices and party-wise detail is effectively a demand that the numbers be auditable to Indian standards, regardless of where the subsidiary sits.

The group's structure is what makes the dispute consequential. Valcambi, the Swiss subsidiary at the centre of the revenue-gap question, is among the world's largest gold refiners, and the Shubh Jewellers retail chain gives the group a visible consumer presence in India. A restatement of consolidated revenue — if Sebi's findings hold through the final order — would ripple through every valuation metric investors use for the stock, from price-to-sales to the debt ratios that lenders watch.

The stakes are therefore larger than a promoter trading ban. A final adverse order could bring monetary penalties and, depending on the findings, more severe directions against the company's access to capital markets. Equally, a full rebuttal would require the records Sebi says have not been produced — which is why the regulator's framing of confidentiality as no defence matters for every multinational group structure listed in India.

What happens next depends on the final order. The confirmatory stage preserves the status quo while Sebi completes its examination; the restraint on Mehta and the disclosure directions remain until then. For Rajesh Exports, whose stock has been under pressure through the investigation, the path back runs through documentation — the very records the regulator says have not been produced.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID SEBI ORDER?
It confirmed interim directions against Rajesh Exports and promoter Rajesh Mehta, continuing the bar on Mehta dealing in the company's securities and directing complete, accurate disclosures.
WHAT ARE THE ALLEGATIONS?
Financial misrepresentation, incomplete disclosures, non-cooperation with the investigation, routing of company funds through personal accounts, and large unexplained trade receivables.
WHAT IS THE REVENUE DISPUTE ABOUT?
Consolidated revenues were far higher than the standalone revenue of Swiss subsidiary Valcambi SA; the company says Valcambi booked only processing fees while GGR booked gross gold value, but Sebi says records do not reconcile the gap.
WHY DID THE COMPANY NOT SHARE RECORDS?
It cited confidentiality, data protection and Swiss law restrictions; Sebi ruled private confidentiality agreements cannot override regulatory obligations. GLOSSARY: - Confirmatory order: A regulator's decision to continue interim directions while a full investigation proceeds. - Interim directions: Temporary restrictions imposed before a final finding of wrongdoing. - Forensic audit: An examination of financial records specifically to detect fraud or misrepresentation. - Consolidated revenue: Combined revenue of a parent company and all its subsidiaries. - Related-party transaction: A deal between a company and its promoters, directors or their entities.
