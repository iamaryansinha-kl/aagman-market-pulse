Goyal Pushes State Outreach to Fix FTA Utilisation Problem

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said the ministry is organising FTA outreach initiatives with state governments.
  • •- The goal is greater utilisation of India's free trade agreements by farmers, workers, MSMEs and small businesses.
  • •- India has implemented FTAs with the UAE, Mauritius, Australia, Oman, the EFTA bloc, the UK and New Zealand in five years.
  • •- Export utilisation has lagged: India's exports to EFTA fell 11% in FY26 despite the pact offering near-total market access.
  • •- Rules-of-origin compliance and awareness remain the binding constraints, experts say.

New Delhi has started treating its free trade agreements less like diplomatic trophies and more like products that need distribution. Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal said on Wednesday that his ministry is organising a series of FTA outreach initiatives across the country, in collaboration with state governments, to ensure the pacts India has signed actually get used. The focus, he said in a social media post, is making sure the benefits reach "our farmers, workers, women, youth, MSMEs and small businesses," enabling them to take more Made in India products to global markets, with more such initiatives to follow.

The framing is a quiet shift. Until now, the government's FTA messaging has centred on what was negotiated — tariff lines conceded and won, market access percentages, investment pledges — with utilisation left to exporters to discover. Wednesday's announcement moves the locus to the states, where export production actually happens, and to the MSME tier, where awareness of preferential schemes has historically been thinnest. It is also a reputational hedge: with Vietnam signing its own EFTA pact this week and India's exports to the bloc falling through the first year of the deal, the utilisation gap is now being discussed openly, and the Centre needs to be seen acting on it.

The inventory he is trying to move is substantial. In the last five years India has implemented free trade agreements with the UAE, Mauritius, Australia, Oman, the four-nation EFTA bloc, the United Kingdom and New Zealand — the densest FTA expansion in the country's trading history. Each was negotiated with the promise of preferential access for Indian exports; cumulatively they cover a large share of India's goods trade. Yet utilisation has consistently lagged the headline tariff arithmetic. The freshest example landed this week: India's exports to EFTA fell nearly 11% in 2025-26 and over 9% in the January-July 2026 stretch, even though the bloc opened 92.2% of its tariff lines covering 99.6% of India's exports when the pact took effect in October 2025. Access exists; shipments do not follow automatically.

The gap has a known anatomy, and the ministry's state-level turn targets it directly. Preferential tariffs under an FTA are not automatic — exporters must claim them, and goods must satisfy rules-of-origin criteria that require documentation many small firms cannot produce without help. States, not the Centre, hold most of the levers that determine whether a local exporter can comply: export infrastructure, product-certification capacity, trade-finance access and the district-level handholding that turns a negotiated tariff line into an actual shipment. India's export basket is also disproportionately state-specific — textiles from Tamil Nadu, engineering goods from Maharashtra and Gujarat, seafood from coastal states, agricultural products from Punjab and Andhra Pradesh — so a national FTA strategy that never reaches the state level is structurally guaranteed to underperform. Development economist Biswajit Dhar has argued that Indian exporters have struggled to use the market access opportunities the EFTA pact created; the outreach push is, in effect, the government's acknowledgment that he is right.

The comparison that haunts the initiative is not rhetorical. Vietnam signed its EFTA agreement this week after two decades of systematically building exporter-compliance capability; its firms claim preferences as a matter of routine, and its EU-preference machinery has been running since 2020. India's challenge is to compress that learning curve: the state-collaboration model, if it genuinely embeds FTA desks in state export promotion machinery rather than running one-off seminars, could shorten the glide path from signature to shipment; Goyal's phrasing about "every state unlocking its unique export potential" points in that direction. The proof, as with the pacts themselves, will be in the export data rather than the press releases.

Whether awareness is the binding constraint is the open question. The critique from trade economists is that paperwork programmes treat the symptom while the disease is competitiveness: logistics costs, product quality, and delivery reliability determine who wins shared markets, and a seminar on rules of origin cannot fix a container terminal. The counter is empirical — Vietnam, whose EFTA deal was signed this week, has spent two decades building exactly this exporter-compliance machinery, and its preference-utilisation rates under existing pacts are the visible result. If India's outreach produces state-level export cells that help even a fraction of its MSMEs claim the preferences already negotiated, the return on what is a modest administrative investment could be outsized; if it remains a series of events, the EFTA export trajectory will keep doing what it has done for a year.

There is also a strategic clock underneath. India's concluded EU agreement — the largest prize in the stack, with $33 billion of labour-intensive exports facing 4% to 26% duties that would go to zero — is awaiting signature authorisation in Brussels. When it takes effect, Indian exporters will be competing for European shelf space against Vietnamese rivals who already know how to use their preferences. The state outreach announced on Wednesday is, read uncharitably, an admission that India has been slow; read charitably, it is the distribution build-out that turns a decade of trade diplomacy into export revenue. The export data over the next four quarters will say which reading was fair.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID Piyush Goyal ANNOUNCE?
A series of FTA outreach initiatives by the Department of Commerce, in collaboration with state governments, to help exporters use India's free trade agreements.
WHICH FTAS HAS INDIA IMPLEMENTED IN THE LAST FIVE YEARS?
Agreements with the UAE, Mauritius, Australia, Oman, the EFTA bloc, the UK and New Zealand.
WHY ARE THE OUTREACH PROGRAMMES NEEDED?
FTA tariff preferences are not automatic: exporters must claim them and meet rules-of-origin requirements, and many small firms lack the compliance capacity to do so.
HOW HAS UTILISATION PERFORMED SO FAR?
Patchy — India's exports to EFTA fell nearly 11% in FY26 despite the pact offering access on 92.2% of the bloc's tariff lines. GLOSSARY: - FTA utilisation: The share of eligible trade that actually claims preferential tariffs under a free trade agreement. - Rules of origin: Criteria that determine where a product was made, required to claim FTA preferences. - MSME: Micro, small and medium enterprises. - Made in India: The government's domestic manufacturing and export promotion branding.
