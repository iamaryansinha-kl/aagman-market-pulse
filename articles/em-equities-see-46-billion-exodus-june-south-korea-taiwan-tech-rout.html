EM Equities See $46 Billion Exodus in June as South Korea, Taiwan Tech Rout

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • Key points unavailable

Emerging markets are bleeding capital at an alarming rate.

Equity investors pulled a staggering $46 billion from emerging market funds in June, driven by a brutal rout in South Korea and Taiwan technology stocks that has shaken confidence in the AI-driven semiconductor boom. The outflows represent one of the largest monthly withdrawals from EM funds on record, and the selloff has spilled over into broader emerging market indices, affecting even countries like India and Brazil that have little direct exposure to the semiconductor cycle.

The South Korea and Taiwan rout is the primary driver. The Kospi has crashed 25% from its June peak, entering bear market territory, while Taiwan's Taiex has also suffered heavy losses as semiconductor stocks — which dominate both indices — have been hammered by concerns that AI infrastructure spending is running ahead of actual demand. SK Hynix, Samsung Electronics, and Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company have all seen their shares tumble.

The $46 billion figure is extraordinary. Monthly EM equity flows are typically in the range of $5-15 billion in either direction. A $46 billion outflow suggests a panic-driven rotation, not a gradual adjustment. Foreign portfolio investors are not just trimming positions; they are fleeing en masse, liquidating holdings across the EM spectrum to raise cash or rotate back to developed markets.

India has been relatively resilient but not immune. While the Nifty has held near record levels, foreign investors have been net sellers in Indian equities in recent sessions, contributing to the pressure. The Indian market's resilience is partly due to strong domestic institutional buying — mutual funds and insurance companies have been absorbing the foreign selling — but if the outflows continue, even domestic support may not be enough to prevent a correction.

The contagion is affecting currency markets. The South Korean won and Taiwanese dollar have both weakened significantly as capital outflows accelerate. The Indian rupee, which had been stable, has come under pressure as part of the broader EM risk-off sentiment. Central banks in EM countries are having to choose between defending their currencies (which drains foreign exchange reserves) or allowing depreciation (which fuels inflation).

The key question is whether this is a temporary correction or the start of a sustained trend. If AI demand proves to be as strong as the bulls expect, the semiconductor rout could reverse quickly, and the $46 billion could flow back just as fast. But if the AI narrative is truly broken — if hyperscalers cut their capital expenditure plans and chip inventory builds up — the EM exodus could be the first wave of a much larger correction in global equity markets.
