FIIs Dump Indian Stocks for 8 Straight Weeks; DIIs Buy Rs 30,313 Crore

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/Foreign institutional investors sold Indian equities worth Rs 30,294 crore in the week ended October 10, extending their selling streak to eight straight weeks.
  • 02/Domestic institutional investors bought Rs 30,313 crore in the same week, absorbing the foreign outflows almost to the crore and cushioning the Nifty 50.
  • 03/Watch US bond yields, Brent crude above $100, US-Iran tensions and US and Indian inflation prints for signs of a reversal.

Foreign institutional investors have now sold Indian stocks for eight consecutive weeks. In the latest trading week, FIIs offloaded shares worth Rs 30,294 crore, according to market data.

The domestic bid answered almost to the crore. Domestic institutional investors bought equities worth Rs 30,313 crore during the same week, absorbing the foreign selling and helping the benchmark recover from its weekly low.

The Nifty 50 closed at 22,520.45 on Friday, gaining 0.4% over the week. The index had touched a fresh 52-week low of 22,180 on Thursday, after climbing to a weekly high of 22,776 on Tuesday.

October has followed the same pattern. FIIs have sold a net Rs 39,779 crore so far this month, while DIIs have purchased Rs 40,355 crore. Despite the domestic buying, the Nifty remains 0.44% below its September-end close of 22,620.45.

The eight-week streak has cost the benchmark dearly. "Over the past eight weeks, FIIs were net sellers throughout while DIIs remained consistent net buyers, limiting the Nifty's 7.57% slide from 24,366.00 to 22,520.45," said Pabitro Mukherjee, Deputy Vice President-Research at Bajaj Broking.

Mukherjee said persistent foreign selling and global headwinds are likely to keep markets volatile. However, record domestic institutional buying should continue to limit the downside.

The market mood during the week was defined by a standoff. "The market tone remained watchful throughout, driven by a persistent tug-of-war between relentless foreign fund outflows and strong domestic counter-buying," said Ravi Singh, Chief Research Officer at Master Capital Services.

The backdrop offered little comfort. Brent crude remained above $100 a barrel, US bond yields moved higher before easing, and the rupee weakened further. The Reserve Bank of India raised the repo rate by 25 basis points, shifting its stance to calibrated tightening.

Singh said market participants interpreted the RBI's move as a measure to anchor inflation and defend the rupee against foreign capital outflows.

There was some relief on the global front. The US 10-year Treasury yield eased from multi-month highs near 5.36% to 5.24%. "This mitigation in global yields eased financial conditions and stemmed immediate capital flight from emerging markets," Singh said.

Singh also flagged a shift in global risk appetite. The hyper-growth narrative around artificial intelligence is showing signs of slowing, and moderation in speculative AI positioning has encouraged international capital to reallocate towards fundamental value plays and broader market exposures.

On the charts, the picture is mixed. The index rebounded 0.44% during the week after eight consecutive weekly losses, finding support near its trendline and the 100-week exponential moving average. "This recovery from oversold territory offers some relief and raises the possibility of a short-term technical rebound," Singh said.

Sectorally, IT, banking and financial services led the weekly recovery. Energy remained muted amid volatile crude oil prices.

So, is a reversal on the cards, or is there more pain ahead? The honest answer in the data: watch the triggers. Mukherjee said investors will closely monitor US and Indian inflation data, US bond yields, Brent crude prices and US-Iran geopolitical tensions in the coming week. The domestic bid has held the floor for eight weeks. Whether it can keep doing so depends largely on forces beyond Dalal Street.

The domestic bid has been the market's load-bearing wall. Mutual funds, insurance companies and other institutions bought Rs 40,355 crore in October alone — more than matching the foreign exit rupee for rupee.

That mirror-buying is why the decline looks orderly rather than disorderly. Foreign selling of this intensity in an earlier era would have produced far deeper drawdowns; the DII channel has structurally changed how Indian equities absorb outflows.

The forces driving foreign sellers are mostly outside India's control. Brent above $100 a barrel, a rupee near record lows and elevated US bond yields all raise the opportunity cost of holding emerging-market equities — and all three were present during the week.

The RBI's policy shift added a domestic complication. The 25-basis-point repo rate increase and the move to calibrated tightening are meant to defend the currency and anchor inflation — but higher rates also change the equity-versus-debt arithmetic for investors.

There was one genuine relief valve. The US 10-year Treasury yield eased from multi-month highs near 5.36% to 5.24% — enough, per Ravi Singh, to ease financial conditions and stem immediate capital flight from emerging markets.

The AI trade is a subtler variable. Singh argued that as the hyper-growth artificial-intelligence narrative cools, speculative positioning unwinds and global capital rotates toward fundamental value — a rotation that historically favours markets like India on valuation grounds.

The technical picture frames the week ahead. Holding the 22,150-22,200 zone keeps the rebound scenario alive toward 22,750 and then 23,000; losing it puts the 52-week low of 22,180 back in play.

Sector rotation already shows the market's internal debate. IT, banking and financial services led the weekly recovery — rate-sensitive and export-tilted sectors — while energy lagged as crude gyrated above $100.

The honest summary is that nobody can date the reversal. What the flow data establishes is narrower: domestic institutions have absorbed every rupee foreign investors sold for eight weeks, and the market still stands 7.57% lower — an anchor that has held, not a tide that has turned.

GLOSSARY: FII (foreign institutional investor): An overseas fund or institution investing in Indian securities; their flows are tracked as a sentiment gauge. DII (domestic institutional investor): Indian institutions — mainly mutual funds and insurers — that invest in domestic equities. Net buy/sell: The difference between an investor class's total purchases and total sales over a period. Exponential moving average (EMA): A price average weighting recent data more heavily, used to identify trend direction. Repo rate: The rate at which the RBI lends to banks; its level influences funding costs across the economy. Drawdown: The decline in an asset's value from its peak to its current or subsequent trough.
