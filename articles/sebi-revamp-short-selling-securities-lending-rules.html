Sebi to Revamp Short-Selling, Securities Lending Rules

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Sebi will overhaul its short-selling and securities lending and borrowing framework, with changes aimed at improving price discovery and tightening the link between cash and derivatives markets.
  • •- The revamp builds on the 2024 framework that banned naked short selling, forced institutions to settle short trades on a gross basis, and required retail investors to disclose short positions by end of day.
  • •- The timing is pointed: foreign investors are heavily short index futures after ten weeks of selling, and the regulator is managing a derivatives market it has already spent two years shrinking.
  • •- Securities lending — the borrow market that makes covered short selling possible — has long been criticised for thin volumes and poor pricing, and is the framework's primary repair target.

Every market regulator eventually confronts the same uncomfortable truth: you cannot have honest price discovery without short sellers, and you cannot have short sellers without a functioning market for borrowed stock. Sebi has decided it is time to fix the borrowing side. The regulator said it will revamp its short-selling and securities lending rules — an overhaul aimed squarely at improving price discovery and strengthening the connection between India's cash and derivatives markets.

The existing architecture dates to a framework Sebi assembled through 2023 and 2024, and its bones are well known to anyone trading Indian equities. Naked short selling — selling stock you have not borrowed and cannot deliver — is banned outright, a rule with no exceptions for retail or institutional investors. Institutions shorting in the cash market must disclose the position upfront and settle on a gross basis at the custodian level, which means no squaring off intra-day to dodge delivery obligations. Retail investors may disclose by end of trading day, and exchanges must publish aggregated short-sell data weekly. The framework was designed for transparency: short selling is permitted, even encouraged, but only in a form where every short can, in principle, be delivered. That last qualifier — in principle — is where the revamp will do its work, because a short that cannot find borrowed stock at a reasonable price is a short that does not happen, whatever the rulebook says.

What has never worked properly is the plumbing underneath it. The Securities Lending and Borrowing scheme — the mechanism through which traders borrow shares to deliver against short sales — has run with anaemic volumes and rigid tenures relative to the size of the cash market it serves. Borrow costs are often uneconomic precisely when demand to short is highest, which pushes would-be short sellers into the derivatives market instead. That migration matters more than it seems: when expressing a negative view requires buying a put or selling a futures contract rather than shorting the cash share, the price signal lands in the derivative, not in the underlying stock. Cash-market price discovery — the thing short selling exists to provide — goes missing. Sebi's stated goals of better price discovery and a stronger cash-derivatives link are, in effect, an admission that the current borrow market fails at exactly the moments it matters most.

The timing gives the overhaul an edge it would not have had in a calm market. Foreign institutional investors have now sold Indian equities for ten consecutive weeks, their index-futures positioning is heavily short, and every rally attempt has been sold into — the backdrop against which "short selling" becomes politically radioactive even when it is doing the economically useful work of price correction. A regulator that tightens the framework now, while acknowledging its legitimacy, is choosing the harder and more principled path: fix the plumbing, keep the practice. Sebi's own recent history points the same direction — the phased equity derivatives rules that shrank weekly expiries and raised contract sizes were about curbing speculative excess, not abolishing downside expression.

The practical questions the revamp must answer are well known to market participants. Can lending tenures be made flexible enough that lenders actually participate? Can borrow fees be discovered on a proper order-matching platform rather than negotiated in a telephone market? Can institutions — the natural lenders of stock, since they hold it anyway — be given cleaner incentives to lend through the exchange mechanism rather than the opaque OTC channel? And can the cash and futures prices be bound more tightly so that the arbitrage which keeps both honest does not leak into one-sided derivatives positioning? The consultation process will also have to reckon with lenders' own conservatism: mutual funds and insurers hold the vast pool of lendable stock but have historically treated lending programmes as an afterthought, and any framework that does not address their operational constraints will discover that mandates on paper do not create supply. Whatever Sebi proposes, the consultation paper will be read against those four tests.

There is a governance subtext, too. A borrow market that only works in good times concentrates short-side expression among the largest, best-connected institutions — precisely the participants a transparency-first regulator watches most closely. Deepening the SLB pool democratises the short side: it lets smaller funds, proprietary desks and ultimately better-informed cash-market pricing do the correcting, rather than leaving the job to concentrated futures positioning that shows up only in weekly data. Sebi's chairmanship has spent its tenure arguing that Indian markets should be more ownership-oriented and less casino; a short-selling framework that prices bearish views in the underlying stock, visibly and deliverably, is more consistent with that philosophy than the current arrangement, which arguably outsources the downside to the derivatives complex and then has to regulate the complex ever harder. For a market that has spent the autumn falling on heavy foreign supply, the arrival of a better-functioning short side is not a threat. It is the mechanism by which prices eventually stop being wrong.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT IS SEBI REVAMPING?
The short-selling framework and the Securities Lending and Borrowing scheme — the rules governing how investors bet on falling prices and borrow the shares needed to settle those trades.
WHAT ARE THE CURRENT SHORT-SELLING RULES?
Naked short selling is banned; institutions must disclose upfront and settle gross with custodians (no intra-day square-off); retail investors disclose by end of day; exchanges publish weekly short-sell data.
WHY NOW?
Sebi wants to fix poor price discovery in the borrow market and strengthen the cash-derivatives link — and the overhaul lands while FII positioning is heavily short after ten weeks of outflows, making a credible, well-plumbed short framework more important than ever. GLOSSARY: SHORT SELLING — Selling borrowed shares in the expectation of buying them back cheaper; a legitimate source of downward price pressure and liquidity. NAKED SHORT SELLING — Selling shares without having borrowed them, creating delivery risk; banned in India. SECURITIES LENDING AND BORROWING — The exchange-traded mechanism through which shares are lent against fees so short sellers can meet delivery obligations. GROSS SETTLEMENT — Requiring each trade to settle individually, preventing institutions from netting a short sale against an offsetting buy within the day.
