Dr Doom Goes Bullish on India, Sees a China-Style Rise

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Roubini called India "a major success story with the potential to become an even bigger one."
  • •- He said the next few decades could be about India as the past ones were about China.
  • •- He expects US tariffs to fall further, saying "markets will force the US to act constructively."
  • •- He warned that 7% growth is not enough; India needs to get closer to 9% for job creation.
  • •- He sees friendshoring benefiting India as China is "increasingly considered risky."
  • •- Indian electronics exports to China rose nearly 40% in the five months through August.

Nouriel Roubini built his reputation on being right when everyone else was wrong. In 2006, at an IMF podium while Wall Street was still euphoric, the economist now known as "Dr Doom" laid out the housing bust, the collapse of mortgage-backed securities and the banking crisis that would arrive two years later, almost exactly as he described it. His framework then was to treat the US financial system not as an invincible superpower but as a troubled emerging market with a massive current-account deficit. So when Roubini says he now sees a major positive shift taking shape, the words carry more weight than they would from any other permabear — and the shift he sees is India.

"India is a major success story with the potential to become an even bigger one," Roubini said in an interview with ET, going considerably further than the polite praise foreign economists usually offer: the next few decades, in his view, could be about India as much as the past several decades were about China. The ingredients he lists are the ones Indian policymakers repeat among themselves — a large domestic market, a deep talent pool, sound macroeconomic fundamentals, an openness to the world — but Roubini adds a numerical ambition they rarely voice in public: with further reforms, he believes India's potential growth could exceed 8 per cent.

His jobs argument is the harder edge of the optimism. "Growth of 7% is not enough. India needs to get closer to 9% to ensure that young people can secure good jobs," he said — a striking demand from an economist praising the world's fastest-growing major economy, and one that reframes the entire policy debate. The arithmetic is demographic: millions of young Indians enter the workforce every year, and manufacturing — electronics, automobiles, textiles and their supplier networks — is the sector that can absorb them at scale, in a way that technology and professional services, for all their glamour, structurally cannot. The AI transition sharpens the urgency: as automation reshapes both manual and cognitive work, population alone stops being an economic advantage and becomes an employment obligation.

On trade, Roubini offered the prediction India's negotiators most want to hear. "Markets will force the US to adopt a more constructive approach, including towards India," he said, expecting American tariffs to fall further as inflation and affordability pressures bite in Washington. "India is a huge and rising economy and is aligned with many of the objectives of the US and the West... Rationality will prevail." The commercial logic he cites is already visible: India's exports to the US rose to $42.79 billion between April and August from $40.39 billion a year earlier, and the first phase of the bilateral trade deal — built on the February framework that cut US tariffs on most Indian goods from 50 per cent to 18 per cent — is in legal drafting. India's ask now is preferential access to compete with suppliers from Vietnam, Bangladesh and Indonesia.

The China comparison is where Roubini parts company with the cautious consensus. "Geopolitics benefits India because China is increasingly considered risky," he said, citing Beijing's demographic decline, excessive leverage and real-estate troubles as forces pushing manufacturers toward friendshoring — and noting that India is less exposed to external shocks given the scale of its domestic market. The most interesting data point in his case is one that inverts the usual narrative: Indian electronics exports to China rose almost 40 per cent in the five months through August, suggesting Indian factories are beginning to supply components into Chinese supply chains, not merely compete with them.

The sceptic's note, which a career built on crisis-warning warrants, is that none of this is automatic. China's rise consumed three decades of deliberate policy; India's opportunity window is real but contingent on reforms — labour, land, logistics, energy — that have been promised longer than many of the young workers Roubini is worried about have been alive. But that is precisely his point: the positioning is done, the global backdrop is turning favourable, and the next three decades are the test. Coming from the man who saw 2008 coming, "India's decade" is a forecast rather than a compliment — and, for once, Dr Doom is smiling.

The interview lands amid a noisy week for India economic commentary: a GDP forecast from Deloitte placing FY27 growth at 7.1-7.3 per cent, the finance minister's trade offensive in Bangkok, and a market that has spent the month repricing everything from the rupee to mid-cap banks. Roubini's contribution is less the specific numbers than the framing — he is, characteristically, arguing from structure rather than sentiment. Demographics, deleveraging China, friendshoring, and a services-to-manufacturing transition are slow variables; betting against them because of one quarter's noise is, in his framework, the mistake. It is a framework that has been right before.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID ROUBINI SAY ABOUT INDIA'S GROWTH?
He called India a major success story that could exceed 8% potential growth with reforms, and said the next few decades could be about India as the past ones were about China.
WHAT IS HIS JOBS ARGUMENT?
7% growth is not enough — India needs growth closer to 9% to create enough good jobs for its young workforce, especially as AI disrupts both manual and cognitive work.
WHAT DOES HE EXPECT ON US TARIFFS?
He expects tariffs to fall further, saying markets and inflation pressures will force Washington toward a constructive approach on India.
WHY DOES HE FAVOUR INDIA OVER CHINA?
Friendshoring, China's demographic and leverage problems, and India's large domestic market; Indian electronics exports to China rose nearly 40% in five months. GLOSSARY: - Friendshoring: Shifting supply chains to geopolitically aligned countries rather than the lowest-cost producer. - Potential growth: The maximum rate an economy can sustain without generating inflation, given its resources and productivity. - Current-account deficit: When a country imports more goods, services and income than it exports — the vulnerability Roubini flagged in the US in 2006. - Legal drafting: The final stage where an agreed trade deal's text is converted into binding legal language.
