India-China Trade Deficit Crosses $100 Billion in Nine Months

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- India's trade deficit with China reached $105.93 billion in the first nine months of 2026, up 20.7% from a year earlier.
  • •- Total bilateral trade rose 25.4% to $144.45 billion between January and September.
  • •- Chinese exports to India touched $125.19 billion, up 23.3%, driven by electronics, machinery and chemicals.
  • •- Indian exports to China jumped 40.7% to $19.26 billion, led by ores, fuels, seafood and agricultural items.
  • •- The deficit hit an all-time high of $112.6 billion in 2025-26 versus $99.2 billion in 2024-25.
  • •- India has repeatedly pressed China to open its markets for IT, pharma and farm produce with little success.

India's trade deficit with China crossed the $100 billion mark in the first nine months of 2026, official Chinese customs data showed on Wednesday, underscoring how deeply the world's two most populous economies remain intertwined even as New Delhi works to reduce its dependence on Beijing.

The deficit widened to $105.93 billion between January and September, up 20.7 per cent compared with the same period last year, according to data released by China's General Administration of Customs. Total bilateral trade expanded by 25.4 per cent to $144.45 billion, one of the fastest growth rates among China's major trading partners.

The composition of that trade explains both the scale of the problem and why it is so hard to fix. Chinese exports to India touched $125.19 billion, a 23.3 per cent increase, and are dominated by electronics, machinery and chemicals — the upstream inputs that feed India's manufacturing base, from smartphone assembly to solar module production and pharmaceutical intermediates. India's exports to China, by contrast, registered a 40.7 per cent jump to $19.26 billion, but the basket is largely primary and resource products: ores, fuels, seafood and agricultural items.

In other words, China sells India the sophisticated components its factories need; India sells China raw materials. The faster India's manufacturing and electronics sectors grow, the more it tends to import from China — a structural dynamic that tariff walls and local-content rules have slowed at the margin but not reversed.

The trajectory is steep even on India's own accounting. According to India's Commerce Ministry, bilateral trade reached $151.1 billion in the last financial year, with Indian exports rising 36.66 per cent to $19.47 billion and imports increasing 16 per cent to $131.63 billion. The trade deficit swelled to an all-time high of $112.6 billion in 2025-26, against $99.2 billion in 2024-25. If the January-September run rate holds through December, the calendar-year deficit will comfortably exceed last year's record.

The gap has become a persistent irritant in the relationship. For years, India has demanded that China open its markets in information technology, pharmaceuticals and agricultural produce — sectors where India is globally competitive — but with little success. Market-access negotiations have repeatedly stalled, and Chinese regulatory approvals for Indian pharma and IT services remain slow and unpredictable.

There are, however, two threads pulling in the opposite direction. First, Indian exports to China are growing faster than imports in percentage terms — 40.7 per cent versus 23.3 per cent in the nine-month period — even if from a small base, suggesting some diversification of the export basket is beginning. Second, the diplomatic temperature has cooled: Commerce Minister Piyush Goyal said this week that the two countries are working to develop relations once again, remarks that came alongside quiet progress on restoring direct air links and easing visa restrictions.

The strategic overlay complicates any simple reading. India's push for supply-chain resilience — the "China plus one" diversification into electronics assembly, defence manufacturing and clean energy — has ironically increased imports of Chinese components in the near term, since India's new factories often still source parts from the very country they are meant to hedge against. Policymakers have responded with production-linked incentives, customs scrutiny of Chinese imports and quality-control orders, but each measure trades short-term cost against long-term capability.

Some context keeps the number in proportion. India's goods deficit with China is partly offset by a services surplus with the world — software, business services and remittances — which cushions the current account even as the goods side deteriorates. But the Chinese gap is qualitatively different from deficits with oil producers: it is concentrated in technology supply chains where switching costs are high and alternatives are thin, which is precisely why it draws strategic rather than merely economic attention.

The export story, meanwhile, is not nothing. Forty-per-cent growth in shipments to China — even off a low base — reflects demand for Indian iron ore, seafood and agricultural produce, and any genuine reopening of Chinese markets to Indian pharma and IT services would compound it. That is the leverage New Delhi is trying to convert in the current diplomatic thaw: access, not tariffs.

For the economy, the deficit matters beyond diplomacy. A $100-billion-plus annual gap with a single country — India's largest goods trade deficit by far — feeds directly into the current account, pressures the rupee, and concentrates strategic vulnerability in sectors where Chinese supply dominance is greatest. Wednesday's data lands at a particularly sensitive moment, with the rupee already past 95 per dollar and the central bank tightening policy in part to defend it.

What to watch: whether the renewed diplomatic engagement produces anything concrete on market access — the one lever that could change the export side of the ledger — and whether India's PLI-era factories begin substituting Chinese components with local ones fast enough to bend the import curve before the deficit sets another record in 2027.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT IS INDIA'S TRADE DEFICIT WITH CHINA?
$105.93 billion in January-September 2026, up 20.7% year on year. It hit a record $112.6 billion in 2025-26.
WHAT DOES INDIA IMPORT FROM CHINA?
Primarily electronics, machinery and chemicals — inputs for India's manufacturing sector — worth $125.19 billion in the first nine months.
WHAT DOES INDIA EXPORT TO CHINA?
Mostly primary products: ores, fuels, seafood and agricultural items, totalling $19.26 billion, up 40.7% on year.
WHY HASN'T THE DEFICIT SHRUNK?
India's manufacturing growth increases demand for Chinese components, while China has been slow to open its markets to Indian IT, pharma and farm products despite repeated requests. GLOSSARY: - Trade deficit: The excess of imports over exports in bilateral trade, paid for in foreign currency. - Current account: A broad measure of trade in goods, services and income; persistent deficits pressure the currency. - PLI: Production-Linked Incentive, government schemes paying manufacturers for incremental output. - China plus one: Corporate strategy of diversifying supply chains beyond China while retaining Chinese operations.
