Shriram Finance Crashes 6% as Brokerages Slash Targets After Q2

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • Key points unavailable

Shares of Shriram Finance fell over 6% on Monday, dropping to Rs 811 on the NSE in morning trade, after several international and domestic brokerages cut their target prices on the stock following its September-quarter earnings. Despite the downgrades to price targets, the consensus rating remains firmly at Buy.

The irony is hard to miss. The NBFC reported a 59% year-on-year jump in standalone net profit to Rs 3,677 crore for the quarter ended September 2026. The market chose to focus on what could go wrong rather than what went right.

The headline numbers were strong across the board. Net interest income rose 38% year-on-year to Rs 8,661 crore, driven by a nearly 16% rise in interest income to Rs 13,669 crore even as interest expenses declined around 10% to Rs 5,008 crore. Net interest margin improved dramatically to 9.52% from 8.19% a year earlier.

Operating profit increased more than 53% year-on-year to Rs 6,809 crore. The cost-to-income ratio improved to 22.52% from 27.76% a year ago, and core operating profit, which excludes other income, rose 53.5% to Rs 6,682 crore.

So why did the stock crash?

The answer lies in credit costs and the monsoon. Management flagged stress in drought-affected areas and raised its credit cost guidance. According to Motilal Oswal, the company indicated that credit costs could rise temporarily by 30 to 40 basis points over the next one or two quarters due to seasonal stress in affected geographies. Crucially, credit costs are still expected to remain below 2.3% for FY27, and the brokerage noted there is no evidence of widespread portfolio deterioration.

The concern is macro rather than company-specific. Lower agricultural output could weigh on rural incomes, consumption, and borrower cash flows in the coming quarters. For a lender whose book is heavily exposed to rural and semi-urban borrowers, that is a genuine headwind. But it is a weather-and-season problem, not a structural one.

The brokerages' reactions reflect this tension between strong near-term numbers and uncertain rural prospects.

Jefferies slashed its target price to Rs 1,145 from Rs 1,210 while maintaining its Buy call. The new target implies more than 32% upside from the previous close of Rs 865. The brokerage noted that the September-quarter profit came in 3% above its estimates, as stronger NII and lower operating expenses more than offset higher credit costs. It highlighted that AUM growth accelerated and NIMs expanded sharply quarter-on-quarter. Jefferies expects the company's AUM to grow at a 17% CAGR over FY26-28, with NIMs remaining resilient even amid rate hikes, though credit costs may stay elevated in the near term.

Citi also kept its Buy rating but cut its target price to Rs 1,100 from Rs 1,200, implying 27% upside. It viewed the Q2 earnings as broadly in line with estimates, with NIM tailwinds offset by higher credit costs. Citi said equity buffers are expected to keep margins elevated in the near term before normalisation, and described the company's AUM growth guidance as conservative. Its target cut reflects a lower normalised return on equity assumption, with drought conditions and elevated crude prices remaining key headwinds.

Motilal Oswal Financial Services cut its target to Rs 1,155, implying 33% upside, also maintaining a Buy call. It pointed to weak monsoons driving higher slippages and writeoffs alongside the raised credit cost guidance, while noting that elevated stress is mainly attributable to deficient rainfall and seasonal pressures in select regions.

The stock's longer-run record adds context to the panic. Shriram Finance shares have fallen more than 12% in a week and 18% in a month, and are down over 20% in 2026 so far. Over one year, however, the stock has still gained around 14%. Over three years it has delivered multibagger returns of more than 126%, and over five years 166%. The company's market capitalisation stands at nearly Rs 1.93 lakh crore.

For investors, the episode illustrates a recurring pattern in Indian financials: when a high-yield lender signals rising credit costs, the market prices in the worst case immediately, even when management quantifies the stress as temporary and bounded. Whether the drought-driven stress proves as contained as the brokerages believe will become clear over the next two quarters of disclosures.

The consensus view is that the market is paying for certainty it does not have. All three brokerages see 27-33% upside from current levels, and all three agree the portfolio is not deteriorating beyond pockets of weather-driven stress. The next couple of quarters will test that confidence.
