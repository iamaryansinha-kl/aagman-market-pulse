India Current Account Deficit Narrows to $5.2 Billion in August

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The current account deficit narrowed to $5.2 billion in August from $6.1 billion a year earlier.
  • •- The capital account recorded a $50.6 billion surplus, against just $0.5 billion in the year-ago month.
  • •- The balance of payments swung to a $45.4 billion surplus from a $5.6 billion deficit.
  • •- The central bank's dollar mobilisation drive pulled in $83 billion of non-resident deposits in August.
  • •- A $22.4 billion net outflow under external commercial borrowings capped the capital account gain.

India's external accounts logged their most dramatic single-month swing in years in August: the current account deficit narrowed to $5.2 billion from $6.1 billion a year earlier, while the capital account posted a $50.6 billion surplus against a mere $0.5 billion in the same month last year — together swinging the balance of payments from a $5.6 billion deficit to a $45.4 billion surplus, according to data released by the Reserve Bank of India.

The current account improvement is the structurally significant part of the print. India's merchandise trade deficit has been running above $25 billion a month all year, yet the August current account gap came in at barely a fifth of that number — the visible hand of a widening services surplus and steady remittance inflows doing the quiet arithmetic that keeps the external account fundable. In plain terms: even with expensive oil pushing up the import bill, the country's earnings from software services, professional services and the diaspora covered the overwhelming majority of the gap.

The capital account number, by contrast, is almost entirely policy-made. The RBI's dedicated dollar mobilisation programme — launched to steady a rupee that had breached record lows against the dollar — drove an accumulation of $83 billion in non-resident deposits in a single month, a figure that dwarfs anything seen in the history of the NRI deposit schemes. The trade-off embedded in that success is the cost: the external commercial borrowing head saw a $22.4 billion net outflow in August as Indian borrowers pulled back from overseas debt, capping what would otherwise have been an even larger capital account surplus. Add ECB repayments to the NRI deposit surge, and August's capital flow story reads as a deliberate, expensive recalibration of India's external liabilities toward the central bank's chosen channel.

Zoom out to the first five months of the financial year and the picture sharpens. The balance of payments shows a cumulative surplus of $58 billion against a $0.8 billion deficit in the year-ago period; the capital account surplus stands at $76.3 billion versus $12 billion; but the current account deficit has widened to $18.3 billion from $12.8 billion. That combination tells the true story of India's external position this year: the structural deficit is growing with the oil bill, and it is being financed not by the confidence-driven inflows of a normal year but by the central bank's engineered mobilisation of non-resident money.

The implications run in two directions. For the rupee, the August data explains the currency's resilience this month — with $45 billion added to reserves in a single month, the RBI's firepower to defend the 95-96 band is plainly not exhausted, and the market knows it. For policy credibility, the question is durability: NRI deposits attracted at premium rates are fair-weather money that reprices quickly, and a capital account built on them is only as stable as the spread the central bank is willing to pay. If Brent retreats and the oil bill normalises, the current account will narrow on its own and the expensive deposits can be allowed to run off. If it does not, August's surplus will need to be repeated, month after month, at a price.

The August CAD number also puts Thursday's September merchandise trade data in proper perspective. The trade deficit widened to $30.21 billion in September, and the market's instinct was to flinch; but the August experience shows what the full external ledger looks like once services exports and remittances are counted — a $25-billion-plus merchandise gap compressed to a $5.2 billion current account hole. The cushion is real, but it is not infinite: services surpluses grow in the high single digits while the oil bill can jump 20 per cent in a quarter, and September's wider merchandise gap will test the arithmetic when next month's data lands.

Finally, the five-month BoP surplus of $58 billion marks a decisive turn from the anxious summer, when foreign portfolio outflows and a sliding rupee had economists dusting off 2013-style 'taper tantrum' comparisons. Those comparisons now look overdone: the central bank has demonstrated it can manufacture dollar inflows at scale, the current account remains comfortably financed, and forex reserves — already covering close to a year of imports — are being rebuilt rather than drawn down. India's external account in October is not fragile; it is expensive. The distinction matters, and August's numbers are the clearest evidence yet of which one applies.

For bond and currency markets, the release resolves a puzzle that had been building all week: how the rupee could rally for four straight sessions through a record merchandise trade gap. The answer is that the trade gap was never the whole story — the invisible earnings that sit beneath it, and the RBI's willingness to pay for dollars, were. As one quarter of data becomes two, the market will shift its attention from whether the external account holds to how much it costs to hold it; that is a far more comfortable question than the one asked in September.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT WAS INDIA'S CURRENT ACCOUNT DEFICIT IN AUGUST?
It narrowed to $5.2 billion from $6.1 billion a year earlier, driven by a narrower merchandise deficit and a widening services surplus.
WHAT DROVE THE BALANCE OF PAYMENTS SURPLUS?
A $50.6 billion capital account surplus, powered by $83 billion of non-resident deposits under the RBI's dollar mobilisation programme.
HOW DOES THE FIVE-MONTH PICTURE LOOK?
The BoP shows a $58 billion surplus, but the current account deficit has widened to $18.3 billion from $12.8 billion a year ago.
WHAT IS THE CONCERN WITH THE CAPITAL INFLOWS?
Non-resident deposits are rate-sensitive and can reverse quickly; the $22.4 billion ECB outflow shows other channels are weakening. GLOSSARY: - Current account deficit: The gap between what a country earns from abroad (exports, services, remittances, income) and what it pays (imports, income to foreigners). - Balance of payments: The sum of the current account and the capital account — the net change in a country's foreign-exchange position. - Non-resident deposits: Deposits held by Indians abroad in domestic banks; a classic channel for mobilising external dollars, typically at premium interest rates. - External commercial borrowings: Overseas loans taken by Indian companies and banks; net outflows here mean repayments exceeded fresh borrowing.
