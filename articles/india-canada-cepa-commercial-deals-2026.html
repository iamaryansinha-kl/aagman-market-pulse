India, Canada Sign Deals, Target Trade Pact by End-2026

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Commercial agreements and MoUs were signed at the Canada-India Business Ceremony in Bengaluru on Friday.
  • •- The proposed CEPA aims to conclude by end-2026; the latest negotiating round was in Ottawa from October 5-9.
  • •- The next round is scheduled in New Delhi in early November.
  • •- Bilateral trade stood at $7.96 billion in FY26.
  • •- Canada estimates the pact could add $4.4-6.5 billion to bilateral trade.
  • •- Collaboration areas flagged: AI, quantum computing, aviation, sustainability and smart mobility.

India and Canada took the next procedural step in their trade reconciliation on Friday, with Indian and Canadian entities signing a series of commercial agreements and memoranda of understanding at the Canada-India Business Ceremony in Bengaluru. The signing took place in the presence of Canada's Minister of International Trade, Maninder Sidhu, as part of a Team Canada Trade Mission to India — the commercial choreography running parallel to negotiations for a Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement that both governments now aim to conclude by the end of 2026.

The CEPA negotiations themselves are moving on an aggressive calendar. The most recent round closed in Ottawa on October 9 after covering market access for goods and services, investment, rules of origin, intellectual property rights and trade remedies — the dense technical core of any modern trade deal. The next round is scheduled in New Delhi in early November, keeping up a pace that trade officials on both sides describe as aimed at a year-end conclusion. Bilateral trade between the countries stood at $7.96 billion in FY26, a modest base with room to grow: Canadian estimates project the agreement could add $4.4 billion to $6.5 billion to bilateral trade.

The diplomatic backdrop makes the speed notable. India-Canada relations spent the better part of two years in a deep freeze following Ottawa's 2023 allegations over the killing of a Sikh separatist on Canadian soil, which New Delhi rejected. The thaw has been gradual — resumed ministerial visits, a police-cooperation understanding, and now a trade mission with an actual negotiating calendar — and Prime Minister Narendra Modi is scheduled to visit Canada on December 11-12, which would have been hard to imagine eighteen months ago. A concluded CEPA by end-2026 would be the clearest proof that the thaw has become a warming.

Minister Sidhu's pitch in Bengaluru was explicitly commercial. Canada, he said, wants to double its non-US exports over the next decade, and "India was a market Canada could not afford to ignore," citing the country's roughly 7 per cent annual growth, 1.4 billion population and trajectory toward becoming the world's third-largest economy. He flagged collaboration opportunities in artificial intelligence, quantum computing, aviation infrastructure, digital innovation, sustainability and smart mobility — a list that leans into Canadian commercial strengths rather than the resources trade that has historically dominated the relationship.

The corporate footprint he cited suggests the relationship is already deeper than the headline trade number implies. Kinaxis, OpenText, CGI, CAE, Magellan Aerospace, MDA Space and BCE Global Tech all operate in India, and Fairfax Financial's investment in Bengaluru's airport — alongside Canadian firms Genetec, Transoft Solutions and RBH Access working on the project — gives the partnership a visible piece of physical infrastructure in India's technology capital.

For India, the Canada track fits a pattern that this week's trade diplomacy has made unmistakable: negotiate everywhere, all at once. Alongside the ongoing US engagement, the recently implemented New Zealand pact, advancing UK and EU talks, and Friday morning's complaints about ASEAN and China, the Canada CEPA is one more thread in the densest trade-calendar India has ever run. The stakes differ by partner — Canada is a mid-sized trade relationship, not a structural one — but the logic is the same: in a world where the largest economies are weaponising market access, a diversified portfolio of trade agreements is insurance.

The year-end deadline is ambitious but plausible; the technical rounds are done or scheduled, the political will is demonstrated, and the December Modi visit gives both capitals a natural deadline to point to. What the agreement ultimately delivers will be judged less by the signing than by what it unlocks — services mobility, investment protection and the aviation-and-technology commerce that the Bengaluru ceremony previewed. The MoUs signed on Friday are gestures; the text negotiated in New Delhi in November will be the substance.

The composition of the prospective deal matters more than its size. Canada's traditional exports to India — pulses, potash, timber, energy — are the commodities of an older trade pattern, and they will feature in any final text. But the partnership both governments are actively selling is the new-economy one: Canadian AI and quantum research meeting India's digital public infrastructure and engineering talent, Canadian aviation and space firms feeding into the world's fastest-growing aviation market, and Canadian pension money — Fairfax being the visible example — finding long-duration Indian infrastructure assets to hold.

For Canadian trade policy, the India push carries a second, strategic weight that officials state openly: reducing dependence on the United States. With Washington's tariff posture unpredictable and the USMCA review looming, Ottawa has made doubling non-US exports a decade-long mission, and India is the single largest growth market on that map. Sidhu's Bengaluru framing — that India is a market Canada cannot afford to ignore — reads, in that light, less like diplomatic courtesy and more like industrial policy. The end-2026 deadline will test whether the two bureaucracies can convert the diplomatic thaw into scheduled commitments before the calendar, and the politics, get harder.
