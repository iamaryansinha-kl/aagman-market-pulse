Piruz Khambatta's Ashoi Book: How 60,000 Parsis Built $400 Billion Empire

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/Piruz Khambatta launches Ashoi book at Mumbai's Taj Mahal Palace on May 9, 2026, tracing how 60,000 Parsis built $400 billion in enterprise value
  • 02/Parsis comprise just 0.004% of India's population yet created institutions like Tata, Godrej, Wadia, Shapoorji Pallonji and Rasna
  • 03/Khambatta announces ₹100 crore startup corpus with Zoroastrian Chamber of Commerce to fund founders across all communities

Piruz Khambatta, Chairman and CEO of Ahmedabad-based Rasna Pvt Ltd, launched his book Ashoi: The Zoroastrian Art of Success and Immortality on May 9, 2026. The event took place at the Crystal Room of Mumbai's Taj Mahal Palace hotel.

Union Minister for Parliamentary Affairs and Minority Affairs Kiren Rijiju attended the launch. He described the Parsi way of doing business as "an enigma" and praised the book as a guide to balancing economic success with sustainability.

Khambatta is the founder of Rasna, the ₹2,000-crore soft drink concentrate maker. He has spent four decades building the business around Ashoi, the Zoroastrian principle of righteousness, purity and integrity in thought, word and deed.

The book highlights a striking anomaly in Indian business history. Parsis account for just 0.004 per cent of India's population, roughly one in every 24,000 Indians. Yet they helped build companies collectively worth nearly $400 billion.

These include Tata Group, Godrej Industries, Wadia Group, Shapoorji Pallonji Group, Poonawalla Group and Rasna. Tata alone generates annual revenues equivalent to several percentage points of India's GDP.

"The most enduring institutions are often those that combine wealth creation with social purpose," Khambatta said. Around two-thirds of Tata Sons is owned by philanthropic trusts funding education, healthcare and scientific research.

Khambatta describes Ashoi as an operating system built on the Zoroastrian triad: Humata, Hukhta, Hvarshta. These translate to Good Thoughts, Good Words and Good Deeds.

In practical terms, it asks three questions before any decision: Is my intention honest? Am I speaking truthfully? Will my action help rather than harm?

"Trust is not a soft virtue," Khambatta said. "It is a hard economic asset—one that appreciates every time you use it and disappears the moment you compromise it."

At the launch, Khambatta announced a ₹100 crore startup corpus to be set up with an upcoming Zoroastrian Chamber of Commerce. The fund will be open to founders across all communities.

"We are not giving grants or interest-free loans. We are investing as partners, because the goal is to build successful businesses, not dependency," Khambatta said.

The model draws inspiration from the Jain International Trade Organisation. JITO's investment platform has mobilised more than ₹260 crore into the startup ecosystem over the past seven years.

Khambatta draws a parallel between Ashoi and classics such as Think and Grow Rich and The Millionaire Next Door. The book distils 50 principles from Zoroastrian texts and his own entrepreneurial journey.

"One does not need to be Parsi to use these ideas, just as one need not be Japanese to learn from Ikigai or a Roman emperor to benefit from Stoicism," he said.

"Making a million is not success," Khambatta added. "If your success is defined only as money, that is the starting of all evils."
