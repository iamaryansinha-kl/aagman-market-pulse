Deloitte Sees 7.1-7.3% India GDP Growth in FY27 on Investment

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Deloitte India projects GDP growth of 7.1-7.3% in FY27, moderating to 6.6-7.0% in FY28.
  • •- Gross fixed capital formation grew 11.9% in Q1 FY27, its fastest in four years.
  • •- Sectors flagged for private capex: semiconductors, electronics, defence, clean energy and AI infrastructure.
  • •- The RBI and OECD forecast 7.1%; the IMF, ADB, S&P and Moody's expect 7.0%.
  • •- Risks: geopolitics, energy prices and global trade disruption.

India's growth story has spent a decade waiting for its third engine. Household consumption carried the economy through the 2010s; government infrastructure spending took the lead in the early 2020s; and private corporate investment — the engine that turns a fast economy into a productive one — kept promising to arrive. Deloitte India's latest Economic Outlook, released on Friday, argues that the waiting is effectively over: the consulting firm projects GDP growth of 7.1-7.3% in the current financial year, with private investment finally joining the other two engines, before a moderation to 6.6-7.0% in FY28.

The evidence Deloitte cites is the most-watched number in India's national accounts. Gross fixed capital formation — the share of GDP devoted to building factories, machines and infrastructure — grew 11.9 per cent in the first quarter of FY27, its fastest expansion in four years. "The question is whether these conditions will encourage private investment to play a larger role in boosting growth in the coming years. Recent indicators offer grounds for optimism," said Rumki Majumdar, economist at Deloitte India, framing the quarter not as a data point but as a possible regime change.

The conditions she lists have been building for some time. Corporate balance sheets are healthier than they have been in years, capacity utilisation has climbed to levels that historically precede capex cycles, and the infrastructure build-out of the past half-decade — highways, ports, power, logistics — has lowered the cost of adding new capacity in exactly the places that need it. What has been missing is the confidence to commit: boards that spent the 2010s deleveraging are only now, Deloitte argues, beginning to spend again.

The report identifies the sectors where that spending is likely to concentrate, and the list reads like a summary of the government's industrial policy: semiconductors, electronics manufacturing, defence production, clean energy and artificial intelligence infrastructure. These are capital-hungry industries with long gestation periods, exactly the kind that turn an investment uptick into a durable expansion of productive capacity. They are also, not incidentally, the sectors where India's import dependence — in chips, electronics and energy technology — creates both the commercial case and the strategic one.

Deloitte's forecast sits comfortably inside the institutional consensus. The Reserve Bank of India and the OECD both project 7.1% growth for FY27; the IMF, the Asian Development Bank, S&P Global Ratings and Moody's expect 7.0%. The alignment matters less for the number itself than for what it implies about the debate: nobody serious is forecasting a slowdown this year, and the disagreements are about tenths of a percentage point, not direction. The firmer question is FY28, where Deloitte's 6.6-7.0% range acknowledges the usual drags — a high base, global uncertainty, the possibility that consumption cools as the post-pandemic tailwind fades.

The risks are the ones readers of this publication will recognise. Geopolitical tensions, energy-price volatility and disruption to global trade all feature in Deloitte's caution list, and each has been live in India this month — crude has been elevated, the rupee has been under pressure, and the trade-policy calendar is as crowded as it has ever been. A private-capex-led economy is more exposed to financing conditions than a consumption-led one, which makes the interest-rate path and the cost of capital the variables to watch as FY27 unfolds.

There is also an external-account dimension to the investment story that Friday's data underscored. A narrowing current-account deficit and a surging capital-account surplus — the twin themes of this week's balance-of-payments numbers — are precisely what a capex upswing looks like from the outside: foreign capital arriving to fund domestic investment faster than imports of consumer goods are rising. If the GFCF trend Deloitte highlights holds, the capital inflows that stabilised the rupee this month should find real economy demand for them, which is the virtuous version of the cycle.

And there is the historical qualifier that every India capex forecast carries. The country has seen at least three false starts for a private-investment supercycle in the past fifteen years, each announced by a strong GFCF print and each smothered by the next external shock or credit event. Deloitte's own FY28 moderation — 6.6-7.0%, a full half-point below FY27 — is effectively a bet that the pattern partially repeats: investment carries this year, and something, eventually, cools it. The difference this time, the report implies, is that the balance sheets doing the investing are not the ones that were over-leveraged a decade ago.

The deeper message of the report is about sequencing. India has spent years improving the business environment — balance sheets, infrastructure, incentives — and Deloitte's verdict is that the improvement phase is complete enough that what follows depends on execution. "India has improved many of the business conditions needed to expand. Turning those improvements into sustained private investment now depends on how confidently firms can plan, finance, and execute their next projects," Majumdar said. Four years after the last capex cycle stalled, the 11.9% GFCF print says firms are planning again. Whether they finance and execute will decide whether FY27 is remembered as the year the third engine finally caught.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT IS DELOITTE'S FY27 GROWTH FORECAST?
7.1-7.3%, supported by consumption, public infrastructure spending and recovering private investment; FY28 growth is seen moderating to 6.6-7.0%.
WHY DOES THE REPORT EMPHASISE PRIVATE INVESTMENT?
GFCF grew 11.9% in Q1 FY27, its fastest in four years, suggesting corporate capex is finally joining household consumption and government spending as a growth driver.
WHICH SECTORS COULD DRIVE CAPEX?
Semiconductors, electronics, defence manufacturing, clean energy and AI infrastructure.
WHAT ARE THE MAIN RISKS?
Geopolitical tensions, energy-price swings and global trade disruption, plus the usual drag from a high base in FY28. GLOSSARY: - Gross fixed capital formation (GFCF): The portion of GDP spent on fixed assets like factories, machinery and infrastructure — a proxy for investment. - Capacity utilisation: The share of existing productive capacity that factories are actually using; high readings typically precede new investment. - Capex cycle: The recurring multi-year period when companies accelerate capital spending. - Base effect: The arithmetic impact of comparing current growth against an unusually high or low previous-year figure.
