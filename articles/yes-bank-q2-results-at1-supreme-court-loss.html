YES Bank Q2 Profit Up 65%; AT1 Ruling Risks Rs 5,217 Crore Hit

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Reported net profit rose 65% to Rs 1,080 crore for Q2FY27.
  • •- Pro-forma, the Rs 8,415 crore AT1 bond hit would turn that into a Rs 5,217 crore post-tax loss.
  • •- The Supreme Court on October 15 upheld quashing the bank's 2020 AT1 write-down, dismissing appeals by the bank, RBI and the government.
  • •- Auditors said reported performance would have been "materially different" had the impact been recognised.
  • •- Pro-forma net worth falls to Rs 46,970 crore from Rs 53,268 crore; RoA to negative 4.3% from 0.9%.
  • •- The stock had already nosedived 20% on the ruling; a review petition is being considered.

YES Bank's second-quarter results are two documents in one: the earnings release, and the shadow earnings release that Indian accounting rules would not yet let it file. The bank on Sunday reported a 65 per cent jump in net profit to Rs 1,080 crore for the September quarter, driven by higher income and improved asset quality. But its own pro-forma calculations show that if the estimated impact of the Rs 8,415 crore Additional Tier-1 bond judgment were factored in, the quarter would instead show a post-tax loss of Rs 5,217 crore. The auditors said so in plain terms: the results do not reflect the judgment's financial impact, and had it been recognised, reported performance "may have been materially different."

The judgment arrived on October 15, two days before the earnings date made the collision unavoidable. The Supreme Court upheld the Bombay High Court's ruling setting aside YES Bank's 2020 decision to write down its AT1 bonds, and dismissed the appeals filed by the bank, its former administrator, the RBI and the Union government. The write-down had been the centrepiece of the rescue orchestrated in March 2020, when the regulator-engineered reconstruction scheme — the one that brought in State Bank of India and private investors — extinguished the AT1 holders' claims to protect the depositors and the system. Six years later, the courts have ruled that extinguishment illegal, and the bill has come back to the bank that was saved.

The pro-forma arithmetic shows how large that bill is. Net worth would fall to Rs 46,970 crore from the reported Rs 53,268 crore — an erosion of Rs 6,298 crore, or roughly 12 per cent of the bank's capital. Borrowings would rise to Rs 86,149 crore from Rs 77,734 crore, as the bonds return to the liability side where they lived before the write-down. The quarter's return on assets would swing from a reported 0.9 per cent to negative 4.3 per cent. None of these figures appears in the official results; they exist only in the disclosure, which is where the market's attention has gone.

Managing director and CEO Vinay Tonse said the bank is examining the ruling in detail and will take appropriate steps in accordance with legal and regulatory requirements. ET reported on October 17 that the bank and the RBI are considering a review petition — the last legal avenue short of compliance. The strategic picture is uncomfortable for all parties: the RBI's 2020 reconstruction was designed precisely to prevent this outcome, the government backed it, and the courts have now told all three institutions that the method was unlawful even if the rescue was necessary. Whatever the review petition achieves, the precedent has moved, and every future resolution of a distressed bank will have to price in the possibility that AT1 investors cannot be wiped out.

For YES Bank's equity holders, the damage is partly already marked. The stock nosedived 20 per cent on the Supreme Court ruling, effectively pre-pricing the capital erosion the pro-forma numbers now quantify. The bank's operating quarter, in isolation, was genuinely strong — the 65 per cent profit growth and improving asset quality are real, and the franchise that Tonse has been rebuilding since 2023 continues to mend. But a quarter in which the auditors formally distance themselves from the headline number is not a quarter the market will credit on its face. The December quarter will be the first filed under the shadow of whatever resolution emerges, and until then, every YES Bank number carries an asterisk.

There is also a balance-sheet timing problem that the disclosure creates rather than resolves. The judgment is final unless a review petition succeeds, yet the bank has chosen not to recognise the liability in these results, citing the evaluation of accounting implications. That is defensible under accounting standards while legal remedies remain genuinely open — but the 20 per cent stock fall says the market has already rendered its verdict on the probability of success. Each additional quarter of non-recognition now compounds the eventual adjustment, and if the review fails, the hit lands on a quarter that will also carry whatever credit costs the oil-driven rate environment is about to deliver. The Rs 6,298 crore pro-forma capital erosion is 12 per cent of net worth; for a bank whose entire equity story is the rebuild, that is the difference between a completed repair and a reopened wound.

The wider lesson is about the architecture of bank rescues. India's AT1 market — the bail-in-able capital layer that allows regulators to impose losses without a taxpayer bailout — has just had its defining test case ruled against the regulator. The implications run beyond YES Bank: the pricing of AT1 instruments across the banking system will reprice this risk, which makes that capital layer more expensive for every bank that issues it, which ultimately raises the cost of the safety the framework was built to provide. The 2020 rescue saved the depositors and the system; the 2026 ruling has billed the system for the method. The review petition now being weighed is, in effect, the RBI's appeal against the long-run cost of its own victory.
