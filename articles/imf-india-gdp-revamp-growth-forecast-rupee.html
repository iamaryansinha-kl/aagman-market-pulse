IMF Backs India's GDP Revamp, Says Rupee Pain Is Not Failure

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The IMF said India's GDP data revisions are consistent with the advice it has given, calling MoSPI's work good practice.
  • •- The Fund raised India's growth forecast to 7.4% for 2026 and 7.0% for 2027, citing a much stronger-than-expected April-June quarter.
  • •- Krishna Srinivasan said currency pressure "should not be treated as a measure of whether an economy is succeeding or failing."
  • •- The rupee has gained for four straight sessions to 95.17 after the RBI's October 10 measures.
  • •- The IMF assumes oil at about $96 a barrel in 2026 and the Strait of Hormuz reopening from March 2027.

The International Monetary Fund on Thursday gave New Delhi a rare double endorsement: approval of the controversial overhaul of India's GDP statistics, and a public reminder that the rupee's troubles are a symptom of an external energy shock, not evidence of domestic economic failure.

Speaking at a press briefing in Bangkok after the release of the Fund's Regional Economic Outlook for Asia and the Pacific, Krishna Srinivasan, director of the IMF's Asia and Pacific Department, said the authorities have issued a note explaining the revisions and that the methodology changes are "entirely consistent" with the advice the IMF has given, including from its statistics department. What the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation has done, he said, is in line with good practice — while adding, diplomatically, that there is "always room for improvement."

The endorsement matters because India's new GDP series — released on February 27 with 2022-23 as the base year, replacing 2011-12 — has drawn scepticism from economists who questioned how revised figures reshaped the growth narrative. The ministry says the quarterly estimates follow the IMF's own Quarterly National Accounts Manual of 2017. The Fund's statistics department signing off blunts much of that criticism.

On growth, the IMF revised India upward because the April-June quarter came in much stronger than expected; MoSPI put real GDP growth at 7.8 per cent for that quarter. The World Economic Outlook now sees India at 7.4 per cent for calendar 2026 — 0.5 percentage point higher than its April forecast and 0.6 point above July — and 7.0 per cent for 2027, with a medium-term path of 6.9 per cent. Srinivasan said growth has "surprised on the upside" in India, and the report credited favourable demographics, a large domestic market and structural reforms. Unusually among major economies, India needs no more fiscal consolidation than currently planned to keep its debt ratio at 2025 levels.

The more consequential message was on the rupee. Srinivasan said pressure on the exchange rate does not point to any disconnect between domestic growth and external markets: the exchange rate is a function of global interest rates, investor risk appetite and oil prices, and India, as an oil importer, has been hit by the energy shock. He said it should not be treated as a measure of whether an economy is succeeding or failing. For an economy whose currency has slid through 95 per dollar this year, the remark reads as an external validation of the government's "strong fundamentals" defence.

The data behind that defence is substantial. According to the RBI's October 7 policy statement, India's foreign exchange reserves stood at $734.6 billion as of October 2 — roughly eleven months of imports. Net foreign direct investment rose to $13.8 billion in April-August, even as foreign portfolio investors pulled out a net $10.3 billion between April and October 5. The same policy statement raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50 per cent, the central bank's first hike in roughly four years, as it moved to contain imported inflation.

The RBI has also intervened directly in the currency market. On October 10 it opened a special window to meet the entire daily dollar needs of Indian Oil, Hindustan Petroleum and Bharat Petroleum, taking oil companies' dollar demand out of the spot market from October 12 until further notice. It simultaneously tightened rules on forex derivatives — barring the rebooking of cancelled rupee derivative contracts and requiring banks to hold a Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve equal to 20 per cent of the notional amount of eligible contracts above $2 million. The measures worked: the rupee closed at 95.17 on Wednesday, its fourth straight gain and a one-month high, rising 171 paise over the four sessions.

Finance Minister Nirmala Sitharaman said on Tuesday that the rupee has depreciated "significantly" over the past year and a half, that most of the volatility comes from external challenges, and that the RBI will continue efforts to prevent excessive moves. The IMF's framing gives that position international cover: the Fund's own regional outlook names India among emerging economies that faced currency pressure after the energy shock, notes that pressure has partly eased, and lists India among those that stepped into foreign exchange markets — a roster that treats intervention as standard practice, not desperation.

The Fund's baseline assumes oil at about $96 a barrel in 2026 and the Strait of Hormuz reopening from March 2027 — assumptions that map directly onto India's import bill. Asia as a whole is projected to grow 5 per cent in 2025, easing to 4.8 per cent in 2026 and 4.4 per cent in 2027, still well ahead of the world. Srinivasan flagged downside risks from prolonged Middle East tension and an El Nino that could add to fiscal and inflation pressure, advising governments to "protect people and not prices" with targeted support.

For India, the IMF's four regional reform priorities — youth employment and AI-era skills, stronger social protection, energy security and deeper regional integration — read like a checklist the country is already partly executing. Thursday's message, delivered from Bangkok with the Fund's full institutional weight behind it: the data is sound, the growth is real, and the currency's fall is the price of oil, not the verdict on the economy.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT DID THE IMF SAY ABOUT INDIA'S GDP REVISIONS?
That the methodology changes are entirely consistent with IMF advice and good practice, while noting there is always room for improvement.
WHAT IS INDIA'S NEW GROWTH FORECAST?
7.4% for calendar 2026 and 7.0% for 2027, up 0.5 and 0.6 percentage points from April and July respectively; medium-term growth is seen at 6.9%.
WHAT DID THE IMF SAY ABOUT THE RUPEE?
That exchange rate pressure is a function of global rates, risk appetite and oil prices — and should not be treated as a measure of economic success or failure.
WHAT MEASURES HAS THE RBI TAKEN?
A special dollar window for oil marketing companies from October 12, tighter forex derivative rules including a 20% risk reserve, and a repo rate hike to 5.50%. GLOSSARY: - Base year: The reference year against which GDP is measured; changing it updates the basket of goods and prices. - Foreign exchange reserves: Central bank holdings of foreign currencies, used to stabilise the rupee. - FCNR deposits: Foreign currency deposits from non-resident Indians, a source of dollar funding. - Strait of Hormuz: The key oil shipping chokepoint; its closure or reopening moves global crude prices. - Repo rate: The RBI's benchmark lending rate to banks, now 5.50%.
