Swiggy Crashes 16% to Record Low as Quick-Commerce Losses Widen

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- Shares fell 16.4% to a record low of Rs 176.70 on Friday; the stock has lost more than half its value since listing on November 13, 2024.
  • •- Quick-commerce adjusted EBITDA losses widened to a three-quarter high of around Rs 900 crore, even as food delivery reported an in-line quarter.
  • •- Morgan Stanley said investors will keep questioning execution until consistent performance, flagging uncertainty around the separate value-food-delivery app.
  • •- Jefferies said reducing quick-commerce losses is essential for value discovery, noting the price does not reflect the food delivery business and cash.

There is a particular kind of pain reserved for companies that were supposed to be past this stage. Swiggy on Friday crashed 16.4% to a record low of Rs 176.70, a level that means the food delivery and quick-commerce platform has now lost more than half the value it commanded at its market debut on November 13, 2024. The trigger was the September quarter's numbers: while the core food delivery business reported an in-line quarter for revenue and EBITDA, the quick-commerce segment's adjusted EBITDA losses widened to a three-quarter high of around Rs 900 crore. The market's verdict was immediate and brutal — the stock's worst single-day showing since the rout began in earnest.

The quick-commerce math is what spooked investors. Net order value in the segment did grow sequentially, but year-on-year growth moderated — a combination that strips away the industry's favourite defence. The bull case for quick commerce has always rested on a two-step argument: losses are large because growth is large, and once growth matures, the losses shrink. A quarter in which losses hit a fresh multi-quarter peak while the pace of growth actually slows attacks both legs of that argument at once. Add rising competitive intensity — with rivals adding dark stores at a ferocious clip and discounting still funding a meaningful share of consumer habit — and the path from Rs 900 crore of quarterly losses to anything resembling segment profitability looks longer today than it did three months ago.

The brokerages said the quiet part plainly. Morgan Stanley said investors would continue to question Swiggy's execution and strategic choices until the company demonstrated consistent performance, acknowledging that the strategy of increasing store density and accelerating dark-store additions could drive growth in monthly transacting users, but only if that growth proves sustainable under more normalised discounting. That last condition is the entire game: growth bought with subsidies is not growth, it is deferred pricing. The brokerage also flagged uncertainty around the company's plan for a separate app for its value-oriented food delivery business — a product whose success will depend on user profitability, customer acquisition costs and lower delivery costs, none of which are proven yet. The subtext of both critiques is the same: Swiggy is asking shareholders to fund a multi-front war — quick commerce, value food delivery, core premium delivery — and the evidence that any one of those fronts is converging on sustainable economics is, this quarter at least, thinner than the cheque being written.

Jefferies was blunter. "Reduction in Q/C losses is essential for true value discovery," the brokerage said, adding that Swiggy's current share price did not appear to factor in the value of its food delivery business and cash at all — meaning the market is treating the whole company as a quick-commerce liability with the rest thrown in for free. It also judged Swiggy's performance lacklustre against market leader Eternal, which now out-executes it on both of its core battlegrounds. That is a remarkable thing for an analyst to write about a company with a profitable core franchise and billions in cash: it says the market has not just lost patience with the growth story, it has begun pricing the stock as if the losses will never end.

That comparison is the one that should worry shareholders most, because it reframes the problem. Swiggy is not losing to a fading incumbent or an irrational new entrant; it is losing relative ground to a competitor with deeper pockets, a bigger food delivery franchise and its own quick-commerce ambitions. In a two-player market where both sides are burning cash to build infrastructure, being the one whose losses are widening while growth moderates is a very bad place to stand. Friday's record low is the market re-pricing that reality in real time. The stock now trades well below its issue price, meaning the premium investors once paid for a duopoly position in Indian food delivery has been fully unwound — what remains is a bet on whether management can convert quick commerce from a cash furnace into something with a defensible payback per dark store. The bull case, such as it is, rests on the food delivery business being genuinely profitable and on order values continuing to grow even as discounts normalise; the bear case is that competition keeps forcing the company to choose between growth and losses, and that the balance sheet will keep funding that choice for longer than shareholders expect. Until the loss line bends decisively downward, every quarter will be another referendum on which story is true.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHY DID SWIGGY'S STOCK CRASH?
Quick-commerce adjusted EBITDA losses widened to around Rs 900 crore, a three-quarter high, while year-on-year growth in net order value moderated — a bad combination of deeper losses and slower growth.
HOW MUCH HAS THE STOCK FALLEN SINCE LISTING?
Shares hit a record low of Rs 176.70 after Friday's 16.4% fall, down more than half from the November 2024 market debut price.
WHAT DO BROKERAGES SAY?
Jefferies says reducing quick-commerce losses is essential for value discovery and the price does not reflect the food delivery business and cash; Morgan Stanley says investors will question execution until performance is consistent. GLOSSARY: QUICK COMMERCE — Delivery of groceries and essentials in 10-30 minutes from neighbourhood dark stores; the segment requires heavy spending on warehouses, inventory and riders before profitability. ADJUSTED EBITDA — Earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, excluding certain one-off items; the loss figure here measures the segment's core operating burn. NET ORDER VALUE — The total value of customer orders after discounts and cancellations; the segment's key growth metric. DARK STORE — A delivery-only warehouse stocked for rapid-dispatch orders; adding them expands coverage but raises fixed costs.
