India Hair Exports: $940 Million Trade Where China Captures the Value

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/India exported around $940 million of human hair in FY2026 — $287 million raw and $652 million semi-processed
  • 02/Raw hair fetches $100-150 a kilo; the same hair turned into wigs can be worth around $1,000 a kilo
  • 03/Exporters demand a ban on raw hair exports, citing smuggling via Myanmar and suspected Chinese-front bidding at Tirupati auctions

India exported around $940 million of human hair in FY2026. That is over Rs 9,000 crore of ponytails, temple offerings and comb-collected strands shipped overseas.

The break-up: $287 million of raw or unprocessed hair, and $652 million of semi-processed hair. The disentangling and straightening is done by thousands of workers, mostly women, in cottage industries across Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh and West Bengal.

India's own finished-hair industry is tiny by comparison. It exported just $1.35 million worth of wigs, extensions, wefts and related products in the year.

The value chain explains why. Raw goli fetches just $100-150 a kilo. Semi-processed hair commands $200-250 a kilo. Turn the same hair into wigs, and it can be worth around $1,000 a kilo.

About 90% of India's hair exports is goli — hair that falls from heads or gets coiled in combs, collected from homes, barbershops and salons. The other 10% is shorn hair donated by devotees at shrines such as the Venkateswara temple in Tirupati, prized as high-quality Remy hair.

Indian hair is valued across the world for its colour and quality. "Indian black and brown hair is in great demand as it provides a versatile base that can be transformed, coloured and blended into almost any shade," says George Cherian, CEO of Raj Hair International in Chennai. "We work with 47 international colours," he adds.

The finished products are striking in their variety: ash-blonde wigs for Scandinavian women, auburn extensions for Russians, and black and brown wefts for African Americans.

So where does India's raw hair actually go? Officially, about 98% of raw hair is destined for Myanmar, where it is processed before making its way to the real market: Xuchang in China's Henan province.

Xuchang is often dubbed the wig capital of the world, producing nearly 60% of finished hair products. Indian exporters allege the actual volume of raw hair leaving India is far higher than the official numbers, with large quantities smuggled to China along what traders call "the wig route."

Suspicion extends to the auction hall. In September, the finest grade-1 black hair — 27 inches or longer — hit Rs 85,000 a kilo at the Tirupati quarterly auction, with about 700 kg of this premium mane on offer.

Cherian recalls leaving an online auction midway after noticing "aggressive, even abnormal" bidding; bidder identities are kept confidential. He and other Chennai exporters suspect Chinese interests may be behind the unnaturally high bids, possibly through dummy exporters deployed to procure the finest Remy hair, available in substantial quantities at Tirupati and the Basilica of Our Lady of Good Health at Velankanni.

Tirupati's Kalyanakatta hall, where nearly 1 lakh pilgrims arrive at the hilltop temple daily, sets the market's tone. Two grades command the highest auction prices: hair measuring 27 inches and above, and hair between 19 and 26 inches — provided it is black, not grey. The longer the hair, the higher the value; hair shorter than 5 inches is largely used as fertiliser.

The industry's answer is a ban. "We have been demanding that the government immediately ban the export of chutti or goli — raw human hair — so that it can be processed in India and create employment for more than a million people, mainly women," says Benjamin Cherian, president of the Human Hair & Hair Products Manufacturers and Exporters Association of India.

He alleges Chinese buyers come as tourists and purchase goli through local agents in Gudiyatham and Vellore in Tamil Nadu, and that hoarding of hair in India has gone up.

Government policy adds to the frustration: raw human hair can be exported at a minimum price of just $65 a kg. "That's too low. How can the export price be $65 a kg when the price in the domestic market is around $150?" asks MM Gupta, chairman of Gupta Group, which works on goli hair and sends 30-40% of its exports to Chinese manufacturing hubs. He alleges some Indian exporters are fronts for Chinese buyers.

China's dominance is not new. Its ascent to the top of the global wig market began in the 1990s, taking the reins from Italy and South Korea. China has some 8,000 factories processing hair, some employing 2,000-3,000 workers. At Cosmoprof Worldwide in Bologna, the annual beauty trade show, only 20-25 Indian hair companies exhibit, compared with more than 500 Chinese hair exporters.

Sathish Gandhi, director of Chennai-based Allure Hair Products, adds another wrinkle. He claims some Chinese companies, as Chinese labour costs have risen, send raw Indian hair to North Korea for processing; the semi-processed hair is then brought back to China, turned into finished products, and shipped to the US and Europe.

A new threat now looms. A law passed by the US House of Representatives last month empowers President Donald Trump to impose tariffs of up to 100% on countries buying Russian oil and gas. For hair exporters heavily dependent on the US market, it is another potential shock after earlier tariff blows.

Diversification is already underway. Allure Hair Products cut US exposure from 90% of its business until 2024 to 60%, seeking buyers in Europe, Africa, Arab markets and India. Tamil Nadu-based Nandalala Indian Hair, run by sisters Radhika and Athira Kumaran, now counts the UAE and Africa as main markets with just 5-10% from the US; its roughly 100 employees include some earning salaries comparable to tech professionals. Their next bet is India's own expanding domestic wig and hair-extension market.

The bigger question: can Chennai or Kolkata become the next Xuchang? "We have a long way to go to catch up with China," admits Gandhi. Gupta is blunter. "In India, we are still fighting for the basics — ban the export of raw hair and stop smuggling," he says. "How can human hair be smuggled from Hyderabad to China on chartered flights when Indian manufacturers themselves are struggling with a shortage of raw material?"
