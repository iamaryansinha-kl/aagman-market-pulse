RBI Dollar Inflow Measures: $40-70B Window to Fix Balance of Payments

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/RBI's June 2026 concessional swap facility for ECBs and FCNR(B) deposits could attract $40-70 billion in dollar inflows
  • 02/India's forex reserves stand at $672 billion, covering 11 months of imports; BoP deficits in FY25 and FY26 signal structural weakness
  • 03/Economists warn the measures are a temporary fix; maturing liabilities in 3-5 years could pressure the rupee without stronger BoP

The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) introduced a concessional swap facility in June 2026. It targets external commercial borrowings (ECBs) and foreign currency non-resident bank deposits (FCNR[B]).

The measures aim to attract dollar inflows amid sharp rupee depreciation. Market participants estimate the facility could bring in $40 billion to $70 billion.

The central bank is effectively buying policymakers time. The cost is largely borne by the RBI, not the government.

However, these inflows are temporary. As ECBs mature and FCNR(B) deposits come due over the next three to five years, those dollars will need to be repaid.

By then, India will require a stronger balance of payments (BoP) or larger forex reserves. Otherwise, the reversed outflows could pressure the rupee again.

India's forex reserves currently stand at $672 billion. RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra has said this provides import cover for about 11 months.

India's BoP has grown volatile recently. After a surplus in FY23-24, the country posted deficits in FY25 and FY26. This reflects weak capital inflows, particularly on the capital account.

"India's foreign exchange reserves need to rise organically, not just on account of banking inflows," said Dhiraj Nim, economist and FX strategist at ANZ Bank. "BoP is a larger concern because we are in a structurally different world where financial conditions are tight."

"No one knows what the situation will be three years from now. But if it doesn't change, we will get back to where we were a month ago," Nim added.

Unlike the 2013 episode, these measures are pre-emptive. The 2013 steps were introduced during a balance of payments crisis.

"If the rupee weakens further over the next few years, the RBI will end up bearing a higher cost," said Abhishek Upadhyay, senior economist at ICICI Securities Primary Dealership.

A further risk stems from currency movements. A weaker rupee would raise the cost of servicing these liabilities. The dollars mobilised under ECB and FCNR(B) routes become more expensive to repay in rupee terms.

India also faces structural challenges. The country has yet to establish a leading position in emerging sectors such as artificial intelligence. The outlook for software exports, a key source of foreign exchange earnings, is becoming more uncertain.
