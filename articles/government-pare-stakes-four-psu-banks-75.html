Government Plans to Pare Stakes in Four PSU Banks to 75%

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The government plans to pare its stake in four PSU banks — Central Bank of India, Uco Bank, Punjab & Sind Bank and Indian Overseas Bank — to 75%.
  • •- The sales will happen over the next year through the offer-for-sale route, depending on market conditions.
  • •- The move serves the Sebi-mandated minimum public shareholding norm and is expected to improve the banks' valuations.
  • •- Simultaneously, the government is working on a consolidation strategy that could identify two more banks for privatisation.

The government's banking-sector reshuffle is entering its next phase. New Delhi is considering paring its stake in four public sector banks — Central Bank of India, Uco Bank, Punjab & Sind Bank and Indian Overseas Bank — to 75%, the minimum public shareholding mandated by the stock market regulator, according to people familiar with the development. Simultaneously, it is working on a strategy for the next round of consolidation among state-run banks that could identify two more lenders for privatisation. The twin tracks — float-raising now, structural consolidation alongside — sketch out the most consequential reshaping of Indian banking since the 2020 amalgamation round that cut the number of PSU banks from 18 to 12.

The mechanics are deliberately gradual. "The first step is to increase public shareholding in the four lenders in the next one year," an official said, adding that while the banks can tap the market as per their own strategy, the government will offload its stake through the offer-for-sale route depending on market conditions. The greater free float is also expected to improve the four banks' valuations — PSU bank stocks have long traded at structurally lower multiples than private peers, and part of the discount is attributed to thin floats and the overhang of government ownership. Reaching 75% would formalise a step toward normal market status while keeping the government firmly in control.

The privatisation track is the more ambitious one. The government announced in its 2021-22 budget that, besides IDBI Bank, it would take up two public sector banks and one general insurance company for privatisation, and the NITI Aayog had reportedly shortlisted Central Bank of India and Indian Overseas Bank as the two candidates. That ambition now has a template: earlier this month the government announced that Prem Watsa-promoted Fairfax Financial Holdings will acquire a 60.72% stake in IDBI Bank for Rs 52,878 crore, giving the long-delayed privatisation programme its first completed deal. The Centre's long-term intention, officials have indicated, is to maintain only three to four large state-owned banks — a dramatic compression from the dozen that exist today.

The legislative path is the binding constraint. Privatising any public sector bank requires changes to the Banking Companies (Acquisition and Transfer of Undertakings) Act, which the government may pursue alongside the consolidation exercise. Officials have framed the next steps as bank-led: "The aim is to have three or four large banks, and some of the suggestions in this regard have to come from the banks themselves based on factors such as technology compatibility, geographical spread and business mix," one said, clarifying that privatisation plans will also weigh these factors. In other words, the consolidation map will be drawn partly by the banks' own compatibility logic, not purely by ministerial fiat — a nuance that matters for employees, depositors and the regional footprints the smaller banks serve.

The timeline pressure comes from the regulator. The government had exempted state-run firms, including financial institutions, from the Sebi minimum public shareholding requirement of 25% public float until August 2026, and a further extension is expected to be notified — but each extension has a cost in credibility with investors who see the norm applied strictly to private companies. The four banks in question are among the smallest and weakest in the state-run stable, survivors of the 2020 consolidation only by virtue of their regional franchises and the political difficulty of merging them into stronger parents. The IDBI template showed the government's willingness to accept valuations anchored to intrinsic value rather than thin-market prices when strategic control is changing hands. Whether that template extends to the four now headed to 75% is the question investors will bring to every offer for sale in the coming year. A 75% government stake is, after all, still dominant ownership — the market will read these sales as housekeeping for the float requirement, not as a change in control, and price them accordingly unless a strategic-sale track opens alongside.

For the broader market, the programme's significance is the float itself. PSU bank stocks have rallied hard in recent years on the back of improving asset quality and the reform narrative, but index weight and institutional ownership remain constrained by exactly the kind of concentrated ownership these sales will dilute. A steady drumbeat of offer-for-sales from the four banks gives the market a recurring supply of a widely-held sector at prices the government needs to defend — which is one reason officials are explicit that timing will depend on market conditions. The programme is as much market management as it is policy, and its success will be measured not in the percentage points of stake sold but in whether the four banks' valuations re-rate once the overhang finally clears. Queries emailed to the finance ministry did not elicit a response till press time.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHICH FOUR BANKS ARE INVOLVED?
Central Bank of India, Uco Bank, Punjab & Sind Bank and Indian Overseas Bank — the government plans to reduce its stake in each to 75% over the next year.
HOW WILL THE STAKES BE SOLD?
Primarily through the offer-for-sale route, depending on market conditions; banks can also tap the market as per their own strategy.
DOES THIS MEAN PRIVATISATION OF THESE BANKS?
Not immediately. Privatisation would require legislative changes to the Banking Companies Act, which the government may pursue alongside a broader consolidation exercise. GLOSSARY: MINIMUM PUBLIC SHAREHOLDING — The Sebi requirement that listed companies maintain at least 25% public float; state-run firms have operated under government exemptions. OFFER FOR SALE — A mechanism where existing shareholders sell stock directly to the public through the exchange, commonly used by the government to divest PSU stakes.
