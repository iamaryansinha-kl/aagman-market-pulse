RBI Revises FCNR(B) Deposit Inflows Upwards to $133 Billion After Robust NRI Response

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • Key points unavailable

The Reserve Bank of India has upwardly revised the amount mobilised under its special Foreign Currency Non-Resident (FCNR-B) deposit scheme to around $133 billion as of August 31, the closing date of the facility.

The latest figure is higher than the provisional data released earlier this month, which showed FCNR(B) mobilisation at $127.226 billion.

Total forex inflows at $143.6 billion The RBI launched the special USD-INR forex swap facility on June 8, 2026. It covers three channels: FCNR(B) deposits, Overseas Foreign Currency Borrowings (OFCBs) and External Commercial Borrowings (ECBs).

According to the latest RBI data reported by authorised dealer banks up to September 18, total inflows under the special facility stood at $143.596 billion.

FCNR(B) deposits accounted for $132.980 billion of the total. OFCBs contributed $5.320 billion, while ECBs accounted for $5.296 billion.

Early closure after strong response The FCNR(B) window was initially scheduled to remain open until September 30. However, the central bank advanced the closing date to August 31 after receiving a robust response from the Indian diaspora.

The RBI said the facility's objective had been achieved ahead of schedule, prompting the early closure of the FCNR(B) window.

The ECB and OFCB windows will remain available until December 31, 2026.

Why the facility was launched The RBI introduced the special facility to mobilise foreign currency resources through the three channels. The move was part of the central bank's efforts to support foreign exchange liquidity amid global currency volatility.

The facility allowed banks to raise foreign currency funds at concessional rates. This helped bolster India's forex reserves and reduce pressure on the rupee.

What are FCNR(B) deposits? FCNR(B) deposits are fixed-term bank deposits held by non-resident Indians in specified foreign currencies. Both the principal and interest are repaid in the same foreign currency.

As the deposits are maintained in foreign currency, depositors are not directly exposed to fluctuations in the rupee's exchange rate against the currency in which the deposit is held.

This arrangement allows NRIs to place foreign-currency funds with Indian banks while avoiding direct rupee exchange-rate exposure on the deposit.
