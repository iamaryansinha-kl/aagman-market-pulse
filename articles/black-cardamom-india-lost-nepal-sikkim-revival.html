India's Black Cardamom Share Falls From 85% to 22% as Nepal Dominates

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/India's share of world large cardamom supply has crashed from nearly 85% to 22%, while Nepal now grows 68% of global output.
  • 02/Exports have slid from over Rs 1,200 crore annually to Rs 150-180 crore, even as prices jumped from Rs 400 to Rs 2,300 per kg.
  • 03/Sikkim's "Mero Alaichi, Mero Dhan" mission is betting on disease-resistant varieties and scientific farming to revive the crop.

India once supplied nearly 85% of the world's large cardamom. Today its share is just 22%, while neighbouring Nepal dominates with 68% of global production. Bhutan accounts for another 8-10%.

The reversal is stark in export data. In the early 2000s, India shipped 5,000-6,000 tonnes a year to more than 20 countries, earning over Rs 1,200 crore annually. Pakistan, Afghanistan and West Asian markets took nearly half of those volumes, with Bangladesh a major buyer.

Now India exports only around 1,200 tonnes of black cardamom a year, earning merely Rs 150-180 crore. Part of the fall reflects a strong domestic market: less surplus remains after local demand is met.

Yet India is missing a boom it once owned. Farm-gate prices have climbed to Rs 2,300 per kg from roughly Rs 400 in the mid-2000s. The global large cardamom market could nearly double to $480 million by 2033 from $250 million in 2025.

Large cardamom is not green elaichi. It grows under forest shade in the Eastern Himalayas, not the Western Ghats. Its smoky flavour seasons biryani, pulao and meat dishes, and it is used in foods, beverages, perfumes and medicines.

In Sikkim's mountain villages the crop once paid for children's education, weddings and family homes. Farmers called it "black gold". Unlike saffron or green cardamom, it rarely made headlines — until the plantations began failing.

A cardamom plant under forest shade should start producing after four years and live 25-30 years. Instead, Sikkim's farmers watch plants dry up in five or six years, if not sooner. Diseases cut harvests to 60-65% of expected output, and replanted saplings take years to yield again.

Sikkim's production tells the story: 5,600 tonnes in 2003-04, a collapse to 2,500 tonnes in 2010, and a recovery to 5,591 tonnes in 2024-25 — still below levels from two decades ago.

The science of the decline traces to a prolonged drought in the mid-2000s. "A prolonged drought in the mid-2000s weakened the crop so severely that normally harmless fungi inside the plant turned aggressive, triggering widespread disease," said KB Subba, senior biotechnologist at Sikkim's science and technology department.

Four diseases now do the damage: leaf blight, two viral infections known as chirkey and foorkey, and rhizome rot. In recent farmer surveys, nearly two-thirds identified crop disease as their biggest challenge.

"What we are seeing is not a single disease problem. It is a complex interaction between climate stress, soil health, plant genetics and microbial behaviour. That is why simple solutions have not worked," Subba said.

Decades of cloning plants from the same genetic stock eroded their disease resistance. Declining soil health, shrinking forest shade and limited irrigation added stress — more than half of farms still depend entirely on rainfall. Warmer winters and unpredictable rain left plants even more vulnerable.

The livelihood stakes are high. About 21% of Sikkim's rural households grow large cardamom, and for nearly 80% of them it is the main income source. In a good year, the crop contributes up to 70% of household earnings, and few alternative crops pay as well on steep, shaded mountain farms.

"Large cardamom is our backbone. It is how most families here earn their living. But in recent years, yields have clearly fallen. Government labs are beginning to support farmers with better planting material, but the help is still uneven as many farmers have yet to receive disease-resistant seeds," said Dhan K Gurung, who heads a farmer community in North Sikkim's cardamom belt.

Sikkim's response is the "Mero Alaichi, Mero Dhan" (My Cardamom, My Wealth) mission. It targets the entire crop system — soil, shade, disease, genetics, irrigation and farming practices. Scientists are testing seed-based propagation and controlled crossbreeding to build plants that withstand disease and drier conditions, aiming to extend plant life to 12-15 years.

"The decline of large cardamom is not just an agricultural issue, it is a livelihood and ecological challenge that requires scientific, institutional and community-led solutions. Revival will be sustainable only when farmers, researchers, markets and policy move in one direction, rather than work in isolation," said Sandeep Tambe, principal secretary, Sikkim.

Some Nepalese cardamom already enters India for re-trading, blending and onward export — a quiet reminder of who now controls the supply. For Sikkim, the mission is to reverse that flow before the $480 million market of 2033 is settled by someone else.

The irony is that this is happening during a spice boom. Indian spice consumption keeps climbing, and the country's own demand is precisely why exports have thinned — production is being eaten at home rather than shipped abroad.

That domestic cushion also explains the price. At Rs 2,300 per kg, large cardamom is nearly six times pricier than in the mid-2000s, which should make farming it more attractive, not less — yet biology, not economics, is the constraint.

The re-export loophole adds one more wrinkle. Nepalese cardamom crossing into India for blending and onward shipment means some "Indian" exports are no longer grown in India, muddying trade data and masking the domestic decline.

GLOSSARY: Large cardamom (black cardamom): A smoky-flavoured spice grown in the Eastern Himalayas, used in biryanis, meats, beverages and perfumes — distinct from green cardamom. Chirkey and foorkey: Viral diseases of large cardamom that stunt growth and distort leaves. Rhizome rot: A fungal disease that attacks the underground stem of the plant, collapsing it from below. Leaf blight: A fungal disease causing browning and death of plant leaves.
