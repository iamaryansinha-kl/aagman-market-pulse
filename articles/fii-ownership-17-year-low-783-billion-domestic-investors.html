FII Ownership at 17-Year Low of 15.1% Despite $783 Billion Holdings

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/FII ownership of Indian equities has fallen to a 17-year low of 15.1%, per OmniScience Capital analysis of FPI assets-under-custody data
  • 02/In absolute terms, foreign holdings have grown 3.6 times to around $783 billion since 2012, when they stood at roughly $217.3 billion
  • 03/Domestic investors are growing the market pie faster than FIIs; watch stock-level opportunities in financials, power and infrastructure

Foreign institutional investor ownership of Indian equities has fallen to a 17-year low of 15.1%. The figure comes from OmniScience Capital analysis of foreign portfolio investor assets-under-custody data.

The headline number hides a paradox. The absolute value of foreign holdings in Indian equities has grown 3.6 times since 2012, reaching around $783 billion.

Back in 2012, FII holdings stood at roughly $217.3 billion, with an ownership share of about 15-16%. The value climbed to around $300 billion by 2015 and $550 billion by 2020.

Holdings peaked at approximately $930 billion in September 2024. The ownership share, however, kept falling through this entire period of growth in dollar terms.

"The ownership share had already declined even as FII holdings rose, falling from around 30% in 2015 and 20% in 2020 to around 17% by 2024," said Vikas Gupta, CEO and chief investment strategist at OmniScience Capital. "This reflected domestic investors increasing market participation faster than FIIs."

The driver is the financialisation of Indian household wealth. Indian households are allocating more of their savings to financial assets instead of real estate, and that money is increasingly finding its way into equities.

Domestic equity participation remains below developed-market levels. That leaves room for further domestic flows, which could reduce the foreign ownership share even more in the coming years.

"The USD 50 billion FII outflow since 2024 also needs context," said Ashwini Shami, president and chief portfolio manager at OmniScience Capital. "It compares with approximately USD 40 billion of inflows between 2017 and 2024, when the FII asset base was smaller."

Currency depreciation has also contributed to the decline in the dollar value of foreign holdings. The lower FII share is a structural shift driven by financialisation, not necessarily a loss of confidence in Indian equities.

Strong domestic institutional flows can support prices during foreign selling. OmniScience Capital suggests focusing on stock-level opportunities in financials, power and infrastructure across largecap, midcap and smallcap segments.

The numbers behind the paradox deserve a closer look. In 2012, foreign investors held around $217.3 billion worth of Indian equities, which already translated into an ownership share of roughly 15 to 16 per cent. By 2015 the holdings had grown to about $300 billion, by 2020 to $550 billion, and they peaked around $930 billion in September 2024.

Yet the ownership share fell steadily through every one of those milestones. Vikas Gupta, chief executive officer and chief investment strategist at OmniScience Capital, notes that the share had already slipped from around 30 per cent in 2015 to 20 per cent in 2020 and about 17 per cent by 2024 — a period when foreign holdings were rising sharply in absolute terms.

The reason is simple arithmetic. As Indian households shifted wealth from real estate and gold into financial assets, domestic mutual funds, insurance companies and retail investors bought stocks faster than foreign institutions did. The total market capitalisation of Indian equities grew, but domestic hands captured most of the incremental shares, diluting foreign stakes proportionally.

The $50 billion of net FII outflows since 2024 looks alarming when viewed in isolation. OmniScience Capital's Ashwini Shami argues the figure needs context: it compares with approximately $40 billion of inflows between 2017 and 2024, when the foreign asset base itself was smaller. Part of the decline in the dollar value of holdings is also mechanical — rupee depreciation shaves the translated value of a portfolio even if its local-currency share price is flat.

For portfolio construction, the implication cuts both ways. A structurally lower foreign ownership share means that foreign selling episodes, while noisy, no longer determine market direction the way they once did. Strong domestic institutional flows can absorb those outflows and support prices through the cycle, which is exactly what has happened during much of the 2024-2026 selloff.

Gupta's counsel is to stop treating the headline ratio as a verdict on India. Investors, he argues, should instead focus on stock-level opportunities, particularly in financials, power and infrastructure, across largecap, midcap and smallcap segments, where domestic ownership trends and policy tailwinds are company-specific rather than macro-mechanical.

The longer-term read is that India's equity market is still domesticating. Financialisation of household wealth has further to run — household equity participation remains below developed-market levels — which means the foreign ownership share could keep drifting lower even if foreign investors resume buying in size. A 17-year low, in that frame, is less a warning sign than a measure of how domestic Indian markets have become.

THE FII SHARE FELL FROM AROUND 30% IN 2015 TO 20% IN 2020 AND ABOUT 17% BY 2024 EVEN AS THE DOLLAR VALUE OF HOLDINGS CLIMBED. INVESTORS SHOULD FOCUS ON STOCK-LEVEL OPPORTUNITIES, PARTICULARLY IN FINANCIALS, POWER AND INFRASTRUCTURE ACROSS LARGECAP, MIDCAP AND SMALLCAP SEGMENTS. GLOSSARY: FII: Foreign Institutional Investor — overseas institutions investing in Indian equities and debt. FPI assets under custody: Securities held on behalf of foreign portfolio investors by local custodian banks, tracked as a proxy for foreign holdings. Financialisation: The long-term shift of household wealth from physical assets like property and gold into financial assets such as stocks and mutual funds. Market breadth: The proportion of stocks participating in a market move, as distinct from index-level performance. Rupee depreciation: A fall in the rupee's value against the dollar, which reduces the dollar-translated value of rupee-denominated holdings.
