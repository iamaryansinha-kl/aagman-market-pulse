India Reimposes Duty on Chinese Titanium Dioxide Imports

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- India has reimposed anti-dumping duties of $460 to $681 per tonne on specified titanium dioxide imports from China for five years.
  • •- The levy follows a fresh Directorate General of Trade Remedies probe that reached the same conclusion as the original 2025 investigation, which the Calcutta High Court had quashed on procedural grounds.
  • •- The duty adds roughly 19-28% to the landed cost of Chinese material, which supplied 57% of Indian demand — about 2.27 lakh tonnes — in the investigation period.
  • •- Domestic producers Kerala Minerals & Metals, Travancore Titanium and VV Titanium stand to gain; paint, ink and masterbatch makers face higher input costs with no grade-based exemption.

Trade remedy law is a pendulum, and it has just swung back. India has reimposed anti-dumping duties ranging from $460 to $681 per tonne on specified titanium dioxide imports from China for five years, ending more than a year of duty-free imports that followed a court decision striking down the original levy. For the domestic pigment industry, it is vindication; for the paint and coatings industry that buys the white pigment by the shipload, it is a cost shock arriving with festive-season demand in full swing.

The backstory is a case study in how slowly trade law grinds, even when the conclusion never changes. The original duties were imposed in May 2025 after the Directorate General of Trade Remedies found that Chinese exporters were selling titanium dioxide in India at unfairly low prices, injuring domestic producers. The Calcutta High Court quashed that notification in September 2025 — not because the dumping was disproven, but because of procedural defects in how confidential information was handled during the investigation. The domestic industry went back to the DGTR, the regulator re-ran the inquiry with the defects cured, and on August 3, 2026 issued fresh final findings reaching the identical substantive conclusion. The finance ministry's notification now puts those findings back into force.

The duty schedule is producer-specific, which makes supplier selection a margin decision rather than a customs formality. The LB Group entities — the Chinese industry's giants, including Henan Billions — draw the lowest rate at $460 per tonne. Shandong Jinhai and Shandong Xianghai face $563, the Anhui Gold Star companies $609, twelve other cooperating producers pay $510, and everyone else, including any non-cooperative exporter, pays the residual $681. Against a landed value of roughly Rs 2.05 lakh per tonne — about $2,466 — those levies add between 19% and 28% to the cost of Chinese material. The notification also closes the obvious workaround that importers would otherwise reach for: Chinese-origin goods routed through any third country, or goods exported from China regardless of stated origin, attract the residual rate. The only immediate comfort for buyers is timing — the authority declined to backdate the levy, so consignments already cleared before notification escaped it. Everything arriving after pays.

Who wins and who pays is no mystery. The beneficiaries are the three petitioners — Kerala Minerals and Metals, Travancore Titanium Products and VV Titanium Pigments — which have spent years arguing that Indian production deserves protection in its home market. The burden falls on the downstream: paint manufacturers, ink producers, plastics and masterbatch formulators, for whom titanium dioxide is the single most important white pigment and an input whose cost moves product pricing. The most contested scope issue went against the users. Paint makers had argued that rutile-sulphate grade — a segment where domestic capacity is negligible, perhaps 3,500 to 4,000 tonnes against national demand near three lakh tonnes — should be exempted. The authority disagreed, holding that rutile-sulphate and rutile-chloride are like articles, leaving economy-paint, ink and masterbatch formulators with no grade-based escape route. Exemptions for food, pharmaceutical, cosmetic, textile and nanomaterial grades remain, and the authority declined one last user request — clarifying whether toilet soaps fall under the cosmetics exclusion — as outside the scope of the remand proceeding, leaving that classification ambiguity to be settled at the customs desk, consignment by consignment.

The strategic picture matters as much as the schedule. Chinese titanium dioxide supplied 57% of Indian demand in the period of investigation — more than 2.26 lakh tonnes — and India is far from alone in pushing back: duties against Chinese TiO2 are already in force in Brazil, the European Union, Saudi Arabia and the Eurasian Economic Union. Each new barrier fragments global flows and pushes Chinese producers to discount ever harder into the markets still open to them, which is precisely the dynamic that produced the dumping finding in the first place. The duties also carry an operational wrinkle that procurement teams cannot ignore: individual producer rates are conditional on a signed manufacturer declaration on the commercial invoice naming the producer and its address. Miss that document, and the consignment pays the residual $681 rate by default — a paperwork detail worth real money at the buying desk. For India, the five-year levy is industrial policy in miniature: a bet that domestic pigment capacity — anchored by public-sector Kerala Minerals and Metals and the storied Travancore Titanium — can capture share at prices that no longer have to compete with dumped imports. For the paint industry, which fought this duty all the way to the High Court and lost on substance after winning on procedure, the message is that procedural victories buy time, not outcomes. The substance of the dumping finding survived every challenge; the only question was whether the paperwork would. It has now been settled, and for five years the world's cheapest white pigment will not be cheap in India — the first pricing letters from pigment distributors to paint plants are likely already being drafted.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT HAS INDIA IMPOSED?
Anti-dumping duties of $460 to $681 per tonne on specified titanium dioxide imports from China, in force for five years, applied producer-by-producer.
WHY WERE THE DUTIES REIMPOSED?
The original 2025 levy was quashed by the Calcutta High Court on procedural grounds; the DGTR re-ran the investigation and reached the same finding of dumping and injury, leading to reimposition.
WHO BENEFITS AND WHO PAYS?
Domestic producers Kerala Minerals & Metals, Travancore Titanium and VV Titanium gain protection; paint, ink, plastics and masterbatch makers face 19-28% higher costs on Chinese material, which supplied 57% of Indian demand. GLOSSARY: ANTI-DUMPING DUTY — A tariff imposed on imports sold below fair value that injures domestic producers; set to close the price gap rather than raise revenue. TITANIUM DIOXIDE — A white pigment essential to paints, inks, plastics and coatings; among the most widely used industrial chemicals. DGTR — The Directorate General of Trade Remedies, the Indian authority that investigates dumping, subsidies and import surges. RUTILE/ANATASE GRADES — The two commercial crystal forms of titanium dioxide; rutile-sulphate was the grade users sought, unsuccessfully, to exempt.
