RBI Bans Forex Derivative Rebooking, $5m Threshold as Rupee at 96.78

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • 01/RBI bans rebooking of cancelled rupee forex derivative contracts and cuts the no-underlying-exposure hedging threshold from $100 million to $5 million
  • 02/A 20% Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve now applies to rupee derivative contracts above $2 million notional, raising the funding cost of large dollar-rupee positions
  • 03/From October 12, the RBI will supply the entire daily dollar needs of IOC, BPCL and HPCL, even as forex reserves fell $12.95 billion in a week

The Reserve Bank of India on Saturday announced a sweeping package of measures to ease pressure on the rupee. The central bank tightened rules for foreign exchange derivatives, restricted the rebooking of cancelled contracts and introduced a cash reserve requirement for large trades.

The rupee is trading at 96.78 against the US dollar, close to its all-time low of 96.95 hit in May. The latest action is the RBI's most direct intervention in the currency derivatives market in recent months.

Under the revised rules, authorised dealers will not be allowed to let users rebook foreign exchange derivative contracts involving the rupee if the contracts were cancelled after the directions were issued. The ban covers both deliverable and non-deliverable contracts.

Rollover of contracts on maturity will continue to be permitted, subject to existing regulations. The rebooking restriction targets a practice where market participants repeatedly cancel and rebook contracts to re-hedge their exposures at more favourable levels.

The RBI has also collapsed the threshold for undertaking forex derivative transactions to hedge contracted exposures without establishing the underlying exposure. The limit has been cut from $100 million equivalent to $5 million equivalent across all authorised dealers.

The corresponding threshold for taking positions in exchange-traded currency derivatives involving the rupee, without establishing underlying exposure, has been reduced from $100 million to $5 million equivalent. The lower limit applies across all recognised stock exchanges taken together.

In a third lever, the RBI introduced a Foreign Exchange Risk Reserve, or FERR, for rupee-involving FX derivative contracts with a notional value exceeding $2 million equivalent. Authorised dealers will have to maintain a cash reserve with the RBI equal to 20% of the rupee equivalent of the notional amount of each eligible transaction.

The FERR applies where such contracts are used to hedge current account exposures and the user purchases foreign currency against the rupee. By locking up dealer cash, the requirement raises the funding cost, or carry, of trading the rupee.

Separately, users entering rupee-involving forex derivative contracts to hedge contracted exposures must now provide an undertaking. The document must confirm that the same underlying exposure has not been hedged with another authorised dealer, and dealers must obtain and retain it.

"The measures are intended to strengthen market discipline and ensure appropriate risk management while maintaining an orderly and transparent foreign exchange market," the RBI said.

Beyond the derivatives market, the RBI moved to take a major source of dollar demand off the table. From October 12, the central bank will meet the entire daily foreign exchange requirement of Indian Oil Corporation, Bharat Petroleum Corporation and Hindustan Petroleum Corporation.

The step removes the three state-run oil marketing companies' dollar demand from the open forex market. It eliminates a large daily source of demand at a time when the rupee is hovering near record lows.

India's forex reserves fell $12.95 billion to $734.60 billion in the week ended October 2, the fourth consecutive weekly decline. Foreign currency assets dropped $10.66 billion to $604.74 billion, while gold holdings fell $2.28 billion to $106.41 billion.

The drop follows an $18.34 billion fall in the previous week. Reserves are now around $51 billion below the record high of $785.70 billion hit in the week ended September 4.

Taken together, the RBI is now fighting on two fronts. It is spending reserves to smooth the rupee's slide while tightening the rules of the derivatives market to curb speculative churn.

The threshold cut is the most dramatic number in the circular. Moving the proof-of-exposure line from $100 million to $5 million means the rule now catches hedging books twenty times smaller than before.

In practice, that pulls mid-sized corporates into the documentation net. Positions that once traded on presumed underlying exposure now need it shown — or they cannot be taken at all.

The FERR reserve adds a funding angle that traders will feel immediately. A bank arranging a rupee derivative above $2 million must park 20% of the notional's rupee equivalent with the RBI — cash that earns nothing while the trade lives.

That is a direct carry cost on shorting the rupee. A speculator funding a large hedge now pays a reserve levy the direction of the trade must overcome before it turns profitable.

The rebooking ban closes a churn loophole. Traders who cancelled contracts to rebook at better rates — repeatedly, across dealers — can now cancel once and lose the slot; rollovers at maturity remain permitted, so genuine hedges are untouched.

The no-double-hedging undertaking polices the other side. A corporate must now certify that the same exposure has not been hedged elsewhere, closing a gap where one invoice could back two derivative positions.

The oil window is the demand-side strike. From October 12, the RBI itself will supply the entire daily dollar requirement of Indian Oil, Bharat Petroleum and Hindustan Petroleum — removing a large, price-insensitive buyer from the market each session.

That mechanism matters because oil companies are among the market's steadiest dollar buyers. Their absorption by the central bank cuts daily demand that dealers routinely positioned against.

The backdrop is a reserve pile burning quickly. Four straight weekly declines have taken the stock to $734.60 billion, roughly $51 billion below the September 4 record — spending the buffer while tightening the rules is a two-front defense.

Saturday circulars of this breadth are rare. That the central bank chose the weekend to drop all three levers at once signals urgency — the rupee at 96.78, a fraction from the May record of 96.95, left little room for gradualism.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT HAS THE RBI ANNOUNCED FOR THE FOREX DERIVATIVES MARKET?
THE RBI HAS BANNED THE REBOOKING OF CANCELLED RUPEE-INVOLVING FOREX DERIVATIVE CONTRACTS, CUT THE THRESHOLD FOR HEDGING WITHOUT ESTABLISHING UNDERLYING EXPOSURE FROM $100 MILLION TO $5 MILLION, AND INTRODUCED A 20% FOREIGN EXCHANGE RISK RESERVE ON CONTRACTS ABOVE $2 MILLION NOTIONAL. IT WILL ALSO MEET THE ENTIRE DAILY DOLLAR REQUIREMENT OF IOC, BPCL AND HPCL FROM OCTOBER 12.
WHY IS THE RBI CRACKING DOWN NOW?
THE RUPEE IS TRADING AT 96.78 AGAINST THE US DOLLAR, CLOSE TO ITS ALL-TIME LOW OF 96.95 HIT IN MAY. THE RBI SAID THE MEASURES ARE INTENDED TO STRENGTHEN MARKET DISCIPLINE AND ENSURE APPROPRIATE RISK MANAGEMENT WHILE MAINTAINING AN ORDERLY AND TRANSPARENT FOREIGN EXCHANGE MARKET.
CAN MARKET PARTICIPANTS REBOOK CANCELLED FOREX CONTRACTS?
NO. AUTHORISED DEALERS CANNOT ALLOW USERS TO REBOOK FOREIGN EXCHANGE DERIVATIVE CONTRACTS INVOLVING THE RUPEE, DELIVERABLE OR NON-DELIVERABLE, IF THE CONTRACTS WERE CANCELLED AFTER THE DIRECTIONS WERE ISSUED. ROLLOVER OF CONTRACTS ON MATURITY CONTINUES TO BE PERMITTED, SUBJECT TO EXISTING REGULATIONS.
HOW DOES THE FOREIGN EXCHANGE RISK RESERVE WORK?
FOR RUPEE-INVOLVING FX DERIVATIVE CONTRACTS WITH A NOTIONAL VALUE ABOVE $2 MILLION EQUIVALENT, AUTHORISED DEALERS MUST MAINTAIN A CASH RESERVE WITH THE RBI EQUAL TO 20% OF THE RUPEE EQUIVALENT OF THE NOTIONAL AMOUNT OF EACH ELIGIBLE TRANSACTION. IT APPLIES TO CONTRACTS USED TO HEDGE CURRENT ACCOUNT EXPOSURES WHERE THE USER PURCHASES FOREIGN CURRENCY AGAINST THE RUPEE.
WHAT IS HAPPENING TO INDIA'S FOREX RESERVES?
RESERVES FELL $12.95 BILLION TO $734.60 BILLION IN THE WEEK ENDED OCTOBER 2, THE FOURTH CONSECUTIVE WEEKLY DECLINE. FOREIGN CURRENCY ASSETS DROPPED $10.66 BILLION TO $604.74 BILLION AND GOLD FELL $2.28 BILLION TO $106.41 BILLION, LEAVING RESERVES AROUND $51 BILLION BELOW THE RECORD HIGH OF $785.70 BILLION HIT IN THE WEEK ENDED SEPTEMBER 4. GLOSSARY: Authorised dealer: A bank permitted by the RBI to deal in foreign exchange. Deliverable contract: A currency derivative settled by actual exchange of the two currencies at expiry. Non-deliverable contract: A cash-settled derivative that pays out the difference without exchanging the underlying currencies. Rebooking: Cancelling a derivative contract and entering a fresh one, typically at better rates. Notional value: The face amount of a derivative contract used to calculate payments, as opposed to the cash actually exchanged. Current account exposure: Overseas payment obligations arising from trade in goods and services. Foreign currency assets: The foreign-denominated portion of India's forex reserves, held mainly in bonds and deposits.
