India Reviews 2019 Mineral Policy Amid Critical-Minerals Push

BY AAGMAN DESK | EST. READ: 3 MIN
EXECUTIVE TLDR //
  • •- The Mines Ministry is constituting a nine-member committee chaired by its Additional Secretary to comprehensively review the National Mineral Policy, 2019.
  • •- The review will focus on critical minerals, recycling and deep-sea resources, with recommendations due within two months of the panel's constitution.
  • •- The policy originally aimed at transparent regulation, sustainable mining and protection of project-affected tribal communities.
  • •- The exercise aligns with the ministry's parallel effort to recast the MMDR Act, 1957, and the National Critical Mineral Mission.
  • •- Industry bodies say the 2019 framework predates the strategic importance now attached to mineral security.
  • •- A revised policy is expected to emphasise investment, advanced technologies and domestic value addition.

India's mineral rulebook is getting its first top-to-bottom reconsideration of the strategic era. The Mines Ministry has decided to constitute a nine-member committee, chaired by the ministry's Additional Secretary, to undertake a comprehensive review of the National Mineral Policy, 2019 — the framework that has governed the country's mining sector through seven years of upheaval it was never designed for. The panel has been asked to deliver recommendations within two months of its constitution, a compressed timeline that signals how far the policy environment has moved past the assumptions of 2019. The committee's formation has not yet been formally notified, and its membership beyond the chair will be watched closely by an industry that has lobbied for exactly this exercise.

The review's brief is explicit about what changed: critical minerals, recycling and deep-sea resources were afterthoughts or absent from the 2019 text, and all three are now first-order national priorities. Critical minerals — lithium, cobalt, rare earths, nickel and their kin — underpin batteries, electronics, defence systems and the energy transition itself, and India's dependence on imports for most of them has turned geology into geopolitics. Recycling enters the frame because a serious critical-minerals strategy must include urban mining and circular-economy recovery, not just fresh extraction: India's electronics waste streams and legacy tailings contain recoverable metal that a 2019-era policy never priced. Deep-sea resources reach further: India's polymetallic nodule ambitions in the central Indian Ocean basin, held under the country's International Seabed Authority exploration contracts, need a domestic policy scaffold before commercial harvesting becomes technically and economically real. The 2019 policy speaks to none of this with any specificity; the review exists to fix that.

The original policy, approved by the Union Cabinet chaired by Prime Minister Narendra Modi, was built on a different set of concerns: more effective regulation, sustainable development, transparency, and the rights of project-affected people — particularly those in tribal areas. Those provisions earned it credibility as a social-licence framework, and the review's mandate includes recommending a comprehensive revision rather than wholesale replacement, which suggests the social and environmental spine will survive even as the strategic content is rewritten. The policy also carried the standard growth agenda of its time — encouraging exploration, attracting private investment, promoting ease of doing business — and industry has spent seven years pointing out that the gap between that agenda and ground-level implementation, from auction design to state-level clearance friction, remains wide. None of those frictions will be fixed by renaming the policy; they require the statutory and administrative follow-through that the MMDR recast is meant to supply, and that the committee can now recommend in coordinated fashion.

The review does not happen in isolation; it is the policy companion to the ministry's parallel effort to recast the MMDR Act, 1957 — the legal backbone of Indian mining. Legislation sets the powers and penalties; the policy sets the direction. Running the two exercises together means the committee can recommend statutory changes the ministry already intends to carry into the MMDR recast, and the National Critical Mineral Mission launched in between the two becomes the programmatic bridge. B K Bhatia, former Director General of the Federation of Indian Mineral Industries, called the revision imperative, citing the "emerging strategic importance of critical and strategic minerals" alongside the MMDR initiative, and framed the goal as a forward-looking framework for unlocking India's mineral potential, attracting investment and advanced technologies, and strengthening mineral security. FICCI's Satija echoed that industry would welcome the chance to contribute.

The international context sharpens the argument. Mineral jurisdictions worldwide — from the United States to Indonesia to the Democratic Republic of Congo — have rewritten mining policy around national priorities, supply-chain resilience and domestic value addition, as Pankaj Satija, Co-Chair of the FICCI Mining Committee, noted in welcoming the review. India's exploration spend remains a fraction of Australia's or Canada's; its discovered reserves are disproportionately unexplored; and China demonstrated again this week, with fresh export curbs on strategic machinery, how readily resource leverage converts into diplomatic pressure. A country that wants to be a manufacturing alternative to China cannot run that ambition on imported minerals governed by a pre-strategic rulebook.

The two-month clock, once the committee is seated, will tell whether this is a revision or a rebrand. The test is specific: does the new text move India from an auction-first minerals regime to an exploration-and-processing-first one, with the fiscal and institutional machinery — recovery of critical minerals from tailings, seabed exploitation frameworks, recycling mandates — that such a shift requires? Industry, judging by the FIMI and FICCI responses, is ready to engage constructively. The geology is patient; the geopolitics is not.

FREQUENTLY ASKED QUESTIONS //
WHAT IS BEING REVIEWED?
The National Mineral Policy, 2019, through a nine-member Mines Ministry committee chaired by the Additional Secretary, with recommendations due within two months.
WHY NOW?
The 2019 policy predates the strategic focus on critical minerals, and the review aligns with the recast of the MMDR Act, 1957, and the National Critical Mineral Mission.
WHAT NEW AREAS WILL THE REVIEW COVER?
Critical and strategic minerals, recycling and circular-economy recovery, and deep-sea resources.
WHAT DID THE 2019 POLICY ORIGINALLY FOCUS ON?
Transparent regulation, sustainable mining, ease of doing business, private investment and the rights of project-affected communities, particularly in tribal areas. GLOSSARY: - National Mineral Policy: The government's overarching framework document governing the mining sector. - MMDR Act: The Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act, 1957, the primary mining statute. - Critical minerals: Minerals essential to strategic technologies with vulnerable supply chains. - Deep-sea resources: Mineral deposits such as polymetallic nodules on the ocean floor, explored under international seabed contracts.
